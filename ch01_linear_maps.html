Linear maps and the four fundamental subspaces

A matrix is a linear map written in coordinates. Whether $Ax = b$ can be solved, and how many solutions it has, is decided by four subspaces the map carries with it and one dimension count that ties them together.

Vector spaces, bases and coordinates

Fix the minimal structure in which "linear combination" makes sense, and find a way to turn abstract vectors into lists of numbers without losing information.

A vector space over a field $\F$ is a set $V$ with an addition $V\times V\to V$ and a scalar multiplication $\F\times V\to V$ such that addition is associative and commutative, there is a zero vector $0$ with $v + 0 = v$, every $v$ has an additive inverse $-v$, and for all $\alpha,\beta\in\F$ and $u,v\in V$:

$$\alpha(u+v) = \alpha u + \alpha v,\qquad (\alpha+\beta)v = \alpha v + \beta v,\qquad (\alpha\beta)v = \alpha(\beta v),\qquad 1\,v = v.$$

A subspace is a subset $U \subseteq V$ that contains $0$ and is closed under addition and scalar multiplication.

The span of a list $v_1,\dots,v_k$ is $\spn(v_1,\dots,v_k) = \{\sum_{i=1}^k c_i v_i : c_i \in \F\}$. The list is linearly independent if $\sum_i c_i v_i = 0$ forces every $c_i = 0$, and linearly dependent otherwise. A basis of $V$ is a linearly independent list that spans $V$. The space $V$ is finite-dimensional if some finite list spans it; its bases then all have the same length (), called the dimension $\dim V$.

Let $V$ be finite-dimensional. (i) Exchange lemma: every linearly independent list in $V$ is at most as long as every list that spans $V$. (ii) Every spanning list of $V$ contains a basis; in particular $V$ has a basis, and all bases of $V$ have the same length. (iii) Every linearly independent list in $V$ extends to a basis, and one of length $\dim V$ is already a basis. (iv) Every subspace $U \subseteq V$ is finite-dimensional with $\dim U \le \dim V$, and $\dim U = \dim V$ only if $U = V$.

Proof sketch

(i) Let $u_1,\dots,u_k$ be independent and let $w_1,\dots,w_l$ span $V$. Put $u_1$ in front of the spanning list. The longer list is dependent, and in a dependence some $w_j$ has a nonzero coefficient, since otherwise $u_1 = 0$; that $w_j$ lies in the span of the others, so deleting it leaves a spanning list. Repeat with $u_2, \dots, u_k$: each time a dependence must involve a remaining $w_j$, because the $u_i$ are independent, and one $w_j$ is deleted. Hence $k \le l$. (ii) Delete from a spanning list every vector that lies in the span of the vectors before it. The rest still spans, and it is independent, because in a dependence the last vector with a nonzero coefficient would lie in the span of the earlier ones. Two bases are both independent and spanning, so by (i) each is at most as long as the other. (iii) Apply the deletion of (ii) to the independent list followed by a spanning list. No vector of the independent list is deleted, so the result is a basis that contains it; if the list already has length $\dim V$, nothing was added. (iv) Choose vectors of $U$ one at a time, each outside the span of those already chosen. The chosen list stays independent, so by (i) the process stops after at most $\dim V$ steps, and then the list spans $U$. If $\dim U = \dim V$, a basis of $U$ is an independent list of length $\dim V$ in $V$, hence a basis of $V$ by (iii), and $U = V$.

Full proofs: Axler, Linear Algebra Done Right (4th ed.), §2A–2C, open access.

Let $\mathcal B = (b_1,\dots,b_n)$ be a basis of $V$. Every $v \in V$ can be written as $v = \sum_{i=1}^n c_i b_i$ for exactly one list of scalars $(c_1,\dots,c_n)$. The coordinate map $v \mapsto [v]_{\mathcal B} = (c_1,\dots,c_n)^\T \in \F^n$ is linear and bijective.

Proof. Existence is spanning. If $v = \sum c_i b_i = \sum d_i b_i$, then $\sum (c_i - d_i) b_i = 0$, and independence gives $c_i = d_i$. Linearity of the coordinate map follows from uniqueness: $\alpha u + \beta v$ has the expansion with coefficients $\alpha [u]_{\mathcal B} + \beta [v]_{\mathcal B}$. It is bijective, with inverse $(c_1,\dots,c_n)^\T \mapsto \sum_i c_i b_i$.

Simple and extreme cases. The zero space $\{0\}$ has the empty basis and dimension 0. Polynomials of degree at most $d$ form a space of dimension $d+1$ with basis $1, t, \dots, t^d$, which is independent because a nonzero polynomial has finitely many roots and so is not the zero function. All polynomials form a space that no finite list spans, since a list of polynomials of degree at most $d$ misses $t^{d+1}$: the first infinite-dimensional example (chapter 5). The field matters: $\C^n$ has dimension $n$ over $\C$ but $2n$ over $\R$, since the standard basis vectors $e_k$ together with their multiples $i\,e_k$ by the imaginary unit form a basis of $\C^n$ over $\R$.

Linear maps and their matrices

A map $T: V \to W$ between vector spaces over $\F$ is linear if $T(\alpha u + \beta v) = \alpha\, T u + \beta\, T v$ for all $u, v \in V$ and $\alpha, \beta \in \F$.

Let $T: V \to W$ be linear, $\mathcal B = (b_1,\dots,b_n)$ a basis of $V$ and $\mathcal C$ a basis of $W$, with $\dim W = m$. There is exactly one matrix $A \in \F^{m\times n}$, the matrix of $T$ in the bases $\mathcal B$ and $\mathcal C$, with

$$[T v]_{\mathcal C} = A\, [v]_{\mathcal B} \quad \text{for all } v \in V,$$

and its $j$-th column is $[T b_j]_{\mathcal C}$. If $S: W \to U$ is linear with matrix $A_S$ in the bases $\mathcal C$ and $\mathcal D$, where $\mathcal D$ is a basis of $U$, then the composition $ST$ has matrix $A_S A$ in the bases $\mathcal B$ and $\mathcal D$.

$[v]_{\mathcal B} \in \F^n$

$[Tv]_{\mathcal C} \in \F^m$
coordinates of the output in the basis of $W$

Proof. For $v = \sum_j c_j b_j$, linearity gives $Tv = \sum_j c_j\, T b_j$, so $[Tv]_{\mathcal C} = \sum_j c_j [Tb_j]_{\mathcal C}$, which is $A [v]_{\mathcal B}$ when column $j$ of $A$ is $[Tb_j]_{\mathcal C}$. Any matrix with this property has those columns (take $v = b_j$). Finally, $[STv]_{\mathcal D} = A_S[Tv]_{\mathcal C} = A_SA\,[v]_{\mathcal B}$ for all $v$, so $A_SA$ is the matrix of $ST$ by uniqueness.

A linear map is determined by what it does to one basis: by , the images $Tb_1, \dots, Tb_n$ fix the matrix, and with it every $Tv$. In the standard basis of $\F^n$ this is the statement that the columns of $A$ are $A e_1, \dots, A e_n$. The whole picture of a $2\times 2$ matrix is therefore the two arrows $Ae_1$ and $Ae_2$: is built on exactly that.

Two tempting claims fail already in the plane. Matrix multiplication does not commute: with $R$ the rotation by $90^\circ$ and $P = \begin{bmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{bmatrix}$, $RP = \begin{bmatrix}0&0\\1&0\end{bmatrix}$ while $PR = \begin{bmatrix}0&-1\\0&0\end{bmatrix}$. And a product can vanish with neither factor zero: $N = \begin{bmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{bmatrix}$ satisfies $N^2 = 0$. Such a matrix, with some power equal to zero, is called nilpotent. Every Jordan block of chapter 3 is a multiple of the identity plus a nilpotent matrix of this kind, with ones on the first superdiagonal (Jordan blocks).

Kernel, image and rank–nullity

For $A \in \F^{m\times n}$, the null space (kernel) is $\Null(A) = \{x \in \F^n : Ax = 0\}$ and the range (image, column space) is $\Range(A) = \{Ax : x \in \F^n\}$. The rank of $A$ is $\rank A = \dim \Range(A)$, and the nullity is $\dim \Null(A)$; shows that both sets are subspaces, so these dimensions are defined.

Let $A \in \F^{m\times n}$ have columns $a_1, \dots, a_n$. (i) $\Null(A)$ is a subspace of $\F^n$, and $\Range(A) = \spn(a_1, \dots, a_n)$ is a subspace of $\F^m$. (ii) The columns are linearly independent if and only if $\Null(A) = \{0\}$. (iii) $\rank A \le \min(m, n)$.

Proof. Everything rests on $Ax = \sum_j x_j a_j$. It shows that $\Range(A)$ is the span of the columns, hence a subspace, and that a nonzero $x$ with $Ax = 0$ is the same thing as a dependence among the columns, which is (ii). $\Null(A)$ contains $0$ and is closed under linear combinations because $A(\alpha x + \beta y) = \alpha Ax + \beta Ay$. (iii) $\Range(A)$ is a subspace of $\F^m$, so its dimension is at most $m$, and it is spanned by $n$ vectors, so its dimension is at most $n$ ().

Four probes. The zero matrix in $\F^{m\times n}$ has rank 0 and nullity $n$. The identity $I_n$ has rank $n$ and nullity 0. A rank-one matrix $uv^\T$ with $u \in \F^m$, $v \in \F^n$ nonzero sends $x$ to $(v^\T x)\,u$, so it has range $\spn(u)$ and null space $\{x : v^\T x = 0\}$, of dimension $n-1$: if $v_k \neq 0$, the equation $v^\T x = 0$ fixes $x_k$ and leaves the other $n - 1$ coordinates free. A wide matrix ($m < n$) has rank at most $m$ (), and in every example its nullity is at least $n - m$. In each case rank and nullity add up to $n$.

For every $A \in \F^{m\times n}$, the rank and the nullity add up to the number of columns.

For every $A \in \F^{m\times n}$,

$$\dim \Null(A) + \rank A = n.$$

Proof. Pick a basis $u_1,\dots,u_k$ of $\Null(A)$ and extend it to a basis $u_1,\dots,u_k, w_1,\dots,w_r$ of $\F^n$ (), so $k + r = n$. The vectors $Aw_1,\dots,Aw_r$ span $\Range(A)$, because $A$ applied to any basis expansion kills the $u_i$ terms. They are independent: if $\sum_j c_j A w_j = 0$ then $\sum_j c_j w_j \in \Null(A)$, so it equals some $\sum_i d_i u_i$, and independence of the full basis forces all $c_j = 0$. Hence $\rank A = r = n - k$.

For square $A \in \F^{n\times n}$ the following are equivalent: (i) $A$ is injective, that is, $\Null(A) = \{0\}$; (ii) $A$ is surjective, that is, $\Range(A) = \F^n$; (iii) $A$ is invertible: $AB = BA = I$ for some $B \in \F^{n\times n}$; (iv) $BA = I$ for some $B \in \F^{n\times n}$; (v) $AB = I$ for some $B \in \F^{n\times n}$. All the matrices $B$ in (iii), (iv) and (v) are then one and the same, the inverse $A^{-1}$. For $m < n$, every $A \in \F^{m\times n}$ has a nonzero null vector.

Proof. Injectivity means $\Null(A) = \{0\}$ because $Ax = Ax'$ exactly when $A(x - x') = 0$. By , $\Null(A) = \{0\}$ exactly when $\rank A = n$, and by the subspace $\Range(A)$ of $\F^n$ has dimension $n$ exactly when it is $\F^n$; so (i) and (ii) are equivalent. If both hold, $A$ is a bijection of $\F^n$, and the inverse map $g$ is linear, since $A(\alpha\, g(y) + \beta\, g(y')) = \alpha y + \beta y'$. By , $g$ is multiplication by a matrix $B$, and $AB = BA = I$ because $A \circ g$ and $g \circ A$ are identity maps: this is (iii). Clearly (iii) implies (iv) and (v). If $BA = I$, then $Ax = 0$ gives $x = BAx = 0$, so (iv) implies (i); if $AB = I$, then every $y = A(By)$ lies in $\Range(A)$, so (v) implies (ii). If $B$ is as in (iii) and $CA = I$, then $C = C(AB) = (CA)B = B$; likewise $AC = I$ gives $C = (BA)C = B(AC) = B$. For $m < n$, $\rank A \le m$ by , so $\dim\Null(A) = n - \rank A \ge n - m > 0$.

Injective and surjective are not equivalent in infinite dimension. On the vector space of square-summable sequences $x = (x_1, x_2, \dots)$, those with $\sum_k \abs{x_k}^2 < \infty$, the right shift $(x_1, x_2, \dots) \mapsto (0, x_1, x_2, \dots)$ is linear and injective, since its output lists every $x_k$, but not surjective, since every output starts with $0$ and $(1, 0, 0, \dots)$ is never reached. The left shift $(x_1, x_2, \dots) \mapsto (x_2, x_3, \dots)$ is surjective, since it sends $(0, y_1, y_2, \dots)$ to $(y_1, y_2, \dots)$, but not injective, since it sends $(1, 0, 0, \dots)$ to $0$. The equivalence of (i) and (ii) in is therefore a finite-dimensional fact; chapter 5 returns to both shifts (the shifts on sequence space).

The four fundamental subspaces

Take a real matrix $A \in \R^{m\times n}$ and the dot product $x^\T y = \sum_i x_i y_i$ on $\R^n$ and $\R^m$. (For complex matrices, replace $A^\T$ by the conjugate transpose $A^*$ and $x^\T y$ by $y^* x$ throughout.) Two vectors are orthogonal, $x \perp y$, if $x^\T y = 0$. The length of $x$ is $\norm{x} = (x^\T x)^{1/2} = (\sum_i x_i^2)^{1/2}$, which vanishes only for $x = 0$. The orthogonal complement of a subspace $S$ is $S^\perp = \{x : x^\T s = 0 \text{ for all } s \in S\}$, and $x \perp S$ means $x \in S^\perp$. Chapter 2 develops this geometry in general.

For every subspace $S \subseteq \R^n$, the orthogonal complement $S^\perp$ is a subspace, $\R^n = S \oplus S^\perp$, $\dim S + \dim S^\perp = n$, and $(S^\perp)^\perp = S$.

Proof. The conditions $x^\T s = 0$ are linear in $x$, so $S^\perp$ is a subspace, and $S \cap S^\perp = \{0\}$ because every $x$ in both has $\norm{x}^2 = x^\T x = 0$. Let $k = \dim S$. If $k = 0$, then $S^\perp = \R^n$. Otherwise take a basis $s_1, \dots, s_k$ of $S$ and let $B \in \R^{k\times n}$ have rows $s_1^\T, \dots, s_k^\T$. A vector is orthogonal to all of $S$ exactly when it is orthogonal to each $s_i$, so $S^\perp = \Null(B)$, and with $\rank B \le k$ () gives $\dim S^\perp \ge n - k$. Conversely, a basis of $S$ followed by a basis $t_1, \dots, t_l$ of $S^\perp$ is independent: if $\sum_i a_i s_i + \sum_j c_j t_j = 0$, then $\sum_i a_i s_i = -\sum_j c_j t_j$ lies in $S \cap S^\perp = \{0\}$, and all coefficients vanish. By , $k + l \le n$; hence $l = n - k$, and the combined list is a basis of $\R^n$. So every $x \in \R^n$ is $s + t$ with $s \in S$ and $t \in S^\perp$, uniquely because $S \cap S^\perp = \{0\}$. Finally, $S \subseteq (S^\perp)^\perp$, and both have dimension $k$ by the dimension count applied to $S$ and to $S^\perp$, so they are equal by .

Chapter 2 proves the same facts with orthonormal bases (the orthogonal-complement proposition).

The matrix $A$ comes with two subspaces of the input space $\R^n$, the row space $\Range(A^\T)$ and the null space $\Null(A)$, and two of the output space $\R^m$, the column space $\Range(A)$ and the left null space $\Null(A^\T) = \{y : y^\T A = 0\}$.

Let $A \in \R^{m\times n}$ have rank $r$. Then

$$\Null(A) = \Range(A^\T)^\perp, \qquad \Null(A^\T) = \Range(A)^\perp,$$

so $\R^n = \Range(A^\T) \oplus \Null(A)$ and $\R^m = \Range(A) \oplus \Null(A^\T)$, with dimensions $r$, $n - r$, $r$ and $m - r$. In particular the row rank $\dim\Range(A^\T)$ equals the column rank $\rank A$. Moreover $A$ maps the row space one-to-one onto the column space.

Proof. $Ax = 0$ says that $x$ has zero dot product with every row of $A$, hence with every linear combination of the rows, that is, $x \perp \Range(A^\T)$. So $\Null(A) = \Range(A^\T)^\perp$, and the same argument applied to $A^\T$ gives $\Null(A^\T) = \Range(A)^\perp$. Now gives both direct sums, and together with rank–nullity, $\dim \Range(A^\T) = n - \dim \Null(A) = r$ and $\dim\Null(A^\T) = m - \dim\Range(A) = m - r$. Finally, write $x = x_r + x_n$ with $x_r \in \Range(A^\T)$ and $x_n \in \Null(A)$. Then $Ax = Ax_r$, so $A$ maps the row space onto the column space; and if $x$ and $x'$ in the row space have $Ax = Ax'$, then $x - x'$ lies in $\Range(A^\T) \cap \Null(A) = \{0\}$.

The name and the picture below follow G. Strang, "The fundamental theorem of linear algebra", Amer. Math. Monthly 100 (1993) 848–855.

The theorem is a symmetry statement. The pair $(A, A^\T)$ plays the same game in opposite directions: what one map kills is exactly what is perpendicular to everything the other one reaches. Restricted to its row space, $A$ is an isomorphism onto its column space. All the information that $A$ loses sits in the null space, since $Ax = Ax'$ exactly when $x - x' \in \Null(A)$; all the unreachability sits in the left null space, since $b$ is reachable exactly when its component in $\Null(A^\T)$, in the splitting $\R^m = \Range(A) \oplus \Null(A^\T)$, is zero (). The singular value decomposition supplies orthonormal bases for all four subspaces at once (chapter 4).

For every $A \in \R^{m\times n}$, $\Null(A^\T A) = \Null(A)$ and $\Range(A^\T A) = \Range(A^\T)$. In particular $\rank A^\T A = \rank A$, and the system $A^\T A x = A^\T b$, the normal equations of least squares in chapter 2, has a solution for every $b \in \R^m$.

Proof. If $A^\T Ax = 0$, then $\norm{Ax}^2 = x^\T A^\T Ax = 0$, so $Ax = 0$; the reverse inclusion is clear. Next, $\Range(A^\T A) \subseteq \Range(A^\T)$, and both have dimension $n - \dim\Null(A)$: the first by and the identity just proved, the second by . They are equal by , and $A^\T b \in \Range(A^\T) = \Range(A^\T A)$.

Solving $Ax = b$

Given $A \in \R^{m\times n}$ and $b \in \R^m$, decide whether $Ax = b$ has a solution and describe the set of all solutions.

A test for existence that uses only $A$ and $b$, and a parametrization of the solution set by a subspace of known dimension.

Let $A \in \R^{m\times n}$ and $b \in \R^m$. Then $Ax = b$ has a solution if and only if $b \perp \Null(A^\T)$. Equivalently, exactly one of the following holds:

  1. there is $x \in \R^n$ with $Ax = b$;
  2. there is $y \in \R^m$ with $A^\T y = 0$ and $y^\T b \neq 0$.

Proof. Solvability means $b \in \Range(A)$. By and , $\Range(A) = (\Range(A)^\perp)^\perp = \Null(A^\T)^\perp$. A vector $y$ as in the second statement is precisely a certificate that $b \notin \Null(A^\T)^\perp$, so exactly one of the two statements holds.

Let $A \in \R^{m\times n}$ have rank $r$, let $b \in \R^m$, and suppose that $x_p \in \R^n$ satisfies $A x_p = b$. The set of all solutions of $Ax = b$ is $x_p + \Null(A) = \{x_p + z : Az = 0\}$, the translate of a subspace of dimension $n - r$ (an affine subspace of dimension $n - r$). Exactly one solution lies in the row space $\Range(A^\T)$.

Proof. $Ax = b$ if and only if $A(x - x_p) = 0$, and $\dim\Null(A) = n - r$ by rank–nullity. Split $x_p = x_r + x_n$ with $x_r \in \Range(A^\T)$ and $x_n \in \Null(A)$ (); then $A x_r = A x_p = b$. Two row-space solutions differ by a vector in $\Range(A^\T) \cap \Null(A) = \{0\}$.

Let $A \in \R^{m\times n}$ and $b \in \Range(A)$, and let $x_r$ be the solution of $Ax = b$ in the row space. Every solution is $x = x_r + z$ with $z \in \Null(A)$, and $\norm{x}^2 = \norm{x_r}^2 + \norm{z}^2$. Hence $x_r$ is the unique solution of smallest length.

Proof. By , $z = x - x_r$ lies in $\Null(A)$, which is orthogonal to $x_r \in \Range(A^\T)$ by . So $\norm{x_r + z}^2 = \norm{x_r}^2 + 2\,x_r^\T z + \norm{z}^2 = \norm{x_r}^2 + \norm{z}^2$, which exceeds $\norm{x_r}^2$ unless $z = 0$.

Chapter 2 identifies $x_r$ with the pseudoinverse solution $A^+ b$ (the pseudoinverse theorem).

Counting solutions needs only $m$, $n$ and $r$: makes this precise, and the table after it sorts the cases by shape, with the dimensions of the null spaces from .

Let $A \in \R^{m\times n}$ have rank $r$. Then $Ax = b$ has a solution for every $b \in \R^m$ if and only if $r = m$. Whenever $Ax = b$ has a solution, the solution is unique if $r = n$, and there are infinitely many solutions if $r < n$. In particular the number of solutions is $0$, $1$ or infinite.

Proof. $\Range(A) = \R^m$ exactly when $\dim\Range(A) = m$, by . A solvable system has the solution set $x_p + \Null(A)$ of , with $\dim\Null(A) = n - r$. If $r = n$, this set is a single point; if $r < n$, it contains the distinct solutions $x_p + tz$, $t \in \R$, for any nonzero $z \in \Null(A)$.

"More equations than unknowns means no solution" fails whenever $b$ happens to lie in the column space: $\begin{bmatrix}1\\2\\3\end{bmatrix} x = \begin{bmatrix}2\\4\\6\end{bmatrix}$ is solved by $x = 2$. The determinant as signed volume

Measure, with one number, how a square matrix scales volume and whether it reverses orientation.

The determinant of $A = (a_{ij}) \in \F^{n\times n}$ is

$$\det A = \sum_{\pi \in S_n} \operatorname{sgn}(\pi) \prod_{i=1}^n a_{\pi(i)\, i}, \qquad\text{so that}\qquad \det\begin{bmatrix} a & b\\ c & d\end{bmatrix} = ad - bc.$$
$S_n$
the set of all $n!$ permutations of $\{1,\dots,n\}$
$\operatorname{sgn}(\pi)$
$+1$ for permutations made of an even number of swaps, $-1$ for an odd number; no permutation is both (Axler, §9B)
$a, b, c, d$
the entries of a $2\times 2$ matrix, row by row

A signed volume of the parallelepiped spanned by $n$ vectors should be linear in each vector, vanish when two of the vectors coincide, and give the unit cube volume 1. shows that these three rules, imposed on the columns, single out the determinant.

Let $A, B \in \F^{n\times n}$. (i) $\det$ is the only function $\F^{n\times n} \to \F$ that is linear in each column separately, vanishes when two columns are equal, and takes the value $1$ at $I$. (ii) $\det(AB) = \det A \det B$ and $\det A^\T = \det A$. (iii) $A$ is invertible if and only if $\det A \neq 0$. (iv) For real $A$ with columns $a_1, \dots, a_n$, $\abs{\det A}$ is the volume of the parallelepiped $\{\sum_j t_j a_j : 0 \le t_j \le 1\}$ that they span, and $A$ multiplies the volume of every region by $\abs{\det A}$. (v) Let $A$ be real and invertible. For every basis $b_1, \dots, b_n$ of $\R^n$, the matrices with columns $b_1, \dots, b_n$ and $Ab_1, \dots, Ab_n$ have determinants of the same sign if $\det A > 0$ and of opposite signs if $\det A < 0$; in the second case $A$ reverses orientation.

Proofs of (i)–(iii): Axler, §9B–9C (alternating multilinear forms, then determinants); Treil, Linear Algebra Done Wrong, chapter 3, which motivates the three rules of (i) by volume. Statement (iv) is the linear case of the change-of-variables theorem for Lebesgue measure, applied to the unit cube for the parallelepiped: G. B. Folland, Real Analysis, 2nd ed. (Wiley, 1999), ch. 2. Statement (v) follows from (ii) and (iii): $\det[\,Ab_1\ \cdots\ Ab_n\,] = \det A\,\det[\,b_1\ \cdots\ b_n\,]$, and the last factor is nonzero because its columns form a basis.

A shear has determinant 1: it distorts shapes but preserves every area. A projection has determinant 0: it flattens. A reflection has determinant $-1$: it preserves areas and flips orientation. A rotation has determinant 1. Rotations and reflections are the only two kinds of real $2\times 2$ orthogonal matrices, and the sign of the determinant tells them apart: .

A matrix $Q \in \R^{2\times 2}$ is orthogonal if $Q^\T Q = I$. The orthogonal $2\times 2$ matrices are exactly the rotations $R_\theta$ of , which have determinant $1$, and the matrices

$$F_\theta = \begin{bmatrix}\cos\theta & \sin\theta\\ \sin\theta & -\cos\theta\end{bmatrix},$$

which have determinant $-1$, where $\theta$ ranges over all angles. $F_\theta$ is the reflection across the line spanned by $u = (\cos\tfrac\theta2, \sin\tfrac\theta2)^\T$: it fixes $u$ and sends the perpendicular vector $w = (-\sin\tfrac\theta2, \cos\tfrac\theta2)^\T$ to $-w$. The reflection in the gallery is $F_{\pi/2}$.

Proof. $Q^\T Q = I$ says that the columns $q_1, q_2$ of $Q$ have length 1 and $q_1^\T q_2 = 0$; this holds for $R_\theta$ and $F_\theta$. Conversely, write $q_1 = (\cos\theta, \sin\theta)^\T$. By , the vectors orthogonal to $q_1$ form a line, spanned by $(-\sin\theta, \cos\theta)^\T$, whose unit vectors are $\pm(-\sin\theta, \cos\theta)^\T$. The plus sign for $q_2$ gives $R_\theta$, with $\det R_\theta = \cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = 1$, and the minus sign gives $F_\theta$, with $\det F_\theta = -\cos^2\theta - \sin^2\theta = -1$. With $\phi = \theta/2$, the angle-difference formulas give $F_\theta u = (\cos(\theta - \phi), \sin(\theta - \phi))^\T = u$ and $F_\theta w = (\sin(\theta - \phi), -\cos(\theta - \phi))^\T = -w$.

The determinant is not additive: for $n \ge 2$, $\det(I_n + I_n) = 2^n$, not $\det I_n + \det I_n = 2$. And a small determinant does not mean "nearly singular": for $0 < \eps < 1$, $\det(\eps I_n) = \eps^n$ is tiny for large $n$, yet $\eps I_n$ is as far from singular, relative to its size, as a matrix can be. Measure the size of a matrix by the largest factor by which it stretches a unit vector. If $\eps I_n + E$ is singular, then $(\eps I_n + E)x = 0$ for some $x$ with $\norm{x} = 1$, so $\norm{Ex} = \eps$, and $E$ is at least as large as $\eps I_n$ itself; no matrix $A$ does better, since $E = -A$ makes $A + E$ singular. Distance to singularity is measured by the smallest singular value, not by the determinant (distance to singularity, chapter 4; conditioning in chapter 6).

Change of basis: similarity versus equivalence

Which properties of a matrix are properties of the underlying linear map, and therefore survive every change of coordinates?

The answer depends on which changes are allowed. If $T: V \to W$ goes between two different spaces, the bases of $V$ and $W$ can be changed independently, and the matrix changes to $P A Q$ with invertible $P \in \F^{m\times m}$, $Q \in \F^{n\times n}$: this is equivalence. If $T: V \to V$ maps a space to itself, one basis serves for input and output, and the matrix changes to $S^{-1} A S$ with invertible $S$: this is similarity. Both descriptions are .

Let $T: V \to W$ be linear with matrix $A \in \F^{m\times n}$ in bases $\mathcal B$ of $V$ and $\mathcal C$ of $W$. The matrices of $T$ in all pairs of bases of $V$ and $W$ are exactly the matrices $PAQ$ with $P \in \F^{m\times m}$ and $Q \in \F^{n\times n}$ invertible. If $W = V$ and $\mathcal C = \mathcal B$, the matrices of $T$ in a single basis, used for input and output, are exactly the matrices $S^{-1}AS$ with $S \in \F^{n\times n}$ invertible.

Proof

Let $\mathcal B'$ and $\mathcal C'$ be bases of $V$ and $W$. Let $Q$ be the matrix of the identity map of $V$ in the bases $\mathcal B'$ (input) and $\mathcal B$ (output): its $j$-th column holds the $\mathcal B$-coordinates of the $j$-th vector of $\mathcal B'$. Let $P$ be the matrix of the identity map of $W$ in the bases $\mathcal C$ and $\mathcal C'$. The composition rule of , applied to $T = \mathrm{id}_W \circ T \circ \mathrm{id}_V$, shows that $PAQ$ is the matrix of $T$ in $\mathcal B'$ and $\mathcal C'$. The matrix of the identity in $\mathcal B$ and $\mathcal B'$ is an inverse of $Q$, because both products are matrices of the identity map in a single basis, which is $I$; likewise $P$ is invertible. Conversely, let $Q$ be invertible. Its columns are independent ( and ), so the $n$ vectors of $V$ with these $\mathcal B$-coordinates are independent () and form a basis $\mathcal B'$ (), for which the construction above returns $Q$. The same construction with the columns of $P^{-1}$ gives $\mathcal C'$, so every $PAQ$ occurs. For $W = V$, $\mathcal C = \mathcal B$ and $\mathcal C' = \mathcal B'$, the matrix $P$ is the inverse of $Q$; put $S = Q$.

Every $A \in \F^{m\times n}$ of rank $r$ is equivalent to

$$\begin{bmatrix} I_r & 0 \\ 0 & 0\end{bmatrix} \in \F^{m\times n}.$$

Two matrices of the same size are equivalent if and only if they have the same rank.

Proof. Reuse the basis from the proof of . In the input space take $w_1,\dots,w_r$ followed by a basis of $\Null(A)$; in the output space take $Aw_1,\dots,Aw_r$, which are independent, extended to a basis (). In these bases the map sends the $j$-th basis vector to the $j$-th basis vector for $j \le r$ and the rest to zero. In matrix form: let $Q$ be the matrix with the input basis as columns and $P^{-1}$ the matrix with the output basis as columns; both are invertible by and . Then $AQ = [\,Aw_1\ \cdots\ Aw_r\ \ 0\ \cdots\ 0\,] = P^{-1}\begin{bmatrix} I_r & 0\\ 0 & 0\end{bmatrix}$, so $PAQ$ is the normal form. Conversely, rank is unchanged by invertible factors: $\Range(AQ) = \Range(A)$ because an invertible $Q$ is surjective, and an invertible $P$ is injective, so it maps a basis of $\Range(A)$ to a basis of $\Range(PA)$. Hence equivalent matrices have equal ranks, and two matrices $A$ and $B$ of rank $r$ are equivalent to each other: if $P_1AQ_1$ and $P_2BQ_2$ are both the normal form, then $B = P_2^{-1}P_1\,A\,Q_1Q_2^{-1}$.

Let $A, S \in \F^{n\times n}$ with $S$ invertible, and let $B = S^{-1}AS$. Then $p(B) = S^{-1}p(A)S$ for every polynomial $p(t) = \sum_k c_kt^k$, where $p(A) = \sum_k c_kA^k$ and $A^0 = I$. In particular $B^2 = B$ if and only if $A^2 = A$, and $B^k = 0$ if and only if $A^k = 0$. Moreover $A$ and $B$ have the same rank, the same trace $\tr A = \sum_i a_{ii}$, the same determinant, and the same characteristic polynomial $\det(\lambda I - A)$, as a function of the scalar $\lambda$.

Proof. In $B^k = S^{-1}AS\,S^{-1}AS \cdots S^{-1}AS$ the inner factors $SS^{-1}$ cancel, so $B^k = S^{-1}A^kS$, and linear combinations give $p(B) = S^{-1}p(A)S$. Hence $B^2 - B = S^{-1}(A^2 - A)S$ and $B^k = S^{-1}A^kS$ vanish exactly when $A^2 - A$ and $A^k$ do. Similarity is the equivalence with $P = S^{-1}$ and $Q = S$, so the rank is kept by . For the trace, $\tr(XY) = \sum_{i,j} x_{ij}y_{ji} = \tr(YX)$ for all $X, Y \in \F^{n\times n}$ gives $\tr\big(S^{-1}(AS)\big) = \tr\big((AS)S^{-1}\big) = \tr A$. For the determinant, $\det B = \det S^{-1}\,\det A\,\det S = \det A$ by , since $\det S^{-1}\det S = \det I = 1$; and $\lambda I - B = S^{-1}(\lambda I - A)S$ gives the characteristic polynomial.

Chapter 3 draws the consequences for eigenvalues and eigenvectors (similarity invariants).

Equal rank does not give similarity. $E = \begin{bmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{bmatrix}$ and $N = \begin{bmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{bmatrix}$ both have rank 1, so they are equivalent (), but they are not similar: by , every matrix $X$ similar to $E$ satisfies $X^2 = X$, while $N^2 = 0 \neq N$. Their traces, 1 and 0, already differ.

A classification problem is the question of which matrices one group action can turn into which, and a normal form is a canonical representative of each orbit. Allowing more changes can only remove invariants: a quantity preserved by every change that an action allows is also preserved by any action that allows fewer changes. Similarity is the special case $P = S^{-1}$, $Q = S$ of equivalence, so every equivalence invariant, such as the rank, is a similarity invariant; the converse fails, since the trace is a similarity invariant () that differs between the equivalent matrices $E$ and $N$. Self-check

  1. $A \in \R^{3\times 5}$ has rank 3. Give the dimensions of the four subspaces. Is $Ax = b$ solvable for every $b$?

    Answer

    By : row space 3, null space $5 - 3 = 2$, column space 3 (all of $\R^3$), left null space 0. Every $b$ is reachable (), and each solution set is a 2-dimensional affine subspace ().

  2. Show that $\Null(A^\T A) = \Null(A)$ and conclude that $\rank A^\T A = \rank A$.

    Answer

    If $A^\T A x = 0$ then $x^\T A^\T A x = \norm{Ax}^2 = 0$, so $Ax = 0$; the other inclusion is immediate. Both matrices have $n$ columns, so rank–nullity gives equal ranks ().

  3. For $A = u v^\T$ with nonzero $u \in \R^m$, $v \in \R^n$, describe all four subspaces.

    Answer

    Since $Ax = (v^\T x)\,u$ and $A^\T y = (u^\T y)\,v$: column space $\spn(u)$, left null space $\{y : u^\T y = 0\}$, row space $\spn(v)$, null space $\{x : v^\T x = 0\}$. Dimensions $1, m-1, 1, n-1$, as requires.

  4. Two $4\times 4$ matrices have rank 2. Must they be equivalent? Must they be similar?

    Answer

    Equivalent, yes (). Similar, not necessarily: the projection $\diag(1, 1, 0, 0)$ and the nilpotent matrix $e_1e_2^\T + e_3e_4^\T$ both have rank 2, but their traces are 2 and 0, and similar matrices have equal traces ().

  5. Give a real $2\times 2$ matrix with $\det A = 1$ that stretches some vector by a factor of at least 10.

    Answer

    The shear $\begin{bmatrix}1 & 10\\0 & 1\end{bmatrix}$ sends $e_2$ to $(10, 1)^\T$, of length $\sqrt{101} \approx 10.05$. Determinant 1 constrains area, not length: $\abs{\det A}$ is the product of the singular values (chapter 4), which for this shear are about $10.1$ and $0.099$ (the same shear in chapter 4).
