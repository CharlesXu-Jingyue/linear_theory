Optimal control and estimation: LQR, Kalman filter, LQG

The linear quadratic regulator (LQR) is the state feedback that minimizes a quadratic cost, and the Kalman filter, also called the linear quadratic estimator (LQE), is the state estimate that minimizes the mean-square error under Gaussian noise. Both come from Riccati equations, and the two are dual: transposing the system matrices and exchanging the weights for the noise covariances turns one into the other. Their combination, the linear quadratic Gaussian (LQG) controller, is optimal and yet carries no guaranteed robustness.

The regulator problem

Among all inputs that bring $\dot x = Ax + Bu$, $x(0) = x_0$, to rest, find the one that minimizes

$$J(u) = \int_0^\infty \bigl(x(t)^\T Q\, x(t) + u(t)^\T R\, u(t)\bigr)\,\dd t .$$
$x(t) \in \R^n$, $u(t) \in \R^m$
state and input; $A \in \R^{n\times n}$, $B \in \R^{n\times m}$
$Q = Q^\T \succeq 0$
the state weight, penalizing deviation from rest; $Q^{1/2}$ is its positive semidefinite square root (functions of a symmetric matrix, chapter 4), so $x^\T Q x = \norm{Q^{1/2}x}^2$
$R = R^\T \succ 0$
the input weight, penalizing effort; $R^{1/2}$ is its positive definite square root

The standing assumptions are $(A, B)$ stabilizable and $(Q^{1/2}, A)$ detectable (stabilizable and detectable, chapter 9: every uncontrollable, respectively unobservable, eigenvalue has negative real part). Each assumption has a job. $R \succ 0$ makes every input cost something, so that the minimization over the input is strictly convex. $(A, B)$ stabilizable makes some input achieve finite cost. $(Q^{1/2}, A)$ detectable makes every unstable mode visible in the cost, so that the optimizer cannot ignore it ( shows what happens otherwise). makes these three statements precise. The discrete-time problem replaces the integral by $\sum_k (x_k^\T Q x_k + u_k^\T R u_k)$ for $x_{k+1} = Ax_k + Bu_k$. There a matrix is stable when it is Schur stable, with all eigenvalues of modulus less than 1, and stabilizable and detectable are meant in the discrete-time sense: every uncontrollable, respectively unobservable, eigenvalue has modulus less than 1.

Consider the continuous-time problem with $Q \succeq 0$ and $R \succ 0$.

  1. If $(A, B)$ is stabilizable, then for every $x_0$ some input has finite cost $J(u)$.
  2. If $(Q^{1/2}, A)$ is detectable, then every input with $J(u) < \infty$ produces a state with $x(t) \to 0$ as $t \to \infty$.
  3. $J$ is strictly convex on the inputs of finite cost, so for each $x_0$ at most one input is optimal.

Proof. (1) Take $F$ with $A - BF$ Hurwitz (tests for stabilizability, chapter 10) and $u = -Fx$; then $\norm{x(t)}$ decays exponentially and $J$ is finite. (2) Take $F$ with $A - FQ^{1/2}$ Hurwitz (tests for detectability, chapter 10) and write $\dot x = (A - FQ^{1/2})x + f$ with $f = FQ^{1/2}x + Bu$. Finite cost gives $\int_0^\infty \norm{Q^{1/2}x}^2\dd t < \infty$ and, as $u^\T Ru \ge \lambda_{\min}(R)\norm{u}^2$ with $\lambda_{\min}(R) > 0$ the smallest eigenvalue of $R$, $\int_0^\infty\norm{u}^2\dd t < \infty$; so $f$ is square integrable. Convolution with the integrable kernel $e^{t(A - FQ^{1/2})}$ maps square-integrable functions to square-integrable functions (Young's inequality), so $x$ is square integrable, and then so is $\dot x$. Hence $\norm{x(t)}^2$, whose derivative $2x^\T\dot x$ is integrable, has a limit as $t \to \infty$, and the limit is $0$ because $\norm{x}^2$ is integrable. (3) The state is affine in the input, so $u \mapsto \int_0^\infty \norm{Q^{1/2}x}^2\dd t$ is convex, while $u \mapsto \int_0^\infty u^\T Ru\,\dd t$ is strictly convex because $R \succ 0$. If two different inputs were optimal, their average would cost strictly less.

Show that the optimal input is a linear state feedback $u = -Kx$ with a constant gain, characterize $K$ through one quadratic matrix equation, and show that the optimal closed loop is stable with optimal cost a quadratic form $x_0^\T P x_0$.

The best linear gain for a scalar plant. Let $\dot x = ax + bu$ with $b \neq 0$, weights $q > 0$ and $r > 0$, and restrict to $u = -kx$ with $bk > a$. Then $x(t) = e^{(a - bk)t}x_0$ and $J = (q + rk^2)\,x_0^2/\bigl(2(bk - a)\bigr)$. Setting the derivative in $k$ to zero gives $rbk^2 - 2ark - bq = 0$, whose root with $bk > a$ is

and $p$ is the positive root of $2ap - (b^2/r)p^2 + q = 0$. Two questions remain: whether a nonlinear or time-varying input could do better (it cannot: ), and what replaces the scalar quadratic equation when $n > 1$ (a Riccati equation).

Discrete time: dynamic programming and the Riccati recursion

Take $x_{k+1} = Ax_k + Bu_k$, a horizon $N$ and the cost $x_N^\T Q_f x_N + \sum_{k=0}^{N-1}(x_k^\T Q x_k + u_k^\T R u_k)$ with $Q \succeq 0$, $R \succ 0$ and terminal weight $Q_f \succeq 0$. Let $J_k(x)$ be the smallest cost of the stages $k, \dots, N$ starting from $x_k = x$ (an infimum over $u_k, \dots, u_{N-1}$, finite and nonnegative because every cost is). The tail of an optimal input sequence is optimal for the tail problem, the principle of optimality, which gives the Bellman recursion of .

In this setting $J_N(x) = x^\T Q_f\, x$ and, for $k = N-1, \dots, 0$ and every $x \in \R^n$,

$$J_k(x) = \inf_{u \in \R^m}\Bigl\{x^\T Q x + u^\T R u + J_{k+1}(Ax + Bu)\Bigr\}.$$

An input sequence $u_k, \dots, u_{N-1}$ is optimal from $x_k = x$ if and only if $u_k$ attains this infimum and $u_{k+1}, \dots, u_{N-1}$ is optimal from $x_{k+1} = Ax + Bu_k$.

Proof. Fix the first input $u$. The cost from $x$ is $x^\T Qx + u^\T Ru$ plus the cost of the remaining inputs from $Ax + Bu$, and the infimum of the latter over those inputs is $J_{k+1}(Ax + Bu)$ by definition. Taking the infimum over $u$ as well gives the recursion. For any sequence, the cost is therefore at least $x^\T Qx + u_k^\T Ru_k + J_{k+1}(Ax + Bu_k)$, with equality exactly when the tail is optimal, and this is at least $J_k(x)$, with equality exactly when $u_k$ attains the infimum.

Let $M \in \R^{m\times m}$ be symmetric positive definite and $g \in \R^m$. Then

$$u^\T M u + 2u^\T g = (u + M^{-1}g)^\T M\,(u + M^{-1}g) - g^\T M^{-1} g,$$

so the minimum over $u$ is $-g^\T M^{-1}g$, attained only at $u = -M^{-1}g$.

Proof. Expand the right-hand side. The first term is nonnegative and vanishes only at $u = -M^{-1}g$ because $M \succ 0$.

In this setting ($Q \succeq 0$, $R \succ 0$, $Q_f \succeq 0$), for every $k = 0, \dots, N$, $J_k(x) = x^\T P_k x$ where $P_N = Q_f$ and

$$P_k = Q + A^\T P_{k+1} A - A^\T P_{k+1} B\,\bigl(R + B^\T P_{k+1} B\bigr)^{-1} B^\T P_{k+1} A .$$

Each $P_k$ is symmetric positive semidefinite, and the unique optimal input is the linear feedback $u_k = -K_k x_k$ with $K_k = (R + B^\T P_{k+1}B)^{-1}B^\T P_{k+1}A$.

Proof. Backward induction on the recursion of . If $J_{k+1}(x) = x^\T P_{k+1}x$ with $P_{k+1} \succeq 0$, the bracket in the Bellman recursion equals $x^\T(Q + A^\T P_{k+1}A)x + u^\T M u + 2u^\T g$ with $M = R + B^\T P_{k+1}B \succ 0$ and $g = B^\T P_{k+1}Ax$. gives the unique minimizer $u = -M^{-1}g = -K_k x$ and the minimum $x^\T P_k x$ with $P_k$ as stated. $P_k$ is symmetric by construction and positive semidefinite because $J_k$ is an infimum of nonnegative costs. By the second part of , an optimal sequence must use the unique minimizer at every stage, so the feedback is the only optimal input.

Nothing restricted the input to be linear: linearity is produced by the quadratic value function. The matrices $P_k$ do not depend on $x_0$ and are computed offline, backward from the horizon. The Riccati recursion is a nonlinear map $P \mapsto \mathcal R(P)$, the right-hand side of the recursion in , defined at least on the positive semidefinite matrices and mapping them to positive semidefinite matrices (its value is the matrix of a minimum of nonnegative costs). Its fixed points solve the discrete-time algebraic Riccati equation

$$P = Q + A^\T P A - A^\T P B\,(R + B^\T P B)^{-1} B^\T P A .$$

A symmetric solution $P$ of the DARE is stabilizing if $A - BK$, with $K = (R + B^\T PB)^{-1}B^\T PA$, is Schur stable: all its eigenvalues have modulus less than 1. A symmetric solution $P$ of the continuous-time equation of is stabilizing if $A - BR^{-1}B^\T P$ is Hurwitz.

Let $Q \succeq 0$, $R \succ 0$, and let $(A, B)$ be stabilizable and $(Q^{1/2}, A)$ detectable, both in the discrete-time sense of . Then the DARE has exactly one symmetric positive semidefinite solution $P$; it is stabilizing (); the recursion of converges to $P$ from every $Q_f \succeq 0$ as $N - k \to \infty$; and $u_k = -Kx_k$ minimizes the infinite-horizon cost, with value $x_0^\T P x_0$.

For $x_{k+1} = ax_k + bu_k$ with weights $q \ge 0$, $r > 0$, the recursion is $p \mapsto f(p)$ with

$$f(p) = q + a^2 p - \frac{a^2b^2p^2}{r + b^2 p} = q + \frac{a^2 r\, p}{r + b^2 p},$$
$$k(p) = \frac{abp}{r + b^2p}, \qquad a - b\,k(p) = \frac{ar}{r + b^2 p}.$$

Here $k(p)$ is the gain computed from $p$ and $a - bk(p)$ the resulting closed-loop factor. Differentiating,

$$f'(p) = \Bigl(\frac{ar}{r + b^2 p}\Bigr)^2 = \bigl(a - b\,k(p)\bigr)^2 :$$

the slope of the Riccati map is the square of the closed-loop factor. On $p \ge 0$ this slope is nonnegative and nonincreasing, so $f$ is nondecreasing and concave there (constant when $a = 0$).

Let $f$ be the scalar Riccati map of , with $a, b \in \R$, $q \ge 0$ and $r > 0$.

  1. A fixed point $p \ge 0$ of $f$ is stabilizing, $\abs{a - bk(p)} < 1$, if and only if $f'(p) < 1$.
  2. If $q > 0$ and $b \neq 0$, $f$ has exactly one fixed point $p^\star \ge 0$, and $p^\star > 0$; every orbit $p_{j+1} = f(p_j)$ with $p_0 \ge 0$ converges to it monotonically, and the optimal closed-loop factor satisfies $\abs{a - bk(p^\star)} < 1$, with $p_j - p^\star$ shrinking asymptotically by the factor $(a - bk(p^\star))^2$ per step.

Proof. (1) By , $f'(p) = (a - bk(p))^2$. (2) Let $g(p) = f(p) - p$. Then $g(0) = q > 0$, $g$ is concave, and $g(p) \to -\infty$ because $f$ is bounded by $q + a^2r/b^2$; so $g$ has exactly one zero $p^\star$ on $p > 0$, and concavity gives $g'(p^\star) \le (g(p^\star) - g(0))/p^\star < 0$, that is, $f'(p^\star) < 1$. For $0 \le p < p^\star$ we have $p < f(p) \le f(p^\star) = p^\star$, so orbits below $p^\star$ increase to a fixed point, which must be $p^\star$; orbits above decrease in the same way. The rate is $f'(p^\star) = (a - bk(p^\star))^2$.

Detectability is not decoration. Take $q = 0$, $b \neq 0$ and $\abs a > 1$. Then $f(p) = a^2rp/(r + b^2p)$ has two fixed points, $0$ and $p^\star = r(a^2 - 1)/b^2$. The solution $p = 0$ is positive semidefinite and optimal in its own terms: with no state cost, doing nothing ($k = 0$) costs nothing, and the closed loop keeps the unstable factor $a$. Orbits from $p_0 > 0$ converge instead to $p^\star$, by the monotonicity argument in the proof of : the only zeros of $g(p) = f(p) - p$ on $p \ge 0$ are $0$ and $p^\star$, $g'(0) = a^2 - 1 > 0$ and $g(p) \to -\infty$, so $g > 0$ on $(0, p^\star)$ and $g < 0$ beyond. The closed-loop factor of $p^\star$ is $ar/(r + b^2p^\star) = 1/a$: the unstable pole is reflected in the unit circle. Uniqueness of the positive semidefinite solution fails because $(q^{1/2}, a) = (0, a)$ is not detectable in the discrete-time sense. For $a < -1$ the pair is detectable in the continuous-time sense, since $a < 0$, so the discrete-time notions in cannot be replaced by the continuous-time ones.

Continuous time: the algebraic Riccati equation

In the regulator problem ($Q \succeq 0$, $R \succ 0$), let $P = P^\T$ satisfy the continuous-time algebraic Riccati equation

$$A^\T P + PA - PBR^{-1}B^\T P + Q = 0$$

and be stabilizing: $A - BK$ is Hurwitz for $K = R^{-1}B^\T P$. Then for every input $u$ whose state satisfies $x(t) \to 0$,

$$J(u) = x_0^\T P x_0 + \int_0^\infty \bigl\lVert R^{1/2}\bigl(u + R^{-1}B^\T P x\bigr)\bigr\rVert^2 \dd t .$$

Consequently $u = -Kx$ is the unique optimal input among them, the optimal cost is $x_0^\T P x_0$, and the optimal closed loop $\dot x = (A - BK)x$ is asymptotically stable.

Proof. Along any trajectory, $\frac{\dd}{\dd t}(x^\T P x) = x^\T(A^\T P + PA)x + 2u^\T B^\T P x$, and the Riccati equation replaces $x^\T(A^\T P + PA)x$ by $x^\T(PBR^{-1}B^\T P - Q)x$. Hence

$$x^\T Q x + u^\T R u + \frac{\dd}{\dd t}\bigl(x^\T P x\bigr) = \bigl(u + R^{-1}B^\T P x\bigr)^\T R\,\bigl(u + R^{-1}B^\T P x\bigr).$$

Integrate over $[0, T]$ and let $T \to \infty$; the boundary term $x(T)^\T P x(T)$ tends to $0$. The integral on the right is nonnegative and vanishes only if $u = -Kx$ along the trajectory, whose state then solves $\dot x = (A - BK)x$.

The restriction to inputs whose state tends to 0 costs nothing under detectability: by , every input of finite cost has this property. below combines this with the existence of $P$.

Let $R \succ 0$, $Q = Q^\T$ and $H = \begin{bmatrix} A & -BR^{-1}B^\T\\ -Q & -A^\T\end{bmatrix} \in \R^{2n\times 2n}$.

  1. $\lambda \in \Lambda(H)$ implies $-\lambda \in \Lambda(H)$, with the same algebraic multiplicity. As $H$ is real, $\Lambda(H)$ is therefore symmetric about both axes, in particular about the imaginary axis.
  2. A symmetric $P$ solves the CARE if and only if, with $T = \begin{bmatrix} I & 0\\ P & I\end{bmatrix}$ and $K = R^{-1}B^\T P$, $$T^{-1} H T = \begin{bmatrix} A - BK & -BR^{-1}B^\T\\ 0 & -(A - BK)^\T\end{bmatrix}.$$ Then $\Lambda(H) = \Lambda(A - BK) \cup \bigl(-\Lambda(A - BK)\bigr)$, and the graph $\Range\begin{bmatrix} I\\ P\end{bmatrix}$ is an invariant subspace of $H$; for the stabilizing solution it is the stable one.

Proof. With $\mathcal J = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & I\\ -I & 0\end{bmatrix}$, the product $\mathcal J H = \begin{bmatrix} -Q & -A^\T\\ -A & BR^{-1}B^\T\end{bmatrix}$ is symmetric, so $\mathcal J H \mathcal J^{-1} = -H^\T$: $H$ is similar to $-H^\T$, whose eigenvalues are the negatives of those of $H$, with the same multiplicities. For item 2, multiply out: the upper blocks of $T^{-1}HT$ are $A - BR^{-1}B^\T P = A - BK$ and $-BR^{-1}B^\T$, the lower-left block is $-(A^\T P + PA - PBR^{-1}B^\T P + Q)$ and the lower-right block is $PBR^{-1}B^\T - A^\T = -(A - BK)^\T$. The spectrum of a block-triangular matrix is the union of the spectra of its diagonal blocks, and the first block column of $HT = T(T^{-1}HT)$ reads $H\begin{bmatrix} I\\ P\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} I\\ P\end{bmatrix}(A - BK)$, which is the invariance. When $A - BK$ is Hurwitz, $H$ acts on the $n$-dimensional graph with stable eigenvalues only, and its other $n$ eigenvalues, those of $-(A - BK)^\T$, lie in the open right half-plane, so the graph is the whole stable invariant subspace.

Let $Q \succeq 0$, $R \succ 0$, $(A, B)$ stabilizable and $(Q^{1/2}, A)$ detectable. For $\dot x = ax + bu$ with $b \neq 0$, $q > 0$, $r > 0$, the CARE is $2ap - (b^2/r)p^2 + q = 0$, with roots $p_\pm = \frac{r}{b^2}\bigl(a \pm \sqrt{a^2 + b^2q/r}\bigr)$ and closed-loop poles $a - (b^2/r)p_\pm = \mp\sqrt{a^2 + b^2q/r}$. Only $p_+$ is stabilizing, and it is the positive root found in the probe of . The Hamiltonian $\begin{bmatrix} a & -b^2/r\\ -q & -a\end{bmatrix}$ has eigenvalues $\pm\sqrt{a^2 + b^2q/r}$. The optimal pole depends on $a$ only through $a^2$: an unstable plant and its stable mirror image receive the same closed loop, at different cost.

For $A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1\\ 0 & 0\end{bmatrix}$, $B = \begin{bmatrix} 0\\ 1\end{bmatrix}$, $Q = I$ and $R = 1$, the stabilizing solution is $P = \begin{bmatrix} \sqrt3 & 1\\ 1 & \sqrt3\end{bmatrix}$: indeed $A^\T P + PA = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & \sqrt3\\ \sqrt3 & 2\end{bmatrix}$ and $PBB^\T P = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & \sqrt3\\ \sqrt3 & 3\end{bmatrix}$ differ by $-I$. The gain is $K = [1\ \ \sqrt3]$, the closed-loop polynomial $s^2 + \sqrt3\, s + 1$: natural frequency 1 and damping ratio $\sqrt3/2$.

The CARE is a Lyapunov equation in disguise. The cost of any stabilizing feedback is a quadratic form computed by a Lyapunov equation, the optimal gain is the one whose Lyapunov solution also solves the CARE, and the optimal cost splits into a state part and an input part. displays the split.

Let $Q \succeq 0$ and $R \succ 0$.

  1. If $A_F = A - BF$ is Hurwitz, the feedback $u = -Fx$ has cost $J = x_0^\T X_F\, x_0$, where $X_F$ is the unique solution of $A_F^\T X + XA_F + Q + F^\T RF = 0$. The state part $\int_0^\infty x^\T Qx\,\dd t$ and the input part $\int_0^\infty u^\T Ru\,\dd t$ of the cost are $x_0^\T X x_0$ for the solutions $X$ of the same equation with $Q$ alone, respectively $F^\T RF$ alone, in place of $Q + F^\T RF$.
  2. If $P$ is the stabilizing solution of the CARE, $K = R^{-1}B^\T P$ and $A_K = A - BK$, then $A_K^\T P + PA_K + Q + K^\T RK = 0$, so $X_K = P$, and along the optimal closed loop $\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\bigl(x^\T Px\bigr) = -\bigl(x^\T Qx + u^\T Ru\bigr)$.

Proof. (1) Along $x(t) = e^{tA_F}x_0$ the integrand is $x^\T(Q + F^\T RF)x$. For a Hurwitz matrix $A_F$ and every symmetric $M$, the equation $A_F^\T X + XA_F + M = 0$ has the unique solution $X = \int_0^\infty e^{tA_F^\T}Me^{tA_F}\dd t$; the proof of Lyapunov's theorem (chapter 7) shows this using only that $A_F$ is Hurwitz. Then $x_0^\T Xx_0 = \int_0^\infty x(t)^\T Mx(t)\,\dd t$, and the split follows by linearity in $M$. (2) With $K = R^{-1}B^\T P$ we have $K^\T B^\T P = PBK = K^\T RK = PBR^{-1}B^\T P$, so the left side equals $A^\T P + PA - PBR^{-1}B^\T P + Q = 0$, and uniqueness in (1) gives $X_K = P$. Along $\dot x = A_Kx$, the derivative of $x^\T Px$ is $x^\T(A_K^\T P + PA_K)x$, which equals $-x^\T(Q + K^\T RK)x$, and $x^\T K^\T RKx = u^\T Ru$.

Let $Q \succeq 0$, $R \succ 0$, $(Q^{1/2}, A)$ detectable, and let $K_0$ be a gain with $A - BK_0$ Hurwitz. For $j = 0, 1, 2, \dots$ let $P_j$ solve the Lyapunov equation

Extreme weights, the symmetric root locus and guaranteed margins

Let $\dot x = ax + bu$ with $b \neq 0$, state weight $q > 0$ and input weight $r = \rho > 0$, as in . The optimal closed-loop pole is $s_\rho = -\sqrt{a^2 + b^2 q/\rho}$.

  1. Expensive control, $\rho \to \infty$: $s_\rho \to -\abs a$. A stable open-loop pole stays where it is, an unstable one is mirrored to $-a$. For $a < 0$ the optimal cost tends to the open-loop cost, $p \to q/(2\abs a)$; for $a > 0$ it grows like $2a\rho/b^2$.
  2. Cheap control, $\rho \to 0$: $s_\rho \approx -\abs b\sqrt{q/\rho} \to -\infty$, the gain satisfies $\abs k \approx \sqrt{q/\rho}$, and $p \approx \sqrt{q\rho}/\abs b \to 0$.

Proof. Read off the closed forms $p = (\rho/b^2)\bigl(a + \sqrt{a^2 + b^2q/\rho}\bigr)$ and $k = bp/\rho = \bigl(a + \sqrt{a^2 + b^2q/\rho}\bigr)/b$. For $a < 0$ rationalize: $p = q/\bigl(\sqrt{a^2 + b^2q/\rho} - a\bigr)$.

Let $Q \succeq 0$, $R \succ 0$, let $P$ be the stabilizing solution of the CARE and $K = R^{-1}B^\T P$, and put $L_K(s) = K(sI - A)^{-1}B$ and $G_Q(s) = Q^{1/2}(sI - A)^{-1}B$. Wherever both sides are defined,

$$\bigl(I + L_K(-s)\bigr)^\T R\,\bigl(I + L_K(s)\bigr) = R + G_Q(-s)^\T G_Q(s).$$

On the imaginary axis this gives $(I + L_K(i\omega))^* R\,(I + L_K(i\omega)) \succeq R$, and for a single input with $R = \rho$,

The Kalman filter

Given $x_{k+1} = Ax_k + w_k$ and $y_k = Cx_k + v_k$, compute recursively the best estimate of $x_k$ from the measurements $y_0, \dots, y_k$. (A known input $Bu_k$ added to the state equation is omitted here; shows that it only shifts the predicted mean.)

$x_0 \sim \mathcal N(\hat x_{0|-1}, \Sigma_{0|-1})$, $w_k \sim \mathcal N(0, W)$ and $v_k \sim \mathcal N(0, V)$ with $W \succeq 0$, $V \succ 0$, and $x_0, w_0, w_1, \dots, v_0, v_1, \dots$ mutually independent. "Best" means minimum mean-square error: among all functions $\hat x$ of the data, minimize $\mathbb E\norm{x_k - \hat x}^2$. The minimizer is the conditional mean $\mathbb E[x_k \mid y_0, \dots, y_k]$ ().

Let $x \in \R^n$ and $z$ be random vectors with $\mathbb E\norm{x}^2 < \infty$, and let $m(z) = \mathbb E[x \mid z]$. For every estimate $\hat x = g(z)$ with $\mathbb E\norm{g(z)}^2 < \infty$,

$$\mathbb E\norm{x - \hat x}^2 = \mathbb E\norm{x - m(z)}^2 + \mathbb E\norm{m(z) - \hat x}^2 ,$$

so the conditional mean minimizes the mean-square error, and every other minimizer equals it with probability 1.

Proof. Write $x - \hat x = (x - m) + (m - \hat x)$. The cross term vanishes, because $m - \hat x$ is a function of $z$ and $\mathbb E[x - m \mid z] = 0$:

$$\mathbb E\bigl[(m - \hat x)^\T(x - m)\bigr] = \mathbb E\bigl[(m - \hat x)^\T\,\mathbb E[x - m \mid z]\bigr] = 0 .$$

Geometrically, $m(z)$ is the orthogonal projection of $x$ onto the closed subspace of square-integrable functions of $z$, as in the projection theorem (chapter 5).

A random walk observed in noise: $x_{k+1} = x_k + w_k$, $y_k = x_k + v_k$ with scalar variances $W > 0$ and $V > 0$. Anticipating , the steady-state prior variance $\sigma$ is the positive root of $\sigma^2 = W(\sigma + V)$ and the gain is $L = \sigma/(\sigma + V)$. The steady-state estimate is $\hat x_{k|k} = (1 - L)\hat x_{k-1|k-1} + Ly_k$, so it weights the measurement taken $j$ steps back by $L(1 - L)^j$. If $W \ll V$, then $\sigma \approx \sqrt{WV}$ and $L \approx \sqrt{W/V}$ is small: the weights decay slowly, over roughly $1/L \approx \sqrt{V/W}$ steps. If $W \gg V$, then $L \to 1$: trust the latest measurement. Since $\sigma/V$ solves $(\sigma/V)^2 = (W/V)(\sigma/V + 1)$, the gain depends only on the ratio $W/V$, as the best observer speed did in the scalar observer example of chapter 10.

This is the claimed conditional distribution.

Proof. Substitute $\Sigma_{k|k} = \Sigma_{k|k-1} - L_kC\Sigma_{k|k-1}$ into $\Sigma_{k+1|k} = A\Sigma_{k|k}A^\T + W$ and compare with $\mathcal R$ after replacing $A, B, Q, R$ by $A^\T, C^\T, W, V$. For the pairs: an eigenvalue $\lambda$ is uncontrollable for $(A^\T, C^\T)$ exactly when $\rank[\lambda I - A^\T,\ C^\T] < n$, and unobservable for $(C, A)$ exactly when $\rank\begin{bmatrix}\lambda I - A\\ C\end{bmatrix} < n$ (the Popov–Belevitch–Hautus (PBH) test, chapter 9). Let $\dot x = Ax + w$ and $y = Cx + v$ in continuous time, where $w$ and $v$ are independent zero-mean white Gaussian noises with intensities $W \succeq 0$ and $V \succ 0$, that is $\mathbb E[w(t)w(\tau)^\T] = W\delta(t - \tau)$ and $\mathbb E[v(t)v(\tau)^\T] = V\delta(t - \tau)$ with $\delta$ the Dirac delta, independent of a Gaussian initial state $x(0)$, and let $(C, A)$ be detectable and $(A, W^{1/2})$ stabilizable.

The conditional mean of $x(t)$ given $y$ on $[0, t]$ is generated by the Kalman–Bucy filter $\dot{\hat x} = A\hat x + L(t)(y - C\hat x)$, started at $\hat x(0) = \mathbb E\,x(0)$, with $L(t) = \Sigma(t)C^\T V^{-1}$, where the error covariance $\Sigma(t)$ solves $\dot\Sigma = A\Sigma + \Sigma A^\T - \Sigma C^\T V^{-1}C\Sigma + W$ from the covariance of $x(0)$. Control and estimation are the same mathematics read in opposite directions of time. The dictionary:

Linear quadratic Gaussian control: $\dot x = Ax + Bu + w$, $y = Cx + v$ with independent white Gaussian noises of intensities $W \succeq 0$ and $V \succ 0$, and the average cost $\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac1T\,\mathbb E\int_0^T (x^\T Q x + u^\T R u)\,\dd t$, minimized over causal controllers, in which $u(t)$ may depend only on $y$ over $[0, t]$. Both pairs satisfy the stabilizability and detectability assumptions above.

In this setting the optimal controller is $u = -K\hat x$, where $K = R^{-1}B^\T P$ is the regulator gain of and $\hat x$ is the steady-state Kalman–Bucy estimate of , $\dot{\hat x} = A\hat x + Bu + L(y - C\hat x)$. The optimal average cost is

$$J^\star = \tr(PW) + \tr\bigl(\Sigma K^\T R K\bigr) = \tr(Q\Sigma) + \tr\bigl(P L V L^\T\bigr),$$

and its first term, $\tr(PW)$, is the optimal average cost of the same problem with the state measured exactly; the second is the price of estimating it.

Proof sketch

Optimality is proved in the sources below; here are the two identities. With the state measured, $u = -Kx$ gives the closed loop $\dot x = (A - BK)x + w$, whose stationary covariance $X$ solves $(A - BK)X + X(A - BK)^\T + W = 0$, and the average cost is $\tr\bigl((Q + K^\T RK)X\bigr)$. By item 2 of , $Q + K^\T RK = -(A - BK)^\T P - P(A - BK)$, and moving $P$ around the trace gives $\tr\bigl((Q + K^\T RK)X\bigr) = \tr(PW)$. The two forms of $J^\star$ agree because their difference, $\tr\bigl(P(W - LVL^\T)\bigr) - \tr\bigl((Q - K^\T RK)\Sigma\bigr)$, vanishes: the filter equation gives $W - LVL^\T = -A\Sigma - \Sigma A^\T$, the CARE gives $Q - K^\T RK = -A^\T P - PA$, and $\tr(PA\Sigma + P\Sigma A^\T) = \tr\bigl((A^\T P + PA)\Sigma\bigr)$.

Statement and proof: Kwakernaak and Sivan, ch. 3 (state feedback with noise) and ch. 5; for discrete time, Bertsekas, vol. 1, chapter on problems with imperfect state information. By the separation theorem of chapter 10, the closed-loop spectrum is $\Lambda(A - BK) \cup \Lambda(A - LC)$.

Optimality does not give robustness. Each half of the controller is robust in its own loop: the regulator has the margins of , and for a single output the filter loop $C(sI - A)^{-1}L$ satisfies the same inequality (item 3 of ). J. C. Doyle ("Guaranteed margins for LQG regulators", IEEE Trans. Autom. Control 23 (1978) 756–757) exhibited a second-order plant for which, as the state weight and the process noise intensity grow, two reasonable design moves, the interval of input gains that the combined loop tolerates shrinks to the single point 1. The reason is structural. The controller is $u = -K_{\rm ob}(s)\,y$ with $K_{\rm ob}(s) = K(sI - A + BK + LC)^{-1}L$ (observer-based stabilization, chapter 10), so the loop broken at the plant input is $K_{\rm ob}(s)\,C(sI - A)^{-1}B$, not $L_K(s)$. Chapter 12 works the example and describes loop transfer recovery.

Optimal feedback control of movement. E. Todorov and M. I. Jordan (Nat. Neurosci. 5 (2002) 1226–1235) modeled motor coordination as stochastic optimal feedback control: the optimal controller corrects only those deviations that interfere with the task goal (their minimal intervention principle), so variability accumulates along task-irrelevant directions, as observed in redundant movements. Their models use signal-dependent motor noise, under which estimation and control no longer separate exactly (Todorov, Neural Comput. 2005). The architecture is the one of : an internal model that predicts the consequences of motor commands, an estimate that fuses this prediction with noisy, delayed sensory feedback, and a feedback gain acting on the estimate.

Kalman-filter decoders for brain–computer interfaces. The hidden state is the intended cursor or hand kinematics with a linear stochastic model, and the observation is the vector of binned firing rates, modeled as $y_k = Cx_k + v_k$ (linear tuning); $A$, $W$, $C$ and $V$ are fitted by least squares on training data and the decoder is the Kalman filter (W. Wu, Y. Gao, E. Bienenstock, J. P. Donoghue and M. J. Black, Neural Comput. 18 (2006) 80–118). V. Gilja and colleagues (Nat. Neurosci. 15 (2012) 1752–1757) improved closed-loop performance by refitting the filter on training data corrected for the user's intention. In closed-loop use the estimator sits inside a feedback loop whose other controller is the user's brain.

Self-check

  1. For $\dot x = x + u$ with $q = 3$ and $r = 1$, find the optimal cost coefficient $p$, the gain and the closed-loop pole.

    Answer

    The CARE $2p - p^2 + 3 = 0$ has the positive root $p = 3$, so $k = bp/r = 3$ and the pole is $1 - 3 = -2 = -\sqrt{a^2 + b^2q/r}$. The other root, $p = -1$, gives the unstable pole $+2$.

  2. Show that along the optimal trajectory $\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\bigl(x^\T P x\bigr) = -(x^\T Q x + u^\T R u)$.

    Answer

    Set $u = -Kx$ in the identity in the proof of : the right-hand side vanishes. Equivalently, the CARE is the closed-loop Lyapunov equation $(A - BK)^\T P + P(A - BK) + Q + K^\T RK = 0$ ().

  3. Why do the Kalman covariances not depend on the measurements, and what follows for implementation?

    Answer

    The conditional covariance in involves only the joint covariance, not the observed value. The gains $L_k$ can therefore be computed offline, and under the hypotheses of they converge, so in steady state a constant gain suffices.

  4. For the random walk $x_{k+1} = x_k + w_k$, $y_k = x_k + v_k$ with $W = 1$ and $V = 2$, find the steady-state prior variance, the gain and the posterior variance.

    Answer

    $\sigma^2 = W(\sigma + V)$ gives $\sigma^2 - \sigma - 2 = 0$, so $\sigma = 2$. The gain is $\sigma/(\sigma + V) = 1/2$ and the posterior variance is $\sigma - L\sigma = 1$.

  5. The continuous-time regulator gain $K$ has a gain margin from $1/2$ to $\infty$. Does the linear quadratic Gaussian controller built with the same $K$?

    Answer

    No. The margins of hold for the loop $L_K(s)$ with the state measured. With an observer, the loop at the plant input is $K_{\rm ob}(s)C(sI - A)^{-1}B$, and Doyle's example shows its margins can be arbitrarily small ().
