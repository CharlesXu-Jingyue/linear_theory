Linear theory in neural circuits and beyond

Recurrent circuits are nonlinear, but near an operating point they are linear systems, and much of what circuit models say about amplification, memory and instability is linear algebra of the connectivity matrix. This chapter derives those mechanisms in small models, states what particular papers showed with them, and ends with linear methods that reach nonlinear, data-driven and distributed problems.

Linearization of rate networks

A recurrent network of $N$ units is a nonlinear ordinary differential equation. Near an operating point, which linear system does it become, and when does that linear system predict what the network does?

The rate network

$$\tau\dot r = -r + W\phi(r) + u(t)$$
$r(t) \in \R^N$
activations of $N$ units (real $N$-vectors); in this form $r_i$ is an input variable and $\phi(r_i)$ the unit's rate
$W \in \R^{N\times N}$
connectivity, $W_{ij}$ the weight from unit $j$ to unit $i$
$\phi$
smooth increasing gain function, applied to each entry
$u(t)$, $\tau > 0$
external input; single-unit time constant

For constant input $u_0$, a fixed point $r^*$ satisfies $r^* = W\phi(r^*) + u_0$. Write $I$ for the identity, $\diag(\cdot)$ for a diagonal matrix, $\Lambda(\cdot)$ for the spectrum and $\alpha(J) = \max\Re\Lambda(J)$ for the spectral abscissa ($\Re$ is the real part).

Let $D = \diag\big(\phi'(r_1^*), \dots, \phi'(r_N^*)\big)$. For deviations $\delta r = r - r^*$ and $\delta u = u - u_0$,

$$\dot{\delta r} = J\,\delta r + \tau^{-1}\delta u + O(\norm{\delta r}^2),\qquad J = \frac{-I + WD}{\tau},$$

so the eigenvalues of $J$ are $\big(\lambda_i(WD) - 1\big)/\tau$, with $\lambda_i(\cdot)$ the eigenvalues.

Proof. Entrywise Taylor expansion gives $\phi(r^* + \delta r) = \phi(r^*) + D\,\delta r + O(\norm{\delta r}^2)$; substitute and use the fixed-point equation.

An equivalence and its invariant. The other common form, $\tau\dot\rho = -\rho + \phi(W\rho + u)$, has the rates $\rho$ as its state. The substitution $r = W\rho + u_0$ matches its fixed points with those above, and the local spectrum is the invariant of the correspondence; the eigenvectors are not. This is .

Let $\phi$ be continuously differentiable and let the input $u = u_0$ be constant. (i) The maps $\rho^* \mapsto W\rho^* + u_0$ and $r^* \mapsto \phi(r^*)$ are mutually inverse bijections between the fixed points of $\tau\dot\rho = -\rho + \phi(W\rho + u_0)$ and those of $\tau\dot r = -r + W\phi(r) + u_0$. (ii) At corresponding fixed points the Jacobians are $(-I + DW)/\tau$ and $(-I + WD)/\tau$, with the same $D = \diag\phi'(r^*)$. They have the same characteristic polynomial; if $D$ is invertible they are similar, $-I + WD = D^{-1}(-I + DW)D$, and $v \mapsto Dv$ maps eigenvectors of the second to eigenvectors of the first.

Proof. (i) If $\rho^* = \phi(W\rho^* + u_0)$, then $r^* = W\rho^* + u_0$ satisfies $\phi(r^*) = \rho^*$, hence $r^* = W\phi(r^*) + u_0$. If $r^* = W\phi(r^*) + u_0$, then $\rho^* = \phi(r^*)$ satisfies $W\rho^* + u_0 = r^*$, hence $\rho^* = \phi(W\rho^* + u_0)$. The two composites are the identity on the respective sets of fixed points. (ii) The chain rule gives the Jacobian $(-I + D'W)/\tau$ with $D' = \diag\phi'(W\rho^* + u_0)$, and $D' = D$ because $W\rho^* + u_0 = r^*$; gives the other Jacobian. The characteristic polynomials of $DW$ and $WD$ agree by the $AB$ and $BA$ theorem (chapter 3) with square factors. Finally $D^{-1}(DW)D = WD$, and $(-I + DW)Dv = D(-I + WD)v$.

Suppose the fixed point is hyperbolic: no eigenvalue of $J$ has zero real part. Then there are neighbourhoods of $r^*$ and of $0$ and a homeomorphism between them that maps trajectories of the nonlinear network (with $u = u_0$) onto trajectories of $\dot\xi = J\xi$, preserving the direction of time. In particular $r^*$ is asymptotically stable if $\alpha(J) < 0$ and unstable if $\alpha(J) > 0$.

Proof: Perko, Differential Equations and Dynamical Systems, 3rd ed., §2.8. The stability statement alone needs no hyperbolicity and is Lyapunov's indirect method: if $\alpha(J) < 0$, the quadratic Lyapunov function of Lyapunov's theorem (chapter 7) is a local Lyapunov function for the nonlinear network; that $\alpha(J) > 0$ implies instability is stated in Perko, §2.9.

Without hyperbolicity the linearization is silent. The scalar equations $\dot x = -x^3$ and $\dot x = x^3$ both have Jacobian $0$ at $x = 0$; the first is asymptotically stable and the second unstable. A line attractor () has exactly this defect: an eigenvalue of $J$ at $0$.

What the conjugacy keeps and what it loses. By the topological classification of hyperbolic linear flows (chapter 7), two such flows on $\R^N$ are topologically conjugate exactly when their stable subspaces have the same dimension. So the conjugacy keeps the dimensions of the stable and unstable manifolds, but not rotation and not transient growth: a stable node and a stable focus are conjugate, and so are a normal and a strongly non-normal stable $J$. The quantitative content of the next two sections, amplification and time constants, comes from the linearization itself, not from the theorem: by differentiable dependence on initial conditions (Perko, §2.3), $\delta r(t) = e^{tJ}\delta r(0) + o(\norm{\delta r(0)})$ uniformly for $t$ in any bounded interval, so the linear predictions hold for small deviations over finite times.

Slow points in trained networks. Sussillo and Barak (2013) locate fixed points of trained recurrent networks numerically, by minimizing $q(r) = \frac12\norm{F(r)}^2$ for the vector field $F(r) = \big(-r + W\phi(r) + u_0\big)/\tau$. Zeros of $q$ are fixed points; they call local minima with small positive $q$ slow points, and argue that near them the linearization still describes the dynamics over the time scales of the task. In a network trained to store three bits they found stable fixed points for the stored patterns, with saddles between them that mediate the transitions. Mante, Sussillo, Shenoy and Newsome (2013) used the same analysis on a network trained to integrate one of two sensory inputs depending on context, and found in each context a set of fixed points approximating a line attractor, with one eigenvalue of the linearization near zero and the others strongly negative.

Inputs are read by left eigenvectors. Let $J$ be diagonalizable, $J = \sum_i \lambda_i v_i\ell_i^\T$, with right eigenvectors $v_i$ and left eigenvectors $\ell_i$ normalized by $\ell_i^\T v_j = \delta_{ij}$ (Kronecker delta). An input pulse $\delta u = b\,\delta(t)$ along a direction $b$ ($\delta(t)$ the Dirac delta) enters the linearization of as $\tau^{-1}b\,\delta(t)$ and produces $\delta r(t) = \tau^{-1}e^{tJ}b = \tau^{-1}\sum_i e^{\lambda_i t}(\ell_i^\T b)\,v_i$ for $t > 0$, by the modal expansion (chapter 7). A slow mode keeps $\ell^\T b$, the projection of the input on its left eigenvector, not on the direction $v$ along which activity is stored. In Mante et al.'s networks both inputs had substantial projections onto the line attractor, yet these projections did not predict which input was integrated; that was decided by the projection on the left eigenvector of the slow mode, which they call the selection vector, and that vector changed with context, aligning with the relevant input and becoming orthogonal to the irrelevant one.

Non-normal amplification in excitatory and inhibitory networks

Cortical circuits combine strong recurrent excitation with strong inhibition. Can such a network be stable, with every eigenvalue safely in the left half-plane, and still amplify particular activity patterns without slowing them down?

Two populations, excitatory and inhibitory, with linear dynamics (gain absorbed into the weights) and time measured in units of $\tau$:

$$\tau\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\begin{bmatrix} r_E\\ r_I\end{bmatrix} = -\begin{bmatrix} r_E\\ r_I\end{bmatrix} + W\begin{bmatrix} r_E\\ r_I\end{bmatrix} + u(t),\qquad W = \begin{bmatrix} w & -kw\\ w & -kw\end{bmatrix}.$$
$r_E$, $r_I$
deviations of the excitatory and inhibitory rates from baseline
$w \ge 0$
strength of the excitatory projections (to E and to I)
$k \ge 1$
inhibition dominance: the inhibitory projections have strength $kw$; Murphy and Miller write $k_I$

The columns respect Dale's law: the first column (outputs of E) is nonnegative and the second (outputs of I) nonpositive. Let $e_+ = (1, 1)^\T/\sqrt2$ (the sum pattern) and $e_- = (1, -1)^\T/\sqrt2$ (the difference pattern), and write $r = a_+e_+ + a_-e_-$.

In the orthonormal basis $Q = [\,e_+\ \ e_-\,]$,

$$Q^\T WQ = \begin{bmatrix} -w(k - 1) & w_{\mathrm{FF}}\\ 0 & 0\end{bmatrix},\qquad w_{\mathrm{FF}} = w(1 + k).$$

This is a Schur form (chapter 3): the eigenvalues of $W$ are $-w(k-1)$, carried by the sum pattern, and $0$; the difference pattern drives the sum pattern through the feedforward weight $w_{\mathrm{FF}}$, and nothing feeds back.

Proof. $W = w\,\mathbf 1 c^\T$ with $\mathbf 1 = (1, 1)^\T$ and $c = (1, -k)^\T$, a rank-one matrix. Hence

$$We_\pm = w\,\mathbf 1\,(c^\T e_\pm) = w(1 \mp k)\,\mathbf 1/\sqrt2 = w(1 \mp k)\,e_+,$$

which gives the two columns.

With $u = 0$, $a_-(0) = 1$, $a_+(0) = 0$ and $\lambda_+ = 1 + w(k - 1)$, the amplitudes obey $\tau\dot a_- = -a_-$ and $\tau\dot a_+ = -\lambda_+a_+ + w_{\mathrm{FF}}\,a_-$, so $a_-(t) = e^{-t/\tau}$ and

$$a_+(t) = \frac{1 + k}{k - 1}\Big(e^{-t/\tau} - e^{-\lambda_+ t/\tau}\Big)\quad (k > 1),\qquad a_+(t) = 2w\,\frac{t}{\tau}\,e^{-t/\tau}\quad (k = 1).$$

For $k = 1$ the peak is $2w/e$ at $t = \tau$. A sustained difference input $u = u_0e_-$ produces the steady state $a_- = u_0$ and $a_+ = \dfrac{w(1 + k)}{1 + w(k - 1)}\,u_0$.

Proof. Variation of constants gives

$$a_+(t) = \frac{w_{\mathrm{FF}}}{\tau}\int_0^t e^{-\lambda_+(t - s)/\tau}e^{-s/\tau}\,\dd s = \frac{w_{\mathrm{FF}}}{\lambda_+ - 1}\Big(e^{-t/\tau} - e^{-\lambda_+t/\tau}\Big),$$

and $w_{\mathrm{FF}}/(\lambda_+ - 1) = (1 + k)/(k - 1)$; for $k = 1$, $\lambda_+ = 1$ and the integral is $w_{\mathrm{FF}}\,t\,e^{-t/\tau}/\tau$. The steady state solves the two equations with $\dot a_\pm = 0$.

Extreme cases. Exact balance, $k = 1$: $W$ is nilpotent, both eigenvalues of $J$ equal $-1/\tau$, and the amplification grows linearly with $w$. Strong excitation with $k > 1$ fixed: as $w \to \infty$ the sum pattern decays ever faster ($\lambda_+ \to \infty$), yet the peak of $a_+$ saturates below $(1 + k)/(k - 1)$, so large gains need $k$ close to 1. Weak coupling: below a threshold no initial condition grows even transiently. The precise statements are .

In the two-population model with $w > 0$ and $u = 0$, let $\omega(J) = \lambda_{\max}\big((J + J^\T)/2\big)$ be the numerical abscissa, with $\lambda_{\max}$ the largest eigenvalue. (i) If $k = 1$, both eigenvalues of $J$ equal $-1/\tau$ and $J$ is a single $2\times 2$ Jordan block. (ii) After a unit difference kick ($a_-(0) = 1$, $a_+(0) = 0$), the sum amplitude peaks at $t^* = \tau\ln\lambda_+/(\lambda_+ - 1)$, with

$$\max_{t\ge0}a_+(t) = w_{\mathrm{FF}}\,\lambda_+^{-\lambda_+/(\lambda_+ - 1)}\quad (k > 1),\qquad \max_{t\ge0}a_+(t) = \frac{w_{\mathrm{FF}}}{e} = \frac{2w}{e}\ \text{ at } t^* = \tau\quad (k = 1).$$

For $k > 1$, $0 \le a_+(t) < (1 + k)/(k - 1)$ for all $t \ge 0$, and the peak increases with $w$ toward this bound; hence a peak above $G > 1$ requires $k < (G + 1)/(G - 1)$. (iii) If $a_-(0) = 0$, then $\norm{r(t)} = \abs{a_+(0)}\,e^{-\lambda_+t/\tau}$: only initial states with a difference component can grow. (iv) $\omega(J) > 0$ if and only if $w_{\mathrm{FF}}^2 > 4\lambda_+$. If so, some initial state grows at first; otherwise $\norm{e^{tJ}} \le 1$ for all $t \ge 0$ and no initial state grows, even transiently.

Proof. (i) $W^2 = w\,(c^\T\mathbf 1)\,W = w(1 - k)W$ vanishes for $k = 1$, so $\tau J + I = W \neq 0$ is nilpotent: $J$ has the single eigenvalue $-1/\tau$ and is not diagonalizable. (ii) For $k > 1$, gives $a_+(t) = \frac{w_{\mathrm{FF}}}{\lambda_+ - 1}\big(e^{-t/\tau} - e^{-\lambda_+t/\tau}\big)$ with $\lambda_+ > 1$. Its derivative vanishes where $e^{(\lambda_+ - 1)t/\tau} = \lambda_+$, which gives $t^*$, and substituting gives the peak; the case $k = 1$ is in . Since $0 \le e^{-t/\tau} - e^{-\lambda_+t/\tau} < 1$ and $w_{\mathrm{FF}}/(\lambda_+ - 1) = (1 + k)/(k - 1)$, the bound follows. The peak equals $\frac{1 + k}{k - 1}h(\lambda_+)$ with $h(\lambda) = (\lambda - 1)\lambda^{-\lambda/(\lambda - 1)}$; $\frac{\dd}{\dd\lambda}\ln h(\lambda) = \ln\lambda/(\lambda - 1)^2 > 0$, $h(\lambda) \to 1$ as $\lambda \to \infty$, and $\lambda_+ = 1 + w(k - 1)$ increases with $w$. (iii) $a_-(t) = a_-(0)e^{-t/\tau} = 0$, so $\tau\dot a_+ = -\lambda_+a_+$, and $\norm{r} = \norm{(a_+, a_-)}$ because $Q$ is orthogonal. (iv) The numerical abscissa is invariant under orthogonal similarity, and in the Schur basis the symmetric part of $\tau J$ is $S = \begin{bmatrix}-\lambda_+ & w_{\mathrm{FF}}/2\\ w_{\mathrm{FF}}/2 & -1\end{bmatrix}$. Its trace is negative, so it has a positive eigenvalue if and only if $\det S = \lambda_+ - w_{\mathrm{FF}}^2/4 < 0$. The rest is the envelope theorem (chapter 6): $\norm{e^{tJ}} \le e^{t\omega(J)}$, and the right derivative of $\norm{e^{tJ}}$ at $t = 0$ is $\omega(J)$.

Eigenvalues do not see the amplification. The normal network $\tau\dot r = Q\diag\big(-\lambda_+, -1\big)Q^\T r$, with Jacobian $J_N = Q\diag(-\lambda_+, -1)Q^\T/\tau$, has the same eigenvalues as the $J$ above, yet $\norm{e^{tJ_N}} = e^{-t/\tau} \le 1$ for all $t \ge 0$, and its steady-state response to a sustained difference input is that input pattern itself, with gain 1. The two networks are indistinguishable by their spectra, yet only the E/I network turns a sustained difference input into a sum-pattern response, of amplitude $w(1 + k)/(1 + w(k - 1))$ per unit input.

What the papers showed. Murphy and Miller (2009) introduced this model and the Schur analysis: strong recurrent excitation stabilized by strong feedback inhibition amplifies difference patterns into sum patterns ("balanced amplification") without slowing the dynamics: every eigenvalue of $\tau J$ is at most $-1$ (), so no mode is slower than a single unit, and the sum pattern's own time constant, $\tau/\lambda_+$, is shorter still. They showed in a detailed biophysical model of cat primary visual cortex that this mechanism can account for spontaneous activity resembling orientation maps. Hennequin, Vogels and Gerstner (2014) started from strong random excitatory connectivity, which is unstable, and used control-theoretic optimization of the inhibitory connections, minimizing a smoothed spectral abscissa of $W$, to move every eigenvalue of $W$ to the left of $\Re\lambda = 1$, so that the linearized dynamics are stable; the resulting stability-optimized circuits are strongly non-normal, produce large transient responses from specific initial states after a preparatory phase, and can drive multidimensional movement-like outputs, with tightly balanced excitation and inhibition.

Memory: integrators, line attractors and feedforward chains

Hold a quantity, such as eye position or an accumulated decision variable, after its input has ended, using units whose own time constant $\tau$ is tens of milliseconds.

A linear network $\tau\dot r = -r + Wr + b\,u(t)$ with input weights $b \in \R^N$ and scalar input $u(t)$. Suppose $W$ has a real simple eigenvalue $\lambda$ with right eigenvector $v$ ($Wv = \lambda v$) and left eigenvector $\ell$ ($\ell^\T W = \lambda\ell^\T$), normalized so that $\ell^\T v = 1$; this is possible because $\ell^\T v \neq 0$ for a simple eigenvalue (the first-order perturbation theorem, chapter 6).

The modal coordinate $a(t) = \ell^\T r(t)$ obeys, independently of the other modes,

$$\tau\dot a = -(1 - \lambda)\,a + (\ell^\T b)\,u(t).$$

For $\lambda = 1$ it is a perfect integrator, $a(t) = a(0) + \frac{\ell^\T b}{\tau}\int_0^t u(s)\,\dd s$; for $\lambda < 1$ it forgets with the effective time constant $\tau_{\mathrm{eff}} = \tau/(1 - \lambda)$; for $\lambda > 1$ it grows with time constant $\tau/(\lambda - 1)$. If $\lambda = 1$ and every other eigenvalue of $W$ has real part below 1, then with $u = 0$ the fixed points form the line $\spn(v)$ and every trajectory converges to $(\ell^\T r(0))\,v$: a line attractor.

Proof. Multiply the network equation on the left by $\ell^\T$ and use $\ell^\T W = \lambda\ell^\T$. The scalar equation is solved by variation of constants. For the last claim, $J = (-I + W)/\tau$ has a simple eigenvalue $0$ with eigenvector $v$ and all other eigenvalues in the open left half-plane. The fixed points form $\Null(W - I) = \spn(v)$, since the eigenvalue $1$ of $W$ is simple. The subspace $\Null(\ell^\T)$ is invariant under $J$ and carries all its other eigenvalues, so $e^{Jt}(I - v\ell^\T) \to 0$, while $e^{Jt}v\ell^\T = v\ell^\T$; hence $e^{Jt} \to v\ell^\T$ as $t \to \infty$.

Simple and extreme cases, all read off . With $\lambda = 0$ there is no feedback and the mode forgets with the single-unit time constant $\tau$. As $\lambda \to 1$ from below, $\tau_{\mathrm{eff}}$ grows without bound, continuously, but at $\lambda = 1$ the type of the flow changes: if the other eigenvalues of $W$ lie to the left of $\Re\lambda = 1$, an isolated stable equilibrium becomes a line of equilibria. Just above 1 the same mode runs away, so the integrator sits exactly on a stability boundary.

With $\tau = 100$ ms, a memory with $\tau_{\mathrm{eff}} = 10$ s requires $\abs{1 - \lambda} = 0.01$; with $\tau = 10$ ms it requires $0.001$. Seung (1996) interpreted the persistent activity of the oculomotor neural integrator, which holds the eyes still between saccades, as a line of fixed points, and showed that linear and nonlinear networks produce line-attractor dynamics when learning tunes their positive feedback precisely.

A line attractor is not robust. Take $\lambda = 1$ in the setting above and perturb $W$ to $W + \eps\,v\ell^\T$ with a small real $\eps \neq 0$. Since $\ell^\T v = 1$, $(W + \eps v\ell^\T)v = (1 + \eps)v$ and $\ell^\T(W + \eps v\ell^\T) = (1 + \eps)\ell^\T$, so by the stored mode now obeys $\tau\dot a = \eps a + (\ell^\T b)u$: memory decays for $\eps < 0$ and grows for $\eps > 0$, with time constant $\tau/\abs\eps$. Almost every perturbation acts this way: by the first-order perturbation theorem (chapter 6), $W + \eps E$ has an eigenvalue $1 + \eps\,\ell^\T Ev + o(\eps)$, which differs from 1 for all small $\eps \neq 0$ whenever $\ell^\T Ev \neq 0$, and $\abs{\ell^\T Ev} \le \norm{E}\,\norm{v}\norm{\ell}$, the size of $E$ times the condition number of the eigenvalue. The line attractor is also non-hyperbolic, so, as in , the linearization cannot decide whether the remembered value of a nonlinear network drifts, or in which direction; the nonlinear terms decide.

Consider the chain $\tau\dot r_1 = -r_1 + u(t)$ and $\tau\dot r_j = -r_j + r_{j-1}$ for $j = 2, \dots, N$, so that $W$ is the shift with ones below the diagonal and every eigenvalue of $J$ equals $-1/\tau$. After an impulse that sets $r_1(0) = 1$,

$$r_j(t) = \frac{(t/\tau)^{j-1}}{(j-1)!}\,e^{-t/\tau},\qquad s_N(t) = \sum_{j=1}^N r_j(t) = e^{-t/\tau}\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\frac{(t/\tau)^i}{i!},\qquad \int_0^\infty s_N(t)\,\dd t = N\tau.$$

The summed activity $s_N(t)$ is the probability that a Poisson variable with mean $t/\tau$ is at most $N - 1$. It decreases strictly from $s_N(0) = 1$ to $0$, satisfies $s_N(t) \ge 1 - t/(N\tau)$, and falls through $1/2$ at a time strictly between $(N - 1)\tau$ and $N\tau$.

Proof. By induction on $j$,

$$r_j(t) = \frac{1}{\tau}\int_0^t e^{-(t - s)/\tau}\,r_{j-1}(s)\,\dd s = \frac{e^{-t/\tau}}{\tau}\int_0^t \frac{(s/\tau)^{j-2}}{(j-2)!}\,\dd s,$$

which is the stated formula. Each $r_j$ integrates to $\tau$, because $\int_0^\infty x^{j-1}e^{-x}\,\dd x = (j-1)!$. In matrix terms, $J = (-I + N_s)/\tau$ with the nilpotent shift $N_s$, and $e^{Jt} = e^{-t/\tau}\sum_i (t/\tau)^iN_s^i/i!$ is the exponential of a Jordan block (chapter 7). Summing the chain equations gives $\tau\dot s_N = -r_N < 0$ for $t > 0$, so $s_N$ decreases strictly, and $s_N(t) \to 0$. Let $K$ be Poisson with mean $t/\tau$. Markov's inequality gives $1 - s_N(t) = \Pr(K \ge N) \le \mathbb E K/N = t/(N\tau)$, with $\mathbb E$ the expectation. Ramanujan's inequality $\sum_{i<n}n^i/i! < e^n/2 < \sum_{i\le n}n^i/i!$ for integers $n \ge 1$ (proved by Szegő, 1928, and Watson, 1929) gives $s_N(N\tau) < \frac12$ (take $n = N$) and, for $N \ge 2$, $s_N\big((N - 1)\tau\big) > \frac12$ (take $n = N - 1$); for $N = 1$, $s_1(0) = 1$.

Two kinds of memory, two linear signatures. The line attractor keeps information in an eigenvalue, $\lambda = 1$, and holds it indefinitely () at the price of fine tuning (). The chain keeps it in non-normal structure, a nilpotent part with every eigenvalue at $-1/\tau$, and holds it for a time proportional to its length, $N\tau$ (). No parameter of the chain sits on a boundary: for every strictly lower-triangular $W$ the matrix $J = (-I + W)/\tau$ is triangular with diagonal $-1/\tau$, so changing the feedforward weights changes the amplitudes but leaves every eigenvalue at $-1/\tau$. Goldman (2009) showed that short-term memory can be maintained by such a purely feedforward mechanism, in which activity passes through a sequence of network states, in architecturally feedforward networks and in recurrent networks that are feedforward in a Schur basis, and that these networks can be tuned to integrate their inputs. Ganguli, Huh and Sompolinsky (2008) defined a Fisher memory curve for linear networks driven by noisy input; its total is exactly 1 for every normal connectivity matrix and at most $N$ for any network of $N$ neurons (), and they showed that a network whose total memory approaches the bound must have a hidden feedforward architecture that amplifies its input superlinearly for a time of order $N$, with input weights matched to that architecture.

Let $W \in \R^{N\times N}$ have spectral radius $\rho(W) < 1$, let $b \in \R^N$ be a unit vector, let $C = \sum_{k\ge0}W^k(W^k)^\T$, and define the Fisher memory curve

$$\mathcal J(k) = b^\T(W^k)^\T C^{-1}W^kb,\qquad k = 0, 1, 2, \dots$$

For the discrete-time network $r_t = Wr_{t-1} + b\,u_t + \zeta_t$, driven by a scalar input $u_t$ and independent Gaussian noise $\zeta_t$ with mean zero and covariance $\nu I$ ($\nu > 0$) and started in the infinite past, $\mathcal J(k)/\nu$ is the Fisher information that $r_t$ carries about the past input $u_{t-k}$, the other inputs being known. Then $\sum_{k\ge0}\mathcal J(k) \le N$, with strict inequality if $N \ge 2$, and $\sum_{k\ge0}\mathcal J(k) = 1$ if $W$ is normal. The bound is approached by an amplifying delay line: for $W = \alpha N_s$, with $N_s$ the shift with ones below the diagonal, and $b = e_1$, the first standard basis vector, the total tends to $N$ as $\alpha \to \infty$.

Proof. The sums converge because $\norm{W^k} \to 0$ geometrically, and $C \succeq I$ is invertible. Fisher information: the state $r_t = \sum_{k\ge0}W^k(b\,u_{t-k} + \zeta_{t-k})$ is Gaussian with covariance $\nu C$ and mean linear in the inputs, the coefficient of $u_{t-k}$ being $W^kb$; for such a Gaussian the Fisher information about $u_{t-k}$ is $(W^kb)^\T(\nu C)^{-1}W^kb = \mathcal J(k)/\nu$. Bound: since $\norm b = 1$, $bb^\T \preceq I$ in the Loewner order (chapter 4), so $C_b = \sum_k W^kbb^\T(W^k)^\T \preceq C$. Then, with $\tr$ the trace, $\sum_k\mathcal J(k) = \tr(C^{-1}C_b) = \tr(C^{-1/2}C_bC^{-1/2})$, the trace of a symmetric matrix between $0$ and $I$, hence at most $N$. If $N \ge 2$, then $C - C_b = \sum_kW^k(I - bb^\T)(W^k)^\T \succeq I - bb^\T \neq 0$, so $N - \sum_k\mathcal J(k) = \tr\big(C^{-1/2}(C - C_b)C^{-1/2}\big) > 0$. Normal case: write $W = U\Lambda U^*$ with $U$ unitary, $U^*$ its conjugate transpose, $\Lambda = \diag(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_N)$, and $\tilde b = U^*b$. Then $C = U\diag\big((1 - \abs{\lambda_i}^2)^{-1}\big)U^*$ and $\mathcal J(k) = \sum_i\abs{\lambda_i}^{2k}(1 - \abs{\lambda_i}^2)\abs{\tilde b_i}^2$, whose sum over $k$ is $\sum_i\abs{\tilde b_i}^2 = \norm b^2 = 1$. Delay line: $W^kb = \alpha^ke_{k+1}$ for $k < N$ and $W^N = 0$, and $C$ is diagonal with $C_{ii} = \sum_{j<i}\alpha^{2j}$, so $\mathcal J(k) = \alpha^{2k}/\sum_{j\le k}\alpha^{2j} \to 1$ for each $k < N$.

Random connectivity, Dale's law and the edge of stability

Real connectivity is too complex to specify. What does a random model predict about the spectrum of $W$, and therefore about the stability of the linearized network?

Let $\xi_{ij}$, $i, j \ge 1$, be independent copies of a real random variable with mean 0 and variance 1, let $g > 0$, and for each $N$ let $W = W_N$ have the entries $W_{ij} = g\,\xi_{ij}/\sqrt N$, $i, j \le N$; so the entries are independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) with mean 0 and variance $g^2/N$. Then, almost surely, the empirical eigenvalue distribution $\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N\delta_{\lambda_i(W_N)}$ ($\delta_z$ the unit point mass at $z$) converges weakly, as $N \to \infty$, to the uniform distribution on the disk $\{z \in \C : \abs z \le g\}$.

Tao and Vu (2010) prove this under a finite variance alone, completing work of Ginibre (Gaussian entries), Girko and Bai; see also Bai and Silverstein, Spectral Analysis of Large Dimensional Random Matrices, 2nd ed., ch. 11. The circular law describes the bulk and allows $o(N)$ eigenvalues outside the disk. That the spectral radius $\rho(W_N)$ also converges to $g$ almost surely is a separate result: the circular law gives $\liminf_N\rho(W_N) \ge g$, and the matching upper bound is proved under moment conditions that Gaussian entries satisfy (Geman, 1986; Bai and Silverstein, ch. 5).

Let $\phi$ be continuously differentiable with $\phi(0) = 0$ and $\phi'(0) = 1$, as for $\phi = \tanh$, and let $W = W_N$ be as in . Then $r = 0$ is a fixed point of $\tau\dot r = -r + W\phi(r)$ with Jacobian $J = (-I + W)/\tau$, and almost surely: (i) the empirical eigenvalue distribution of $J$ converges weakly to the uniform distribution on the disk of radius $g/\tau$ centred at $-1/\tau$; (ii) if $g > 1$, the fraction of eigenvalues of $J$ in the open right half-plane has limit inferior at least the positive share of that disk lying there, so for all large $N$, $\alpha(J) > 0$ and $r = 0$ is unstable; (iii) if $g < 1$ and $\rho(W_N) \to g$ (for example for Gaussian entries), then for all large $N$, $\alpha(J) < 0$ and $r = 0$ is asymptotically stable.

Proof. with $D = \phi'(0)I = I$ gives $J$, whose eigenvalues are $(\lambda_i(W) - 1)/\tau$. This affine map carries the disk of onto the stated disk and preserves weak convergence, which is (i). (ii) For $g > 1$ the open half-plane $\{\Re z > 0\}$ meets the limiting disk in a set of positive area, and weak convergence gives $\liminf_N\mu_N(U) \ge \mu(U)$ for every open set $U$ (the portmanteau theorem), where $\mu_N$ and $\mu$ are the empirical and limiting distributions. Hence $\alpha(J) > 0$ for all large $N$, and instability follows from Lyapunov's indirect method (the source of ). (iii) $\rho(W_N) \to g < 1$ forces $\Re\lambda_i(W_N) < 1$ for every $i$ once $N$ is large, so $\alpha(J) < 0$ and applies.

Finite size and symmetry. The circular law is a statement about $N \to \infty$: the default sample in ($N = 200$, $g = 0.8$) has spectral radius $0.836$ and rightmost eigenvalue $0.799$, so at this size the edge of the spectrum is not sharp at $g$, and a particular network with $g$ just below 1 can be unstable: in a simulation for this guide, about 8 percent of 1000 Gaussian samples with $N = 200$ and $g = 0.95$ had an eigenvalue of $W$ to the right of $\Re\lambda = 1$. Symmetry changes the answer: a symmetric $W$ whose off-diagonal entries have the same variance $g^2/N$ has real eigenvalues filling $[-2g, 2g]$, so $-I + W$ loses stability already at $g = 1/2$. If the $\xi_{ij}$ also have finite fourth moments, the largest eigenvalue $\lambda_{\max}(W_N)$ converges to $2g$ almost surely. The transition to chaos. Sompolinsky, Crisanti and Sommers (1988) studied $\dot x_i = -x_i + \sum_j W_{ij}\tanh(x_j)$ with i.i.d. Gaussian $W_{ij}$ of variance $g^2/N$. The zero state is stable for $g < 1$; at $g = 1$ it loses stability, as predicts, and in the limit $N \to \infty$ a dynamic mean-field theory shows that for $g > 1$ the network is chaotic. Linear theory locates the transition; the saturation of $\tanh$ decides what happens after it, bounded irregular fluctuations instead of exponential growth.

Dale's law. A fraction $f$ of the units is excitatory: their columns have mean $\mu_E$ and the remaining columns have mean $\mu_I < 0$, both in units of $g/\sqrt N$, with fluctuations of variance $g^2/N$ as before. Then $W = \frac{g}{\sqrt N}(\mathbf 1\mu^\T + Z)$, where $\mathbf 1$ is the all-ones vector, $\mu_j \in \{\mu_E, \mu_I\}$ is the mean of column $j$, and $Z$ has i.i.d. standard Gaussian entries. The means are balanced if $f\mu_E + (1 - f)\mu_I = 0$, that is, $\mu^\T\mathbf 1 = 0$.

The mean part $\frac{g}{\sqrt N}\mathbf 1\mu^\T$ has rank one, so its eigenvalues are $0$ and $g\sqrt N\,m$, with eigenvector $\mathbf 1$, where $m = \mu^\T\mathbf 1/N = f\mu_E + (1 - f)\mu_I$ is the mean weight (with $fN$ excitatory columns). If excitation dominates, $m > 0$, and this eigenvalue survives the random part as a real outlier that grows like $\sqrt N$:

Let $W = \frac{g}{\sqrt N}(\mathbf 1\mu^\T + Z)$ as above, with $g > 0$, mean weight $m = \mu^\T\mathbf 1/N > 0$ and $s = \norm{\mu}/\sqrt N = \sqrt{f\mu_E^2 + (1 - f)\mu_I^2}$, and suppose that $\norm{Z} \le c\sqrt N$ for a constant $c > 0$, where $\norm{\cdot}$ is the spectral norm. If $\sqrt N > 4c\,(s + m/2)/m^2$, then $W$ has a real eigenvalue $\lambda > \frac12 g\sqrt N\,m$ with

$$\abs{\lambda - g\sqrt N\,m} \le \frac{g\,s\,c\,\sqrt N}{\sqrt N\,m/2 - c},$$

a bound that tends to $2gsc/m$ as $N \to \infty$. If the entries of $Z$ are i.i.d. with mean 0, variance 1 and finite fourth moment, then $\norm{Z}/\sqrt N \to 2$ almost surely, so for every $g > 0$, almost surely, $-I + W$ is unstable for all large $N$.

Proof. Let $A = gZ/\sqrt N$, so $\norm A \le cg$, and $\theta = g\sqrt N\,m$. For real $\lambda > \norm A$, $\lambda I - A$ is invertible, and the matrix determinant lemma gives $\det(\lambda I - W) = \det(\lambda I - A)\,\big(1 - h(\lambda)\big)$ with $h(\lambda) = \frac{g}{\sqrt N}\mu^\T(\lambda I - A)^{-1}\mathbf 1$, a continuous real function that tends to 0 as $\lambda \to \infty$. The Neumann series $(\lambda I - A)^{-1} = \sum_{k\ge0}A^k\lambda^{-k-1}$ gives $h(\lambda) = \theta/\lambda + R(\lambda)$, where

$$\abs{R(\lambda)} \le \frac{g}{\sqrt N}\norm\mu\,\norm{\mathbf 1}\sum_{k\ge1}\frac{\norm A^k}{\lambda^{k+1}} \le \frac{g^2sc\sqrt N}{\lambda(\lambda - cg)}\qquad(\lambda > cg).$$

The hypothesis on $N$ is equivalent to $\theta/2 > cg$ together with $g^2sc\sqrt N < (\theta/2)(\theta/2 - cg)$, so $\abs{R(\theta/2)} < 1$ and $h(\theta/2) > 2 - 1 = 1$. By the intermediate value theorem $h(\lambda) = 1$ for some $\lambda > \theta/2$, an eigenvalue of $W$. There $\lambda - \theta = \lambda R(\lambda)$, so $\abs{\lambda - \theta} \le g^2sc\sqrt N/(\theta/2 - cg)$, which is the bound. The limit $\norm Z/\sqrt N \to 2$ is the theorem on the largest eigenvalue of the sample covariance matrix $ZZ^\T/N$, which tends to 4 (Bai and Silverstein, ch. 5); with $c = 3$ the hypotheses then hold for all large $N$, and $\lambda > \frac12 g\sqrt N\,m > 1$ eventually.

Balanced means are not enough. If the means are balanced, $m = 0$ and says nothing: the mean part is nilpotent, $(\mathbf 1\mu^\T)^2 = \mathbf 1(\mu^\T\mathbf 1)\mu^\T = 0$, yet its norm, $g\sqrt{f\mu_E^2 + (1 - f)\mu_I^2}\,\sqrt N$, grows with $N$. Rajan and Abbott (2006) found that a few eigenvalues then lie outside the disk, at locations that vary from sample to sample, and that their number does not appear to go to zero as $N$ grows. In the default sample of under Dale: balanced means, with $N = 200$, $g = 0.8$, $f = 0.8$ and $\mu_E = 3$, five eigenvalues lie beyond $1.1g$ and the rightmost is near $2.3$: the linearized network is unstable although the bulk radius is $0.8$.

Let $\mu^\T\mathbf 1 = 0$, let $P = I - \frac1N\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\T$, and let $W = \frac{g}{\sqrt N}\big(\mathbf 1\mu^\T + ZP\big)$, so that every row of $W$ sums to zero. If the columns of $Q \in \R^{N\times(N-1)}$ are an orthonormal basis of $\mathbf 1^\perp$, then

$$\Lambda(W) = \{0\}\ \cup\ \Lambda\Big(\tfrac{g}{\sqrt N}\,Q^\T ZQ\Big).$$

The mean structure does not affect the spectrum. If $Z$ is Gaussian, $Q^\T ZQ$ is again an $(N-1)\times(N-1)$ matrix of i.i.d. standard Gaussian entries, so the spectrum is one zero plus a circular-law sample of radius about $g$.

Proof. Let $e = \mathbf 1/\sqrt N$, so that $U = [\,e\ \ Q\,]$ is orthogonal. Since $\mu^\T e = 0$ and $Pe = 0$, $We = 0$; since $PQ = Q$ and $Q^\T\mathbf 1 = 0$, $Q^\T WQ = \frac{g}{\sqrt N}Q^\T ZQ$. Hence

$$U^\T WU = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & e^\T WQ\\ 0 & \frac{g}{\sqrt N}Q^\T ZQ\end{bmatrix},$$

which is block upper triangular. For Gaussian $Z$, $U^\T ZU$ has i.i.d. standard Gaussian entries (orthogonal invariance), and $Q^\T ZQ$ is one of its blocks.

Rajan and Abbott imposed this constraint to force the outliers back inside the disk, and noted that it makes the spectrum that of the fluctuations alone, as shows: under exact balance the spectrum depends on the variances of the weights, not on their means. They also computed what changes when the excitatory and inhibitory variances differ:

Let $0 < f < 1$ and $\gamma_E, \gamma_I > 0$ be fixed, let $Z$ have i.i.d. standard Gaussian entries, and let $W = \frac{1}{\sqrt N}Z\,\diag(\gamma_1, \dots, \gamma_N)$ with $\gamma_j = \gamma_E$ on a fraction $f$ of the columns (the excitatory ones) and $\gamma_j = \gamma_I$ on the others, so that the entry variances are $\sigma_E^2 = \gamma_E^2/N$ and $\sigma_I^2 = \gamma_I^2/N$. As $N \to \infty$, the empirical eigenvalue distribution of $W$ converges weakly in probability to a rotation-invariant distribution on the disk of radius

$$R = \sqrt{f\gamma_E^2 + (1 - f)\gamma_I^2} = \sqrt{N\big(f\sigma_E^2 + (1 - f)\sigma_I^2\big)},$$

which is uniform only if $\gamma_E = \gamma_I$.

Rajan and Abbott (2006) derived the radius and the density, which has a high-density central region when the variances differ; a rigorous theory of non-Hermitian random matrices with a variance profile, whose results on separable profiles cover this column profile, is Cook, Hachem, Najim and Renfrew (2018).

Optimal feedback control. Todorov and Jordan (2002) proposed that motor coordination is the output of an optimal feedback controller acting on state estimates, the linear quadratic Gaussian setting of chapter 11 extended with signal-dependent noise, and showed that such controllers obey a minimal intervention principle: they correct only deviations that interfere with the task and let variability accumulate in task-irrelevant directions, which matches the structure of motor variability in their experiments.

Two redundant effectors $x = (x_1, x_2)$ with $\dot x = u$ must keep the task variable $x_1 + x_2$ at zero, with cost $\int_0^\infty \big(q\,(x_1 + x_2)^2 + \rho\norm{u}^2\big)\dd t$, where $q, \rho > 0$. The value function is $x^\T Px$ with $P = \gamma\,\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\T$, $\gamma = \sqrt{\rho q/2}$, and the optimal feedback is

$$u = -\tfrac{1}{\rho}Px = -\sqrt{\tfrac{q}{2\rho}}\,(x_1 + x_2)\,\mathbf 1.$$

Indeed $P^2 = 2\gamma^2\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\T = \rho q\,\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\T$, so the Riccati equation $-P^2/\rho + q\,\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\T = 0$ holds. Completing the square as in the regulator theorem (chapter 11) gives cost $x(0)^\T Px(0)$ for this $u$ and at least that for any other input with finite cost. That theorem assumes a stabilizing $P$, which this one is not, but its proof only needs the boundary term $x(T)^\T Px(T) = \gamma\,y(T)^2$, $y = x_1 + x_2$, to vanish as $T \to \infty$; finite cost makes $y$ and $\dot y = u_1 + u_2$ square integrable, so $y(T)^2$, whose derivative $2y\dot y$ is integrable, converges, and its limit is $0$ because $y^2$ is integrable. Both effectors receive the same correction, proportional to the task error, and the difference $x_1 - x_2$ receives none: with additive noise it diffuses freely while the task variable keeps a bounded variance. The problem fails the detectability assumption of the existence theorem for the Riccati equation (chapter 11) exactly in the task-irrelevant direction, which is why that direction is left alone.

Preparation as an initial condition. Churchland et al. (2012) found rotational structure in motor cortex population activity during reaching, consistent with a dynamical system whose state at movement onset is set by preparatory activity; Shenoy, Sahani and Churchland (2013) review this dynamical systems view, in which preparation chooses the initial condition and the intrinsic dynamics generate the movement. Kaufman, Churchland, Ryu and Shenoy (2014) showed that preparatory activity lies largely in output-null directions, the null space of the readout in chapter 1's language. Kao, Sadabadi and Hennequin (2021) formulate preparation as optimal anticipatory control of the upcoming movement and show that the solution requires internal feedback control of the cortical dynamics: unlike a simple feedforward strategy, feedback enables fast preparation by selectively controlling the cortical state in the small subspace that matters for the movement, and feedback, not feedforward control, explains the orthogonality between preparatory and movement activity observed in reaching monkeys. They propose a thalamo-cortical loop gated by the basal ganglia as the circuit that implements this control. In linear terms, reaching a prescribed state with least input energy is the minimum-energy problem of chapter 9, and the unit initial state that the linearized network amplifies most at a given time $t$ is a top right singular vector of $e^{tJ}$, since $\max_{\norm{x_0} = 1}\norm{e^{tJ}x_0} = \norm{e^{tJ}}$ is the largest singular value (norms from singular values, chapter 4).

Controllability of brain networks, and its caveats. Gu et al. (2015) applied controllability measures, among them the Gramian-based average controllability, to linear network models whose state matrix is a diffusion-imaging connectome and whose inputs enter at chosen regions. Pasqualetti, Zampieri and Bullo (2014) had shown that the energy needed to control a network from few driver nodes can grow exponentially with its size (their theorem in chapter 9, for symmetric stable networks), so that rank tests mislead. Tu et al. (2018), applying the same methods to four datasets, found that the minimum set of driver nodes was always larger than one, contrary to single-node controllability, and that the reported relations between controllability measures and weighted degree also hold in randomized networks.

Spike-coding networks. Boerlin, Machens and Denève (2013) derive a network of integrate-and-fire neurons whose linear readout of filtered spike trains tracks the solution of a prescribed linear system $\dot x = Ax + c(t)$: each neuron fires only when its spike reduces the readout error, which produces tight balance of excitation and inhibition, fast recurrent connections that implement the error feedback, and slow recurrent connections that implement $A$.

System level synthesis. A discrete-time plant $x_{t+1} = Ax_t + Bu_t + d_t$, with integer time $t$, state $x_t \in \R^n$, input $u_t \in \R^m$ and disturbance $d_t$ (Anderson et al. write $w$), is controlled by causal linear state feedback. In the $z$-domain ($z$ the shift variable of chapter 8), the closed loop maps the disturbance to the state and to the input, $x = \Phi_xd$ and $u = \Phi_ud$. Write $\frac1z\mathcal{RH}_\infty$ for the strictly proper real-rational transfer matrices with poles in the open unit disk.

The pairs $(\Phi_x, \Phi_u)$ achieved by internally stabilizing state feedback are exactly those in $\frac1z\mathcal{RH}_\infty$ that satisfy

$$\begin{bmatrix} zI - A & -B\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\Phi_x\\ \Phi_u\end{bmatrix} = I,$$

and the controller $K = \Phi_u\Phi_x^{-1}$ achieves them: as a transfer function it gives $x = \Phi_xd$ and $u = \Phi_ud$, and implemented through the realization built directly from $\Phi_x$ and $\Phi_u$ (cited below) it makes the loop internally stable.

Proof of necessity. Internal stability makes $\Phi_x$ and $\Phi_u$ stable. They are strictly proper because, from zero initial state, $x_t$ depends only on $d_s$ with $s < t$, and so does $u_t$, which a causal controller computes from $x_s$ with $s \le t$. With zero initial state the plant reads $zx = Ax + Bu + d$, that is, $(zI - A)x - Bu = d$. Substituting $x = \Phi_xd$ and $u = \Phi_ud$ for every $d$ gives the constraint. Sufficiency, and a realization of $K$ built directly from $\Phi_x$ and $\Phi_u$: Anderson, Doyle, Low and Matni (2019), state-feedback section.

What the constraint means. On the impulse-response coefficients $\Phi_x[t]$ and $\Phi_u[t]$ of the closed loop (the coefficients of $z^{-t}$), the constraint is a recursion: the response to a unit disturbance is itself a trajectory of the plant, driven by the inputs $\Phi_u[t]$ (). The constraint is linear in these coefficients, and so are requirements that the response to a disturbance at one node stay within a neighbourhood, vanish after a finite time, or start only after a communication delay: they are sparsity patterns on $\Phi_x[t]$ and $\Phi_u[t]$. Anderson et al. (2019) contrast this with imposing the same structure directly on $K$, which in general leads to a nonconvex problem.

Write $\Phi_x = \sum_{t\ge1}\Phi_x[t]z^{-t}$ and $\Phi_u = \sum_{t\ge1}\Phi_u[t]z^{-t}$. (i) The constraint of holds if and only if $\Phi_x[1] = I$ and $\Phi_x[t + 1] = A\Phi_x[t] + B\Phi_u[t]$ for all $t \ge 1$; so for a unit disturbance $d_0 = e_j$ (the $j$th standard basis vector) and $d_t = 0$ otherwise, the closed-loop state $x_t = \Phi_x[t]e_j$ is the plant's trajectory under the inputs $u_t = \Phi_u[t]e_j$. (ii) For any subspaces $\mathcal S_x[t]$ and $\mathcal S_u[t]$ of matrices of the right sizes, such as sparsity patterns, the set of achievable pairs with $\Phi_x[t] \in \mathcal S_x[t]$ and $\Phi_u[t] \in \mathcal S_u[t]$ for all $t$ is convex, so minimizing a convex cost over it is a convex program.

Proof. (i) Multiplying out,

$$(zI - A)\Phi_x - B\Phi_u = \Phi_x[1] + \sum_{t\ge1}\big(\Phi_x[t + 1] - A\Phi_x[t] - B\Phi_u[t]\big)z^{-t},$$

which equals $I$ exactly when the stated equations hold. (ii) By , the achievable pairs are the solutions of a linear equation in the vector space $\frac1z\mathcal{RH}_\infty \times \frac1z\mathcal{RH}_\infty$, an affine set; the subspace conditions are linear, and an intersection of convex sets is convex.

For the scalar plant $x_{t+1} = ax_t + u_t + d_t$, take $\Phi_x = z^{-1}$ and $\Phi_u = -az^{-1}$. Then $(z - a)z^{-1} + az^{-1} = 1$, so the pair is achievable, and $K = \Phi_u\Phi_x^{-1} = -a$: with $u_t = -ax_t$ the state is $x_{t+1} = d_t$, and each disturbance affects the state for exactly one step.

Internal feedback. Li, Sarma, Sejnowski and Doyle (2023), building on this line of distributed control theory, argue that the cortex's internal feedback, from motor toward sensory areas, compensates for internal delays: it filters out self-generated and other predictable changes in early sensory areas, so that unpredicted, actionable information reaches the fast motor pathways. Their models build in a speed–accuracy trade-off for neural channels (tracts of fast, large neurons convey less accurate signals than tracts of many small ones), and internal feedback lets the fastest components carry only the compressed, unpredicted part of the input; they argue that internal feedback is needed for state estimation, localization of function and attention, and relate the model to observations such as motor signals in visual cortex.

Linear methods beyond linear systems: Koopman operators and DMD

For nonlinear dynamics, or for data without a model, can linear operators still be used, and what do they recover?

For a map $x_{t+1} = F(x_t)$ on a state space $\mathcal X$, the Koopman operator acts on observables $\psi: \mathcal X \to \C$ by $(\mathcal K\psi)(x) = \psi(F(x))$; it acts on a space of functions, usually infinite-dimensional. A Koopman eigenfunction is an observable $\psi \neq 0$ with $\mathcal K\psi = \lambda\psi$ for a scalar $\lambda$. For a flow $\dot x = f(x)$ with time-$s$ map $\varphi_s$ ($x(s) = \varphi_s(x(0))$), the Koopman operators are $\mathcal K^s\psi = \psi\circ\varphi_s$ for $s \ge 0$.

(i) $\mathcal K(\alpha\psi_1 + \beta\psi_2) = \alpha\,\mathcal K\psi_1 + \beta\,\mathcal K\psi_2$ for all observables $\psi_1, \psi_2$ and scalars $\alpha, \beta$, even when $F$ is nonlinear. (ii) If $\mathcal K\psi = \lambda\psi$, then $\psi(x_t) = \lambda^t\psi(x_0)$ along every trajectory. (iii) Let $f$ be continuously differentiable with solutions defined for all $s \ge 0$. Then $\mathcal K^{s + s'} = \mathcal K^s\mathcal K^{s'}$ for $s, s' \ge 0$, and for every continuously differentiable $\psi$, $\frac{\dd}{\dd s}(\mathcal K^s\psi)(x)\big|_{s=0} = \nabla\psi(x)\cdot f(x)$: the generator of the semigroup acts as $\psi \mapsto \nabla\psi\cdot f$. If $\nabla\psi\cdot f = \lambda\psi$, then $\psi(x(s)) = e^{\lambda s}\psi(x(0))$ along every trajectory.

Proof. (i) Both sides take the value $\alpha\psi_1(F(x)) + \beta\psi_2(F(x))$ at $x$. (ii) $\psi(x_{t+1}) = (\mathcal K\psi)(x_t) = \lambda\psi(x_t)$; induct on $t$. (iii) $\mathcal K^s(\mathcal K^{s'}\psi) = \psi\circ\varphi_{s'}\circ\varphi_s = \psi\circ\varphi_{s + s'}$ by the flow property, and the chain rule gives $\frac{\dd}{\dd s}\psi(\varphi_s(x)) = \nabla\psi(\varphi_s(x))\cdot f(\varphi_s(x))$, which is the generator formula at $s = 0$. If $\nabla\psi\cdot f = \lambda\psi$, the same identity reads $\frac{\dd}{\dd s}\psi(x(s)) = \lambda\psi(x(s))$.

For $\dot x_1 = a_1x_1$ and $\dot x_2 = a_2(x_2 - x_1^2)$ with real parameters $a_1, a_2$, the observables $(x_1, x_2, x_1^2)$ satisfy

$$\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\begin{bmatrix} x_1\\ x_2\\ x_1^2\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} a_1 & 0 & 0\\ 0 & a_2 & -a_2\\ 0 & 0 & 2a_1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} x_1\\ x_2\\ x_1^2\end{bmatrix},$$

because $\frac{\dd}{\dd t}x_1^2 = 2x_1\dot x_1 = 2a_1x_1^2$. A nonlinear flow becomes an exact three-dimensional linear system, with generator eigenvalues $a_1$, $a_2$ and $2a_1$: a left eigenvector $w$ of the matrix gives the eigenfunction $w^\T(x_1, x_2, x_1^2)^\T$ of . This example is standard in the Koopman literature (Brunton, Budišić, Kaiser and Kutz, 2022). Not every system closes up like this: for a bistable system no finite set of continuous observables containing the state does (, a precise form of an observation of Brunton, Brunton, Proctor and Kutz, 2016), and every finite linear model of it is an approximation.

Let $f$ be continuously differentiable on a connected open set $\mathcal X \subseteq \R^n$, with the solutions of $\dot x = f(x)$ defined for all $t \ge 0$. Suppose that continuous observables $\Psi = (\psi_1, \dots, \psi_k)^\T: \mathcal X \to \R^k$ satisfy $\Psi(x(t)) = e^{tL}\Psi(x(0))$ along every trajectory, for a matrix $L \in \R^{k\times k}$, and contain the state linearly: $x = C\Psi(x)$ for a matrix $C \in \R^{n\times k}$. Then every asymptotically stable equilibrium attracts every initial state in $\mathcal X$. In particular, a system with two asymptotically stable equilibria has no such finite linear representation.

Proof. Let $x^*$ be asymptotically stable, with basin of attraction $B$, which is open by continuous dependence on initial conditions. The set $\mathcal L$ of $y \in \R^k$ for which $e^{tL}y$ converges as $t \to \infty$ is a linear subspace, hence closed, and $Py = \lim_{t\to\infty}e^{tL}y$ is linear on it, hence continuous. For $x_0 \in B$, $e^{tL}\Psi(x_0) = \Psi(x(t)) \to \Psi(x^*)$, so $\Psi(B) \subseteq \mathcal L$ and $P\Psi = \Psi(x^*)$ on $B$. If $\bar x \in \mathcal X$ is a limit of points of $B$, continuity gives $\Psi(\bar x) \in \mathcal L$ and $P\Psi(\bar x) = \Psi(x^*)$, so the trajectory from $\bar x$ satisfies $x(t) = Ce^{tL}\Psi(\bar x) \to C\Psi(x^*) = x^*$, that is, $\bar x \in B$. Thus $B$ is open and closed in the connected set $\mathcal X$, and nonempty, so $B = \mathcal X$.

Given snapshot pairs $(x_t, x_{t+1})$ for $t = 0, \dots, m - 1$, collected in $X = [\,x_0\ \cdots\ x_{m-1}\,]$ and $X' = [\,x_1\ \cdots\ x_m\,]$, DMD takes the least-squares operator

$$\hat A = X'X^+ = \operatorname*{arg\,min}_{A}\ \norm{X' - AX}_F,$$

the minimizer of smallest Frobenius norm $\norm{\cdot}_F$, with $X^+$ the Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse, by the matrix least-squares corollary (chapter 2). Its eigenvalues and eigenvectors are the DMD eigenvalues and modes. For high-dimensional data one computes them from a truncated singular value decomposition (SVD) of $X$ (chapter 4) through a small matrix (; Schmid, 2010; Tu et al., 2014).

Let $1 \le p \le \rank X$, let $U_p\Sigma_pV_p^\T$ be the truncated SVD of $X$ that keeps its $p$ largest singular values, and set $\hat A_p = X'V_p\Sigma_p^{-1}U_p^\T$ and $\tilde A_p = U_p^\T X'V_p\Sigma_p^{-1} \in \R^{p\times p}$. Then $\hat A_p$ and $\tilde A_p$ have the same nonzero eigenvalues, with the same algebraic multiplicities. If $\tilde A_py = \lambda y$ with $\lambda \neq 0$ and $y \neq 0$, then $\phi = X'V_p\Sigma_p^{-1}y$ is nonzero and $\hat A_p\phi = \lambda\phi$. For $p = \rank X$, $\hat A_p = X'X^+ = \hat A$.

Proof. With $E = X'V_p\Sigma_p^{-1}$ and $H = U_p^\T$, $\hat A_p = EH$ and $\tilde A_p = HE$, so the $AB$ and $BA$ theorem (chapter 3) gives the eigenvalues. Then $\hat A_p\phi = EHEy = E\tilde A_py = \lambda\phi$, and $H\phi = \tilde A_py = \lambda y \neq 0$, so $\phi \neq 0$. For $p = \rank X$, $V_p\Sigma_p^{-1}U_p^\T = X^+$ by the pseudoinverse from the SVD (chapter 4).

If the data come from a linear system, $X' = AX$, then $\hat A = A\,P_{\Range(X)}$, where $P_{\Range(X)} = XX^+$ is the orthogonal projector onto the span of the snapshots. Hence $\hat A = A$ whenever the snapshots span the state space; otherwise DMD reproduces $A$ on the explored subspace and sets it to zero on the orthogonal complement.

Proof. $X'X^+ = AXX^+$, and $XX^+$ is the orthogonal projector onto $\Range(X)$ by the pseudoinverse theorem (chapter 2).

Simple and extreme cases. With a single snapshot pair ($m = 1$), $X$ has rank at most one and so has $\hat A$, whatever the true $A$. In the plane, two pairs suffice when $x_0$ and $x_1$ are independent, and noise-free data then give $\hat A = A$ exactly, by . A trajectory that starts on an eigenvector never leaves its span, so only that eigenvalue can be recovered (see the last self-check question).

Let $x_{t+1} = Ax_t$ with $A \in \R^{n\times n}$, and let the snapshots be observed with noise, $y_t = x_t + \eta_t$ for $t = 0, \dots, m$, where the $\eta_t$ are independent with mean zero and covariance $\sigma^2 I$, $\sigma > 0$. Let $S = \sum_{t<m}x_tx_t^\T$ and let $\mathbb E$ denote the expectation. When $\sum_{t<m}y_ty_t^\T$ is invertible, the DMD estimate from the noisy snapshots is

$$\hat A = \Big(\sum_{t<m} y_{t+1}y_t^\T\Big)\Big(\sum_{t<m} y_ty_t^\T\Big)^{-1},\qquad \mathbb E\sum_{t<m} y_{t+1}y_t^\T = AS,\qquad \mathbb E\sum_{t<m} y_ty_t^\T = S + m\sigma^2 I.$$

Replacing both sums by their expectations gives the first-order prediction $\bar A = A\,S\,\big(S + m\sigma^2 I\big)^{-1}$, and $\norm{\bar A} \le \frac{s_1}{s_1 + m\sigma^2}\norm{A}$, with $s_1$ the largest eigenvalue of $S$. So if $A$ is normal and nonzero, every eigenvalue of $\bar A$ has modulus at most $\frac{s_1}{s_1 + m\sigma^2}\,\rho(A) < \rho(A)$: DMD reports too much damping. If $S = sI$, as in the scalar case, $\bar A = \frac{s}{s + m\sigma^2}A$, and every eigenvalue is shrunk toward zero by the same factor. For a decaying scalar transient ($\abs a < 1$, $x_0 \neq 0$), the factor $\frac{S}{S + m\sigma^2}$ decreases strictly as $m$ grows and tends to $0$: more snapshots make the bias worse.

Proof. With $Y = [\,y_0\ \cdots\ y_{m-1}\,]$ and $Y' = [\,y_1\ \cdots\ y_m\,]$, $\hat A = Y'Y^\T(YY^\T)^{-1} = Y'Y^+$ by the full-row-rank formula of the pseudoinverse theorem (chapter 2). The noise is independent across time and of the signal, so the cross terms have mean zero, $\mathbb E\,\eta_{t+1}\eta_t^\T = 0$ and $\mathbb E\,\eta_t\eta_t^\T = \sigma^2I$; with $\sum_t x_{t+1}x_t^\T = A\sum_t x_tx_t^\T = AS$ this gives the two expectations. The matrix $M = S(S + m\sigma^2I)^{-1}$ is symmetric, because $S \succeq 0$ commutes with $(S + m\sigma^2I)^{-1}$, with eigenvalues $s_i/(s_i + m\sigma^2) \in [0, 1)$ for the eigenvalues $s_i$ of $S$, so $\norm{\bar A} \le \norm{A}\,\norm{M} = \norm{A}\,s_1/(s_1 + m\sigma^2)$. For normal $A$, $\norm{A} = \rho(A)$ by unitary diagonalization (chapter 4), and every eigenvalue of $\bar A$ has modulus at most $\norm{\bar A}$ (the spectral radius is below every induced norm, chapter 5). In the scalar case $S = S_m = \sum_{t<m}c_t$ with $c_t = x_0^2a^{2t}$, a nonincreasing sequence with $c_t < c_0$ for $t \ge 1$. Hence $c_m < S_m/m$, so $\frac{S_{m+1}}{m + 1} = \frac{S_m + c_m}{m + 1} < \frac{S_m}{m}$: the ratio $m\sigma^2/S_m$ increases strictly, and without bound because $S_m \le x_0^2/(1 - a^2)$. The prediction replaces the expectation of a ratio by the ratio of expectations, which is accurate when the sums concentrate; the visual checks it against repeated noisy samples.

This is the errors-in-variables bias of least squares. Dawson, Hemati, Williams and Rowley (2016) characterize it for DMD, including its dependence on the number of snapshots and the noise level, and propose corrections; total DMD, a total-least-squares formulation (Hemati, Rowley, Deem and Cattafesta, 2017), is designed to remove it.

What DMD can and cannot recover. It recovers a linear system exactly from noise-free data that span the state space (). It cannot see directions that the data never explore, it reports biased eigenvalues from noisy snapshots (), and for a nonlinear system it fits a linear operator on the chosen observables, which represents the Koopman operator on their span only when that span is invariant, as in ; then it is recovered exactly (), and for a bistable system no such span of continuous observables contains the state ().

Let $x_{t+1} = F(x_t)$, and let real observables $\Psi = (\psi_1, \dots, \psi_k)^\T$ satisfy $\Psi(F(x)) = K\Psi(x)$ for every $x$, for a matrix $K \in \R^{k\times k}$, so that their span is invariant under $\mathcal K$. DMD applied to the lifted snapshots, $X = [\,\Psi(x_0)\ \cdots\ \Psi(x_{m-1})\,]$ and $X' = [\,\Psi(x_1)\ \cdots\ \Psi(x_m)\,]$, returns $\hat K = X'X^+ = K\,P_{\Range(X)}$; if the columns of $X$ span $\R^k$, then $\hat K = K$, and every left eigenvector $w$ of $K$, $w^\T K = \lambda w^\T$, gives the Koopman eigenfunction $w^\T\Psi$ with eigenvalue $\lambda$.

Proof. The lifted snapshot matrices satisfy $X' = KX$, so gives $\hat K$. If the columns of $X$ span $\R^k$, no nonzero $c \in \C^k$ has $c^\T\Psi(x_t) = 0$ for all $t < m$, so $w^\T\Psi$ is not the zero function; and $\mathcal K(w^\T\Psi) = w^\T\Psi\circ F = w^\T K\Psi = \lambda\,w^\T\Psi$.

Self-check

  1. In the two-population model take $w = 4$ and $k = 1$. What are the eigenvalues of $\tau J$, the peak of the sum amplitude after a unit difference kick, and the steady-state sum response to a sustained unit difference input?

    Answer

    $W$ is nilpotent, so both eigenvalues of $\tau J$ are $-1$. The peak of $a_+$ is $2w/e = 8/e \approx 2.94$ at $t = \tau$, and the steady state is $a_+ = w(1 + k)/(1 + w(k - 1)) = 2w = 8$.

  2. A line-attractor model with $\tau = 20$ ms must hold a value with an effective time constant of at least 20 s. How close to 1 must the eigenvalue be?

    Answer

    $\tau/\abs{1 - \lambda} \ge 20$ s requires $\abs{1 - \lambda} \le 0.001$.

  3. A feedforward chain has $N = 10$ units with $\tau = 50$ ms. What do its eigenvalues say about memory, and what does the chain actually do?

    Answer

    Every eigenvalue of $J$ is $-1/\tau = -20$ s$^{-1}$, which suggests a memory of 50 ms. The summed activity has area $N\tau = 0.5$ s: it stays near its initial value for a few hundred milliseconds and falls through half of it between $(N - 1)\tau = 0.45$ s and $N\tau = 0.5$ s ().

  4. A large random network has i.i.d. Gaussian weights with $g = 0.9$. Is $-I + W$ stable? What changes with Dale's law and balanced means, and what with balance in every row?

    Answer

    Without Dale's law the spectrum fills the disk of radius about $0.9$, so $-I + W$ is stable with high probability (). With balanced means only, outliers can leave the disk and cross $\Re\lambda = 1$, so stability is not guaranteed (). With every row balanced the mean drops out of the spectrum () and stability returns.

  5. DMD is applied to noise-free snapshots of one trajectory of $x_{t+1} = Ax_t$ that starts on an eigenvector $v$ of $A$ with eigenvalue $\lambda$. What is $\hat A$?

    Answer

    All snapshots are multiples of $v$, so $\Range(X) = \spn(v)$ and, by , $\hat A = \lambda\,vv^\T/(v^\T v)$: the eigenvalue $\lambda$ is recovered, and every other eigenvalue is reported as $0$.
