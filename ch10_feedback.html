Feedback: state feedback, observers and the loop

Feedback replaces the open-loop matrix $A$ by a closed-loop matrix of the designer's choosing, within limits set by controllability and observability. This chapter makes those limits exact, prices the redesign in input size, transients and noise, and then turns to the loop view, where a handful of closed-loop transfer functions record what every feedback law gains and gives up.

State feedback and pole placement

Given $\dot x = Ax + Bu$ with the whole state measured, choose a linear law $u = -Kx$ so that the closed-loop system $\dot x = (A - BK)x$ has prescribed eigenvalues. Which spectra can be reached, and which cannot?

$A \in \R^{n\times n}$ and $B \in \R^{n\times m}$ are real, the state $x(t) \in \R^n$ is available, and the gain $K \in \R^{m\times n}$ must be real. A real closed-loop matrix has a characteristic polynomial with real coefficients, so its spectrum is closed under complex conjugation, with multiplicities (the characteristic-polynomial theorem, chapter 3). The target is therefore a conjugation-closed list $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n$, equivalently a monic polynomial $\chi(s) = \prod_i (s - \lambda_i) = s^n + \alpha_1 s^{n-1} + \dots + \alpha_n$ with real coefficients. Write $\det(sI - A) = s^n + a_1 s^{n-1} + \dots + a_n$ and $\mathcal C = [B,\ AB,\ \dots,\ A^{n-1}B]$ for the controllability matrix of chapter 9. With an external input $r$, the law $u = -Kx + r$ gives $\dot x = (A - BK)x + Br$.

Three small cases. A scalar plant $\dot x = ax + bu$ with $u = -kx$ has the closed-loop pole $a - bk$: any real number $\lambda$ if $b \neq 0$, by $k = (a - \lambda)/b$, and $a$ for every $k$ if $b = 0$. The double integrator $\ddot q = u$ with state $x = (q, \dot q)$ has $A = \begin{bmatrix}0 & 1\\ 0 & 0\end{bmatrix}$, $B = \begin{bmatrix}0\\ 1\end{bmatrix}$, and $K = [k_1\ \ k_2]$ gives $A - BK = \begin{bmatrix}0 & 1\\ -k_1 & -k_2\end{bmatrix}$ with characteristic polynomial $s^2 + k_2 s + k_1$: every monic real quadratic, each from exactly one gain. The diagonal plant $A = \diag(1, -2)$ with $B = e_1 = (1, 0)^\T$ gives $A - BK = \begin{bmatrix}1 - k_1 & -k_2\\ 0 & -2\end{bmatrix}$: the eigenvalue $-2$ survives every gain, because no input reaches the second coordinate.

Every conjugation-closed spectrum can be produced by some $K$ exactly when $(A, B)$ is controllable, and an uncontrollable eigenvalue stays where it is under every feedback. and confirm both halves.

For a single input the proof goes through a coordinate system in which the feedback acts on one row only and every closed-loop matrix is a companion matrix, whose structure is explicit.

Let $\psi(s) = s^n + \psi_1 s^{n-1} + \dots + \psi_n$ have real coefficients, and let its companion matrix $M_\psi \in \R^{n\times n}$ have first row $(-\psi_1, \dots, -\psi_n)$, ones on the subdiagonal and zeros elsewhere. Put $\theta(s) = (s^{n-1}, \dots, s, 1)^\T$, and let $e_1$ be the first standard basis vector. Then:

  1. $(sI - M_\psi)\,\theta(s) = \psi(s)\,e_1$ for every $s \in \C$, so $(sI - M_\psi)^{-1}e_1 = \theta(s)/\psi(s)$ whenever $\psi(s) \neq 0$;
  2. $\det(sI - M_\psi) = \psi(s)$;
  3. $\rank(\lambda I - M_\psi) \ge n - 1$ for every $\lambda \in \C$; hence every eigenvalue $\lambda$ of $M_\psi$ has geometric multiplicity one (a one-dimensional eigenspace), spanned by $\theta(\lambda)$.

Proof. (1) Row 1 of $(sI - M_\psi)\,\theta(s)$ is $(s + \psi_1)s^{n-1} + \psi_2 s^{n-2} + \dots + \psi_n = \psi(s)$, and row $l \ge 2$ is $-s^{n-l+1} + s\cdot s^{n-l} = 0$. (2) Deleting the first row and the last column of $sI - M_\psi$ leaves an upper triangular matrix with every diagonal entry equal to $-1$. Adding $s^{n-j}$ times column $j$ to column $n$, for $j = 1, \dots, n - 1$, does not change the determinant and, by (1), turns column $n$ into $\psi(s)\,e_1$; expanding along that column gives $\det(sI - M_\psi) = (-1)^{n+1}\psi(s)\,(-1)^{n-1} = \psi(s)$. (3) The same triangular submatrix, at $s = \lambda$, is invertible, so $\rank(\lambda I - M_\psi) \ge n - 1$. If $\psi(\lambda) = 0$, then (1) puts $\theta(\lambda)$, which is nonzero because its last entry is 1, in the null space of $\lambda I - M_\psi$, and that null space has dimension at most one.

Let $w \in \C^n$ be nonzero with $w^* A = \lambda w^*$ and $w^* B = 0$, where $w^*$ is the conjugate transpose: $w$ is a left eigenvector of $A$ orthogonal to every column of $B$. Then $w^*(A - BK) = \lambda w^*$ for every $K$, so $\lambda \in \Lambda(A - BK)$, the spectrum of $A - BK$; and every solution of $\dot x = Ax + Bu$ satisfies $w^*x(t) = e^{\lambda t}\,w^*x(0)$, whatever the input. More completely, in coordinates where

$$A = \begin{bmatrix} A_{11} & A_{12}\\ 0 & A_{22}\end{bmatrix},\quad B = \begin{bmatrix} B_1\\ 0\end{bmatrix},\quad K = [K_1\ \ K_2] \quad\Longrightarrow\quad A - BK = \begin{bmatrix} A_{11} - B_1K_1 & A_{12} - B_1 K_2\\ 0 & A_{22}\end{bmatrix},$$

with $(A_{11}, B_1)$ controllable (the decomposition of chapter 9, reachable subspace first), the eigenvalues of $A_{22}$ are fixed and those of $A_{11} - B_1 K_1$ can be assigned freely.

Proof. $w^*(A - BK) = w^*A - (w^*B)K = \lambda w^*$, so $w^*\bigl(\lambda I - (A - BK)\bigr) = 0$ with $w \neq 0$ and $\lambda I - (A - BK)$ is singular. Along solutions, $\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\,w^*x = w^*Ax + w^*Bu = \lambda\,w^*x$. The block formula is direct multiplication. For square blocks $P$ and $R$, $\begin{bmatrix}P & Q\\ 0 & R\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}I & 0\\ 0 & R\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}P & Q\\ 0 & I\end{bmatrix}$, and expanding the two factors along their identity rows gives the determinants $\det R$ and $\det P$; by multiplicativity of the determinant (chapter 1), applied to $sI - (A - BK)$, the characteristic polynomial of $A - BK$ is $\det(sI - A_{11} + B_1K_1)\,\det(sI - A_{22})$. State feedback acts on pairs: $(A, B) \mapsto (A - BK, B)$, together with coordinate changes $(TAT^{-1}, TB)$ and input changes $B \mapsto BV$ with invertible $V$. Its invariants say what feedback cannot do. Feedback leaves the controllable subspace and each partial range $\Range[B,\ AB,\ \dots,\ A^{k-1}B]$ unchanged (); the ranks of these partial ranges survive all three operations, and for controllable pairs they are complete invariants (). With one input and one output a second invariant appears: the zeros ().

Let $A \in \R^{n\times n}$, $B \in \R^{n\times m}$ and $K \in \R^{m\times n}$, and write $\mathcal C_k(A, B) = [B,\ AB,\ \dots,\ A^{k-1}B]$ for $k \ge 1$, so that $\mathcal C = \mathcal C_n$. Then $\Range\,\mathcal C_k(A - BK, B) = \Range\,\mathcal C_k(A, B)$ for every $k$. In particular state feedback changes neither the reachable subspace $\Range\,\mathcal C$ nor controllability, and the ranks $\rank\mathcal C_k$, $k = 1, \dots, n$, are unchanged by state feedback, by coordinate changes $(TAT^{-1}, TB)$ and by input changes $B \mapsto BV$ with $V$ invertible.

Let $(A, b)$ be controllable with $b \in \R^n$, and let $c \in \R^{1\times n}$. There is a polynomial $\beta$ of degree at most $n - 1$ such that for every $K$

$$c(sI - A)^{-1}b = \frac{\beta(s)}{\det(sI - A)}, \qquad c(sI - A + bK)^{-1}b = \frac{\beta(s)}{\det(sI - A + bK)}.$$

Proof. In the coordinates of , write $cT^{-1} = [\beta_1\ \cdots\ \beta_n]$ and $\beta(s) = \beta_1 s^{n-1} + \dots + \beta_n = cT^{-1}\theta(s)$. Placing a closed-loop pole on a root of $\beta$ therefore cancels it from the closed-loop transfer function and hides its mode from the output: feedback preserves controllability but can destroy observability.

What fast poles cost

promises any spectrum and says nothing about the size of $K$, of the input $u$, or of the state on its way to zero. Find how these scale with the speed of the requested poles.

The scalar plant $\dot x = ax + bu$, $b \neq 0$, with $u = -kx$ and closed-loop pole $\lambda = a - bk < 0$ has $x(t) = e^{\lambda t}x_0$ and $u(t) = -k e^{\lambda t}x_0$, so

$$\abs{u(0)} = \frac{\abs{a - \lambda}}{\abs b}\abs{x_0}, \qquad \int_0^\infty u(t)^2\,\dd t = \frac{(a - \lambda)^2}{2 b^2 \abs\lambda}\, x_0^2 .$$

Both grow linearly in $\abs\lambda$ as $\lambda \to -\infty$. For an unstable plant ($a > 0$) the energy, as a function of $\mu = -\lambda > 0$, is $(a+\mu)^2 x_0^2/(2b^2\mu)$, minimized at $\mu = a$ with value $2a x_0^2/b^2$: among static gains, stabilization has a minimum price, and the cheapest pole is the mirror image $-a$ of the unstable one. Chapter 11 recovers this as the limit of expensive optimal control (its proposition on cheap and expensive control).

Scaling. For the double integrator, requesting the poles $c\lambda_1, c\lambda_2$ instead of $\lambda_1, \lambda_2$ (with $c > 0$) gives $\chi(s) = s^2 - c(\lambda_1 + \lambda_2)s + c^2\lambda_1\lambda_2$, hence $k_1 = c^2\lambda_1\lambda_2$ and $k_2 = -c(\lambda_1 + \lambda_2)$. The closed loop is the old one with time sped up by $c$: from an initial state at rest, $q(t)$ becomes $q(ct)$, the velocity gains a factor $c$, the input a factor $c^2$. For the chain of $n$ integrators, $q^{(n)} = u$ with state $(q, \dot q, \dots, q^{(n-1)})$, the gain on the first state is the constant coefficient of the closed-loop characteristic polynomial; scaling the poles by $c$ multiplies the coefficient $\alpha_j$ by $c^j$, so this gain grows like $c^n$. The rate is the same for every controllable single-input plant ().

Then

$$K(c) = \sum_{j=0}^{n} c^j\alpha_j\; e_n^\T\,\mathcal C^{-1}A^{n-j},$$

a polynomial in $c$ of degree $n$ with leading coefficient $\alpha_n e_n^\T\mathcal C^{-1} \neq 0$. Hence $\norm{K(c)}/c^n \to \abs{\alpha_n}\,\norm{e_n^\T\mathcal C^{-1}} > 0$ as $c \to \infty$, with $\norm{\cdot}$ the Euclidean norm, and the initial input $u(0) = -K(c)x_0$ grows like $c^n$ for every initial state $x_0$ with $e_n^\T\mathcal C^{-1}x_0 \neq 0$.

Double integrator, both poles at $-c$, initial state $x(0) = (1, 0)$. Then $\chi(s) = (s + c)^2$, $K = [c^2\ \ 2c]$, and

$$q(t) = (1 + ct)e^{-ct}, \qquad \dot q(t) = -c^2 t\, e^{-ct}, \qquad u(t) = c^2 (ct - 1)e^{-ct}.$$

The position converges faster as $c$ grows, but $\max_t\abs{\dot q(t)} = c/e$ (at $t = 1/c$, with $e$ Euler's number) and $\max_t \abs{u(t)} = \abs{u(0)} = c^2$. The velocity peaks in proportion to $c$ and the input in proportion to $c^2$: the peaking phenomenon. An actuator with a bounded range therefore saturates once $c$ is large enough, and for nonlinear systems H. J. Sussmann and P. V. Kokotović (IEEE Trans. Autom. Control 36 (1991) 424–440) analysed the consequences of peaking for global stabilization by high-gain feedback.

"The eigenvalues are the design" can fail as soon as there are two inputs. Take $A = 0 \in \R^{2\times 2}$ and $B = I_2$, for which $A - BK = -K$ can be any matrix. For each $w \in \R$ the gain $K_w = \begin{bmatrix}1 & -w\\ 0 & 2\end{bmatrix}$ gives

$$A - BK_w = \begin{bmatrix}-1 & w\\ 0 & -2\end{bmatrix}, \qquad e^{(A - BK_w)t} = \begin{bmatrix} e^{-t} & w(e^{-t} - e^{-2t})\\ 0 & e^{-2t}\end{bmatrix}.$$

The spectrum is $\{-1, -2\}$ for every $w$, yet at $t = \ln 2$ the off-diagonal entry is $w/4$, so in the spectral norm $\norm{e^{(A - BK_w)t}} \ge \abs w/4$, and $\norm{K_w} \ge \abs w$. Same eigenvalues, unbounded transient amplification and gain: $K_w$ keeps the eigenvector $e_1$ for $-1$ and turns the eigenvector for $-2$, a multiple of $(w, -1)^\T$, toward it as $\abs w$ grows. The spare freedom in a multi-input gain is the freedom to choose eigenvectors (), and chapter 6 explains with this very matrix (its transient-growth example) why non-normal choices amplify.

Observers

Only $y = Cx$ with $C \in \R^{p\times n}$ is measured. Reconstruct $x(t)$ online from the records of $u$ and $y$, with an error that decays at a rate of our choosing.

Two naive estimators fail in opposite ways. Simulating the model, $\dot{\hat x} = A\hat x + Bu$, gives the error equation $\dot{\tilde x} = A\tilde x$ for $\tilde x = x - \hat x$: nothing is ever corrected, so the error decays from every initial error only if $A$ is Hurwitz (chapter 7), and then only at the plant's own rate; for an oscillator or an unstable plant it is useless. Differentiating the output is exact in principle, since for $u = 0$ the stacked derivatives $(y, \dot y, \dots, y^{(n-1)})$ equal $\mathcal O x$, with $\mathcal O$ the observability matrix, which has rank $n$ when $(C, A)$ is observable (chapter 9), so that they determine $x$; but it amplifies measurement noise without bound, because differentiation multiplies a sinusoid of frequency $\omega$ by $\omega$. An observer simulates and corrects.

For the plant $\dot x = Ax + Bu$, $y = Cx$, the observer with gain $L \in \R^{n\times p}$ is the system

$$\dot{\hat x} = A\hat x + Bu + L\,(y - C\hat x).$$

It runs a copy of the model and corrects it in proportion to the output prediction error $y - C\hat x$.

The last row needs a noise model. With process noise $w$ and measurement noise $v$, $\dot x = Ax + Bu + w$ and $y = Cx + v$, the error obeys $\dot{\tilde x} = (A - LC)\tilde x + w - Lv$. Faster observer poles make the homogeneous part decay faster, but with one output the gain that places them grows like the $n$-th power of the speed factor ( applied to $(A^\T, C^\T)$), and the term $-Lv$ injects the measurement noise with that gain. How much noise reaches the error follows from the second moments of a stable linear system driven by white noise.

Let $F \in \R^{n\times n}$ be Hurwitz, and let $\xi$ be white noise, independent of the initial state $z(0)$, with zero mean and intensity $Q \succeq 0$ (positive semidefinite), that is, $\mathbb E[\xi(t)\xi(t')^\T] = Q\,\delta(t - t')$, where $\mathbb E$ is expectation and $\delta$ the Dirac delta. The solution of $\dot z = Fz + \xi$ has the second-moment matrix

$$\Sigma(t) = \mathbb E\bigl[z(t)z(t)^\T\bigr] = e^{Ft}\,\Sigma(0)\,e^{F^\T t} + \int_0^t e^{F\tau}Qe^{F^\T\tau}\,\dd\tau ,$$

which converges as $t \to \infty$ to the unique solution $\Sigma_\infty$ of $F\Sigma + \Sigma F^\T + Q = 0$. In the scalar case $F = -\gamma$ with $\gamma > 0$ and $Q = q$, $\Sigma_\infty = q/(2\gamma)$.

Proof. By variation of constants (chapter 8), $z(t) = e^{Ft}z(0) + \int_0^t e^{F(t - \tau)}\xi(\tau)\,\dd\tau$. The cross terms have zero mean, by independence and $\mathbb E\,\xi = 0$, and the rule $\mathbb E[\xi(\tau)\xi(\tau')^\T] = Q\,\delta(\tau - \tau')$ collapses the double integral of the noise term to $\int_0^t e^{F(t-\tau)}Qe^{F^\T(t - \tau)}\,\dd\tau$, the integral above after the substitution $\tau \mapsto t - \tau$. (Rigorously, $\xi\,\dd t$ is the increment of a Brownian motion, and this step is the Itô isometry.) Since $F$ is Hurwitz, $\norm{e^{Ft}} \le Me^{-\beta t}$ for some $M, \beta > 0$ (chapter 7), so the first term tends to zero and the integral converges to $\Sigma_\infty = \int_0^\infty e^{F\tau}Qe^{F^\T\tau}\,\dd\tau$. Then $F\Sigma_\infty + \Sigma_\infty F^\T = \int_0^\infty \frac{\dd}{\dd\tau}\bigl(e^{F\tau}Qe^{F^\T\tau}\bigr)\,\dd\tau = -Q$, and the solution is unique because the map $X \mapsto FX + XF^\T$ has the eigenvalues $\lambda_i + \lambda_j \neq 0$, sums of eigenvalues of $F$ (the Lyapunov operator of chapter 7, for $F^\T$).

Take $\dot x = ax + w$, $y = x + v$ with independent zero-mean white noises of intensities $W, V > 0$: $\mathbb E[w(t)w(t')] = W\,\delta(t - t')$ and $\mathbb E[v(t)v(t')] = V\,\delta(t - t')$. A gain $\ell > a$ gives $\dot{\tilde x} = -(\ell - a)\tilde x + w - \ell v$, a stable scalar system driven by white noise of intensity $W + \ell^2 V$, since the intensities of independent noises add. By with $\gamma = \ell - a$ and $q = W + \ell^2 V$, the stationary error variance is

$$\sigma^2(\ell) = \lim_{t\to\infty}\mathbb E\bigl[\tilde x(t)^2\bigr] = \frac{W + \ell^2 V}{2(\ell - a)} .$$

It blows up as $\ell \downarrow a$, where the correction is too slow and process noise accumulates, and grows like $\ell V/2$ as $\ell \to \infty$, where measurement noise is injected at full gain. Setting $\dd\sigma^2/\dd\ell = 0$ gives $V\ell^2 - 2aV\ell - W = 0$, whose root above $a$ is the best gain:

$$\ell^\star = a + \sqrt{a^2 + W/V}, \qquad \sigma^2(\ell^\star) = V\ell^\star .$$

The optimal observer speed $\ell^\star - a = \sqrt{a^2 + W/V}$ is set by the ratio of process to measurement noise. The steady-state Kalman–Bucy gain of chapter 11 is exactly $\ell^\star$.

Output feedback and the separation principle

Feed back the estimate instead of the state: $u = -K\hat x + r$. The closed loop has state $(x, \hat x) \in \R^{2n}$, and the controller is now a dynamic system from $y$ (and $r$) to $u$: the observer followed by the gain $K$.

For the plant $\dot x = Ax + Bu$, $y = Cx$, the observer of and $u = -K\hat x + r$, the coordinates $(x, \tilde x)$ give

$$\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\begin{bmatrix} x\\ \tilde x\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} A - BK & BK\\ 0 & A - LC\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} x\\ \tilde x\end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} B\\ 0\end{bmatrix} r .$$

What is not separated. The modes of $A - LC$ are uncontrollable from $r$ (), which is why they vanish from the reference response, but everything the observer does not know excites them. A load disturbance at the plant input, $\dot x = Ax + B(u + d)$, gives $\dot{\tilde x} = (A - LC)\tilde x + Bd$, and measurement noise gives $\dot{\tilde x} = (A - LC)\tilde x - Lv$. Robustness is not separated either. Broken at the plant input, the state-feedback loop has the loop transfer function $K(sI - A)^{-1}B$, while the observer-based loop has $K_{\rm ob}(s)\,C(sI - A)^{-1}B$. The two can differ greatly: chapter 12 presents Doyle's 1978 example, in which the second has arbitrarily small stability margins although the first is a linear quadratic regulator loop of chapter 11, which has large guaranteed margins (its margin corollary).

The loop in transfer-function form

A single-input single-output plant with proper rational transfer function $G(s)$ is in negative feedback with a proper rational controller $K(s)$. The external signals are the reference $r$, a load disturbance $d$ added at the plant input and measurement noise $v$ added to the measured output, so $y = G(u + d)$ and $u = K(r - y - v)$. The loop transfer function is $L(s) = G(s)K(s)$. Two warnings on notation: $K(s)$ is a transfer function and $K$ alone the state-feedback gain (the observer-based controller of is $K(s) = K_{\rm ob}(s)$), and $L(s)$ is not the observer gain $L$. The loop is well posed if $1 + L(\infty) \neq 0$, which holds automatically when $G$ is strictly proper, because then $L(\infty) = 0$. On the imaginary axis we write $L(i\omega)$ with $i^2 = -1$; engineering texts write $j$ for the imaginary unit.

For the single-input single-output loop above, with zero initial conditions, the Laplace transforms of the signals satisfy

$$y = T\,r + GS\,d - T\,v, \qquad u = KS\,(r - v) - T\,d, \qquad e = r - y = S\,r - GS\,d + T\,v,$$

with the sensitivity function $S = 1/(1 + L)$ and the complementary sensitivity function $T = L/(1 + L)$. In particular $S + T = 1$. These are identities between rational functions, $1 + L$ being nonzero by well-posedness, and they hold whether or not the loop is stable.

Proof. Substituting $u$ into $y = G(u + d)$ gives $(1 + GK)\,y = GK\,r + G\,d - GK\,v$. Then $r - y - v = S\,r - GS\,d - S\,v$, so $u = KS\,r - KGS\,d - KS\,v$ with $KGS = LS = T$, because scalar transfer functions commute.

Integral action and the internal model principle

Track a constant reference and reject a constant load disturbance exactly, for a plant whose parameters are known only approximately.

Proportional control of a first-order plant. Let $\dot y = -ay + b(u + d)$ with real $a$ and $b > 0$, and $u = k_p(r - y)$ with gain $k_p$. The closed loop $\dot y = -(a + bk_p)y + b(k_p r + d)$ is stable if and only if $a + bk_p > 0$, and then, for constant $r$ and $d$,

$$e_\infty = \lim_{t\to\infty}\bigl(r - y(t)\bigr) = \frac{ar - bd}{a + bk_p}.$$

Unless $ar = bd$, the error is small only for large $k_p$, and its value depends on the uncertain $a$ and $b$.

Add an integrator: $\dot z = r - y$ and $u = k_p(r - y) + k_i z$ with integral gain $k_i$. At any equilibrium $\dot z = 0$, that is $y = r$, whatever $a$, $b$ and $d$. The integrator settles where $k_i z_\infty = ar/b - d$, exactly the input that cancels the disturbance and holds $y = r$. The closed loop has characteristic polynomial $s^2 + (a + bk_p)s + bk_i$, which has both roots in the open left half-plane if and only if $a + bk_p > 0$ and $bk_i > 0$: the roots have sum $-(a + bk_p)$ and product $bk_i$, two real roots are both negative exactly when their sum is negative and their product positive, and a complex pair has real part $-(a + bk_p)/2$ and a positive product.

Because $G$ is proper, $\deg n_r, \deg n_d < \deg g$ and $\deg\phi = \deg(d_Gd_K)$, the numerator has degree at most $\deg d_G + \deg g - 1 + \deg d_K - \deg g = \deg\phi - 1$. So $E$ is strictly proper with poles among the roots of $\phi$, all in the open left half-plane, and $e(t)$ is a sum of decaying exponentials times polynomials.

A controller thus rejects the signals whose generator it contains. For one fixed plant the converse fails, because the plant itself can block a signal.

Let $G(s) = \dfrac{s^2 + 1}{s\,(s^2 + 2s + 3)}$ and $K(s) = 1$. Then $\phi(s) = s\,(s^2 + 2s + 3) + s^2 + 1 = (s + 1)^3$, so the loop is internally stable (). With $r = v = 0$ and the load disturbance $d(t) = \sin t$, whose transform is $1/(s^2 + 1)$, and the factorization in the proof of give

$$E(s) = -\frac{n_G(s)\,d_K(s)}{\phi(s)\,(s^2 + 1)} = -\frac{1}{(s + 1)^3},$$

so $e(t) = -\tfrac12 t^2e^{-t} \to 0$, although $K$ has no poles at $\pm i$: the plant zeros at $\pm i$ cancel the generator $s^2 + 1$ of the disturbance, as the controller poles do in .

The converse holds for rejection that survives perturbations of the plant:

Let the references and disturbances be generated by an autonomous linear system, the exosystem: $\dot\eta = 0$ for constants, $\ddot\eta = -\omega_0^2\eta$ for sinusoids of frequency $\omega_0$. A linear controller that keeps the loop internally stable, drives the error to zero for every such signal, and continues to do so under every sufficiently small perturbation of the plant, must contain a copy of the exosystem's dynamics driven by the error: an integrator for constants, an oscillator at $\omega_0$ for sinusoids.

Precise statement and proof: B. A. Francis and W. M. Wonham, "The internal model principle of control theory", Automatica 12 (1976) 457–465. and are the sufficiency half for single-loop systems.

Robust perfect adaptation. When Escherichia coli is exposed to a step of attractant, its tumbling frequency changes and then returns exactly to the prestimulus value, and this precision is robust to large changes in protein levels (predicted in the model of N. Barkai and S. Leibler, Nature 387 (1997) 913–917, and observed by U. Alon, M. G. Surette, N. Barkai and S. Leibler, Nature 397 (1999) 168–171). T.-M. Yi, Y. Huang, M. I. Simon and J. Doyle (PNAS 97 (2000) 4649–4653) showed that integral feedback is structurally inherent in the Barkai–Leibler model of the chemotaxis network, with the receptor methylation level acting as the integral of the deviation of receptor activity from its adapted value, and argued that integral control in some form is necessary for perfect adaptation to be robust. Read this way, the methylation level is the cell's memory of the stimulus: like $z_\infty$ above, it settles exactly where the disturbance is cancelled.

Proportional–integral–derivative control, $K(s) = k_p + k_i/s + k_d s$, is the engineering default built on these ideas. The derivative term is not proper: its gain $\abs{k_d\,i\omega} = \abs{k_d}\,\omega$ grows without bound, so it amplifies high-frequency noise, and it is implemented filtered, as $k_d s/(1 + sT_f)$ with a small time constant $T_f > 0$, whose gain $\abs{k_d}\,\omega/\sqrt{1 + \omega^2T_f^2}$ stays below $\abs{k_d}/T_f$. The resulting $K$ is proper, and for $k_i \neq 0$ it has a pole at $0$: the integral term is the internal model of constants, and every internally stable loop with this controller has zero steady-state error to constant references and load disturbances (). As a rule of thumb, the proportional term sets the loop gain and speed, and the derivative term, whose phase is $+90^\circ$ at every frequency before filtering, adds phase lead and damping. When the actuator saturates, the integrator keeps integrating an error it cannot remove (integrator windup) and needs anti-windup logic (Åström and Murray, ch. 11).

Self-check

  1. For the double integrator $\ddot q = u$, find the gain that puts both closed-loop poles at $-2$, and the initial input from $x(0) = (1, 0)$.

    Answer

    $\chi(s) = (s + 2)^2 = s^2 + 4s + 4$ and $\det(sI - A + BK) = s^2 + k_2 s + k_1$, so $K = [4\ \ 4]$ and $u(0) = -Kx(0) = -4$. Doubling the pole speed would make it $-16$.

  2. Let $A = I_2$ and let $b \in \R^2$ be any single input. Show that $1 \in \Lambda(A - bK)$ for every $K$, and say which test fails.

    Answer

    $K \in \R^{1\times 2}$ has a nonzero null vector $x$, and $(I - bK)x = x$. The other eigenvalue is $1 - Kb$ (the trace is $2 - Kb$). The PBH test fails at $\lambda = 1$: $\rank[\lambda I - A,\ b] = \rank[0,\ b] = 1 < 2$. Every $w \perp b$ is a left eigenvector orthogonal to the input.

  3. Why does the observer not appear in the transfer function from $r$ to $y$ of an observer-based controller, and why does that not make it irrelevant?

    Answer

    In $(x, \tilde x)$ coordinates the error dynamics are not driven by $r$, so the observer modes are uncontrollable from $r$ (). They are driven by initial estimation errors, load disturbances ($Bd$) and measurement noise ($-Lv$), and the loop transfer function at the plant input changes from $K(sI - A)^{-1}B$ to $K_{\rm ob}(s)\,C(sI - A)^{-1}B$, which governs robustness (chapter 12).

  4. Show that $\max\bigl(\abs{S(i\omega)}, \abs{T(i\omega)}\bigr) \ge 1/2$ at every frequency. In , which of the four closed-loop maps reveals the instability?

    Answer

    As in , $1 = \abs{S + T} \le \abs S + \abs T \le 2\max(\abs S, \abs T)$. The map $GS = (s + 1)/\bigl((s - 1)(s + 2)\bigr)$ from load disturbance to output has the pole at $1$; $S$, $T$ and $KS$ are stable.

  5. The plant $G(s) = s/(s + 1)$ has a zero at the origin. Is the loop with $K(s) = k_i/s$, $k_i > 0$, internally stable?

    Answer

    No. $\phi = (s + 1)s + s\,k_i = s(s + 1 + k_i)$ has a root at $0$: the integrator cancels the plant zero. $S$, $T$ and $GS$ are stable, but $KS = k_i(s + 1)/\bigl(s(s + 1 + k_i)\bigr)$ has a pole at $0$, so a constant measurement offset makes the input ramp without bound while the output looks fine. By , an integrator in $K$ is compatible with internal stability only if $G$ has no zero at the origin, that is, $n_G(0) \neq 0$.
