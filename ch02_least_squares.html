Inner products, projections and least squares

An inner product gives a vector space lengths and angles, and with them a notion of the closest point. Orthogonal projection then answers the two questions chapter 1 left open about a linear system $Ax = b$, with matrix $A \in \R^{m\times n}$, unknown $x \in \R^n$ and right-hand side $b \in \R^m$: which $x$ comes closest when there is no solution, and which solution is smallest when there are many. Measure size with a different norm and the answers change, sometimes to sparse ones.

Inner products and norms

Add lengths and angles to a vector space in a way that is compatible with linearity, so that "perpendicular" and "closest" make sense.

Let $V$ be a vector space over $\F$, where $\F$ is $\R$ (the real numbers) or $\C$ (the complex numbers). An inner product on $V$ is a map $V\times V \to \F$, $(x, y) \mapsto \inner{x}{y}$, such that for all $x, y, z \in V$ and $\alpha \in \F$

$$\inner{\alpha x + y}{z} = \alpha\inner{x}{z} + \inner{y}{z},\qquad \inner{y}{x} = \overline{\inner{x}{y}},\qquad \inner{x}{x} > 0 \ \text{ for } x \ne 0,$$

where the bar denotes complex conjugation (no effect on real numbers). The first two rules give conjugate linearity in the second slot: $\inner{x}{\alpha y} = \overline{\inner{\alpha y}{x}} = \bar\alpha\,\overline{\inner{y}{x}} = \bar\alpha\inner{x}{y}$, and in the same way $\inner{x}{y + z} = \inner{x}{y} + \inner{x}{z}$. The standard inner products are $\inner{x}{y} = y^\T x = \sum_i x_i y_i$ on $\R^n$ and $\inner{x}{y} = y^* x = \sum_i x_i\bar y_i$ on $\C^n$, where $y^\T$ is the transpose and $y^* = \bar y^\T$ the conjugate transpose.

Three more inner products recur in the guide.

(i) Let $W \in \C^{n\times n}$ be positive definite, meaning $W = W^*$ and $x^*Wx > 0$ for every $x \ne 0$, written $W \succ 0$. Then $\inner{x}{y}_W = y^*Wx$ is an inner product on $\C^n$, and for real $W \succ 0$ its restriction $y^\T Wx$ is an inner product on $\R^n$. (ii) On the space $C[a, b]$ of continuous functions $[a, b] \to \F$, with $a < b$, $\inner{f}{g} = \int_a^b f(t)\overline{g(t)}\,\dd t$ is an inner product, the starting point of chapter 5. (iii) On $\F^{m\times n}$, the Frobenius inner product $\inner{A}{B} = \tr(B^*A) = \sum_{ij} a_{ij}\bar b_{ij}$, where $\tr$ is the trace, is an inner product; it induces the Frobenius norm $\norm{A}_F = (\sum_{ij}\abs{a_{ij}}^2)^{1/2}$.

Proof. (i) $y^*W(\alpha x + x') = \alpha\,y^*Wx + y^*Wx'$; $\overline{x^*Wy} = (x^*Wy)^* = y^*W^*x = y^*Wx$, which is conjugate symmetry; and positivity is the hypothesis. With $W = I$ this covers the standard inner products, since $x^*x = \sum_i\abs{x_i}^2$. (ii) Linearity and conjugate symmetry hold because the integral is linear. If $f \ne 0$, then $\abs{f}^2$ is continuous, nonnegative and positive at some point, hence on an interval of positive length, so $\int_a^b\abs{f(t)}^2\,\dd t > 0$. (iii) $\tr(B^*A) = \sum_j\sum_i \bar b_{ij}a_{ij}$ is the standard inner product of the vectors in $\F^{mn}$ obtained by stacking the columns of $A$ and of $B$.

Over $\C$ the conjugate cannot be dropped: the nonzero vector $x = (1, i)^\T$, with $i$ the imaginary unit (engineering texts write $j$), has $x^\T x = 1 + i^2 = 0$ but $x^* x = 2$, so the form $y^\T x$ violates positivity on $\C^2$.

A norm on $V$ is a map $\norm{\cdot}: V \to [0, \infty)$ such that $\norm{x} = 0$ only for $x = 0$, $\norm{\alpha x} = \abs{\alpha}\norm{x}$, and $\norm{x + y} \le \norm{x} + \norm{y}$ (the triangle inequality). An inner product induces the norm $\norm{x} = \sqrt{\inner{x}{x}}$; for the standard inner product on $\F^n$ this is the Euclidean norm $\norm{x} = (\sum_i \abs{x_i}^2)^{1/2}$. In an inner product space, $\abs{\inner{x}{y}} \le \norm{x}\norm{y}$ for all $x, y$, with equality if and only if $x$ and $y$ are linearly dependent.

Proof. If $y = 0$ both sides vanish. Otherwise let $c = \inner{x}{y}/\norm{y}^2$ and expand, using linearity in the first slot and conjugate linearity in the second:

$$0 \le \norm{x - cy}^2 = \norm{x}^2 - \bar c\inner{x}{y} - c\inner{y}{x} + \abs{c}^2\norm{y}^2 = \norm{x}^2 - \frac{\abs{\inner{x}{y}}^2}{\norm{y}^2}.$$

Equality holds exactly when $x = cy$. The vector $cy$ is the projection of $x$ onto the line through $y$ (), so the inequality says that a projection is never longer than the vector it came from; extends this to every subspace.

The induced map $x \mapsto \norm{x} = \sqrt{\inner{x}{x}}$ is a norm; in particular $\norm{x + y} \le \norm{x} + \norm{y}$. In a real inner product space the angle $\theta \in [0, \pi]$ between nonzero $x$ and $y$ is defined by $\cos\theta = \inner{x}{y}/(\norm{x}\norm{y})$, a number in $[-1, 1]$ by Cauchy–Schwarz.

Proof. $\norm{x} = 0$ only for $x = 0$ by positivity, and $\norm{\alpha x}^2 = \alpha\bar\alpha\inner{x}{x} = \abs{\alpha}^2\norm{x}^2$. For the triangle inequality, expand and apply Cauchy–Schwarz, writing $\Re$ for the real part:

$$\norm{x + y}^2 = \norm{x}^2 + 2\Re\inner{x}{y} + \norm{y}^2 \le \norm{x}^2 + 2\norm{x}\norm{y} + \norm{y}^2 = (\norm{x} + \norm{y})^2.$$

The $p$-norms on $\F^n$ are $\norm{x}_p = (\sum_i \abs{x_i}^p)^{1/p}$ for $1 \le p < \infty$ and $\norm{x}_\infty = \max_i \abs{x_i}$. Their unit balls in $\R^2$ are a diamond for $p = 1$, the disk for $p = 2$ and a square for $p = \infty$, each inside the next (part (iii) of ). The extreme case $0 < p < 1$ fails to be a norm: with $e_k$ the $k$-th standard basis vector, $\norm{e_1 + e_2}_p = 2^{1/p} > 2 = \norm{e_1}_p + \norm{e_2}_p$, so the midpoint $\tfrac12(e_1 + e_2)$ of two points of the "unit ball" lies outside it, and the "unit ball" is not convex.

Let $1 \le p \le \infty$, and define the dual exponent $q \in [1, \infty]$ by $1/p + 1/q = 1$, with $q = \infty$ for $p = 1$ and $q = 1$ for $p = \infty$. For all $x, y \in \F^n$: (i) Hölder's inequality $\abs{\sum_i x_iy_i} \le \norm{x}_p\norm{y}_q$ holds; for $p = q = 2$, applied to $x$ and $\bar y$, it is Cauchy–Schwarz for the standard inner product. (ii) Minkowski's inequality $\norm{x + y}_p \le \norm{x}_p + \norm{y}_p$ holds, so $\norm{\cdot}_p$ is a norm. (iii) With $\mathbf 1 = (1, \dots, 1)^\T$ the all-ones vector,

$$\norm{x}_\infty \le \norm{x}_2 \le \norm{x}_1 \le \sqrt{n}\,\norm{x}_2 \le n\norm{x}_\infty,$$

and no constant can be improved: $x = e_1$ gives equality in the first two inequalities and $x = \mathbf 1$ in the last two.

Proof

(i) For $p = 1$, $\abs{\sum_i x_iy_i} \le \sum_i\abs{x_i}\abs{y_i} \le \max_i\abs{y_i}\sum_i\abs{x_i}$, and $p = \infty$ is the same with the roles of $x$ and $y$ exchanged. Let $1 < p < \infty$. By homogeneity, and as the case $x = 0$ or $y = 0$ is trivial, we may assume $\norm{x}_p = \norm{y}_q = 1$. For $s, t > 0$, concavity of the logarithm gives $\log(\tfrac1p s^p + \tfrac1q t^q) \ge \tfrac1p\log s^p + \tfrac1q\log t^q = \log(st)$, that is, Young's inequality $st \le s^p/p + t^q/q$, which also holds when $s$ or $t$ is $0$. Summing it over $i$,

$$\sum_i\abs{x_i}\abs{y_i} \le \sum_i\Big(\frac{\abs{x_i}^p}{p} + \frac{\abs{y_i}^q}{q}\Big) = \frac1p + \frac1q = 1.$$

(ii) For $p = 1$ and $p = \infty$ this follows from $\abs{x_i + y_i} \le \abs{x_i} + \abs{y_i}$. For $1 < p < \infty$ let $s_i = \abs{x_i + y_i}^{p-1}$. Since $(p - 1)q = p$, $\norm{s}_q = \norm{x + y}_p^{p-1}$, and (i), applied to $(\abs{x_i})$ and $(s_i)$ and to $(\abs{y_i})$ and $(s_i)$, gives

$$\begin{aligned}\norm{x + y}_p^p = \sum_i s_i\abs{x_i + y_i} &\le \sum_i s_i\abs{x_i} + \sum_i s_i\abs{y_i}\\ &\le \norm{x + y}_p^{p-1}\big(\norm{x}_p + \norm{y}_p\big);\end{aligned}$$

divide by $\norm{x + y}_p^{p-1}$ when it is nonzero. Definiteness and $\norm{\alpha x}_p = \abs{\alpha}\norm{x}_p$ are clear.

(iii) $\max_i\abs{x_i}^2 \le \sum_i\abs{x_i}^2 \le (\sum_i\abs{x_i})^2$ gives the first two inequalities; the third is (i) with $p = 2$ for $(\abs{x_1}, \dots, \abs{x_n})$ and $\mathbf 1$; the fourth is $\sum_i\abs{x_i}^2 \le n\max_i\abs{x_i}^2$. Finally $\norm{e_1}_p = 1$ for every $p$, and $\norm{\mathbf 1}_1 = n$, $\norm{\mathbf 1}_2 = \sqrt n$, $\norm{\mathbf 1}_\infty = 1$.

A norm induced by an inner product satisfies

$$\norm{x + y}^2 + \norm{x - y}^2 = 2\norm{x}^2 + 2\norm{y}^2 \qquad \text{for all } x, y.$$

Conversely, every norm satisfying this identity is induced by exactly one inner product, recovered by the polarization identity $\inner{x}{y} = \tfrac14(\norm{x + y}^2 - \norm{x - y}^2)$ in the real case, plus $\tfrac{i}{4}(\norm{x + iy}^2 - \norm{x - iy}^2)$ in the complex case.

Proof. Expanding $\norm{x \pm y}^2 = \norm{x}^2 \pm 2\Re\inner{x}{y} + \norm{y}^2$ and adding gives the law; subtracting gives $\norm{x + y}^2 - \norm{x - y}^2 = 4\Re\inner{x}{y}$, so the norm determines the inner product (in the complex case apply this also to $iy$, using $\Re\inner{x}{iy} = \Im\inner{x}{y}$, where $\Im$ is the imaginary part). That the polarization formula defines an inner product whenever the law holds is the substantial direction, proved in P. Jordan and J. von Neumann, "On inner products in linear, metric spaces", Ann. of Math. 36 (1935) 719–723.

The $\ell_1$ and $\ell_\infty$ norms (the $p$-norms with $p = 1$ and $p = \infty$) come from no inner product. With $x = e_1$ and $y = e_2$ in $\R^2$, $\norm{x \pm y}_1 = 2$, so the left side of the law is $8$ while the right side is $4$; and $\norm{x \pm y}_\infty = 1$ makes the left side $2$. In general $\norm{e_1 \pm e_2}_p = 2^{1/p}$, and $2\cdot 2^{2/p} = 4$ only for $p = 2$: among the $p$-norms, the Euclidean norm is the only one with an inner product behind it.

For any two norms $\norm{\cdot}_a$ and $\norm{\cdot}_b$ on $\F^n$ there are constants $0 < c \le C$ with $c\norm{x}_a \le \norm{x}_b \le C\norm{x}_a$ for all $x \in \F^n$.

Idea: every norm is continuous, and the unit sphere of $\norm{\cdot}_2$ is compact, so $\norm{x}_b$ attains a positive minimum and a finite maximum on it. Proof: E. Kreyszig, Introductory Functional Analysis with Applications, §2.4; Horn and Johnson, Matrix Analysis, ch. 5.

Let $\norm{\cdot}_a$ and $\norm{\cdot}_b$ be norms on $\F^n$. A sequence $(x_k)$ in $\F^n$ converges to $x$, and a subset of $\F^n$ is bounded, in $\norm{\cdot}_a$ if and only if in $\norm{\cdot}_b$. Hence continuity of a map from $\F^n$ into a normed space, or from a normed space into $\F^n$, does not depend on the norm on $\F^n$.

Proof. Apply the two bounds of to $x_k - x$ and to the elements of the set; continuity between normed spaces is sequential continuity.

Equivalence does not make the norms interchangeable. The constants depend on $n$: by part (iii) of , the best constant in $\norm{x}_1 \le C\norm{x}_2$ is $\sqrt n$. And the minimizers of a problem depend on the norm, as shows. In infinite dimension equivalence fails outright (chapter 5, normed spaces).

Orthogonality, Gram–Schmidt and QR

Find bases in which coordinates are computed by inner products alone, and factor a matrix so that such a basis for its column space becomes visible.

Vectors $x$ and $y$ are orthogonal, $x \perp y$, if $\inner{x}{y} = 0$. A list $q_1, \dots, q_k$ is orthonormal if $\inner{q_i}{q_j} = \delta_{ij}$, where the Kronecker delta $\delta_{ij}$ is $1$ for $i = j$ and $0$ otherwise. The orthogonal complement of a subspace $\mathcal S$ is $\mathcal S^\perp = \{x : \inner{x}{s} = 0 \text{ for all } s \in \mathcal S\}$, again a subspace ().

(i) If $x \perp y$ then $\norm{x + y}^2 = \norm{x}^2 + \norm{y}^2$. (ii) Orthonormal lists are linearly independent. (iii) If $q_1, \dots, q_n$ is an orthonormal basis of $V$, then for every $x \in V$

$$x = \sum_{i=1}^n \inner{x}{q_i}\,q_i, \qquad \norm{x}^2 = \sum_{i=1}^n \abs{\inner{x}{q_i}}^2.$$

Proof. (i) The cross terms in $\norm{x + y}^2$ are $2\Re\inner{x}{y} = 0$. (ii) If $\sum_j c_j q_j = 0$, the inner product with $q_i$ gives $c_i = 0$. (iii) Write $x = \sum_j c_j q_j$; the inner product with $q_i$ gives $c_i = \inner{x}{q_i}$, and (i) applied term by term gives the norm.

Let $a_1, \dots, a_n$ be linearly independent vectors in an inner product space. Define recursively, for $k = 1, \dots, n$,

$$v_k = a_k - \sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\inner{a_k}{q_j}\,q_j, \qquad q_k = \frac{v_k}{\norm{v_k}}.$$

Then $q_1, \dots, q_n$ is orthonormal and $\spn(q_1, \dots, q_k) = \spn(a_1, \dots, a_k)$ for every $k$.

Proof. Induction on $k$; assume the claims for $k - 1$. Then $v_k \ne 0$, for otherwise $a_k$ would lie in $\spn(q_1, \dots, q_{k-1}) = \spn(a_1, \dots, a_{k-1})$. For $i < k$, $\inner{v_k}{q_i} = \inner{a_k}{q_i} - \inner{a_k}{q_i} = 0$ by orthonormality of $q_1, \dots, q_{k-1}$. The recursion shows $q_k \in \spn(a_1, \dots, a_k)$ and $a_k \in \spn(q_1, \dots, q_k)$, so the spans agree.

Let $A \in \F^{m\times n}$, $m \ge n$, have full column rank. Then $A = \hat Q\hat R$, where $\hat Q \in \F^{m\times n}$ has orthonormal columns ($\hat Q^*\hat Q = I_n$, the $n\times n$ identity) and $\hat R \in \F^{n\times n}$ is upper triangular with positive diagonal; this reduced QR factorization is unique. Appending $m - n$ further orthonormal columns gives a full QR factorization $A = QR$ with $Q \in \F^{m\times m}$ unitary ($Q^*Q = QQ^* = I_m$) and $R = \begin{bmatrix}\hat R\\ 0\end{bmatrix}$. A full QR factorization exists for every $A$ with $m \ge n$, whatever its rank.

Proof. Apply Gram–Schmidt to the columns $a_k$ of $A$. The recursion rearranges to $a_k = \sum_{j < k}\inner{a_k}{q_j}\,q_j + \norm{v_k}\,q_k$, which is column $k$ of $\hat Q\hat R$ with $r_{jk} = \inner{a_k}{q_j}$ for $j < k$ and $r_{kk} = \norm{v_k} > 0$. The extra columns come from extending $q_1, \dots, q_n$ to an orthonormal basis of $\F^m$ (run Gram–Schmidt on $q_1, \dots, q_n, e_1, \dots, e_m$ and skip every vector whose $v_k$ vanishes). Uniqueness and the rank-deficient case: Trefethen and Bau, Numerical Linear Algebra, Lecture 7.

A rule of thumb from numerical practice: in floating point the recursion as written (classical Gram–Schmidt) loses orthogonality when the columns are nearly dependent. Modified Gram–Schmidt and Householder reflections, which in exact arithmetic compute the same factors (Householder up to factors of modulus $1$, signs in the real case, on the columns of $\hat Q$ and the rows of $\hat R$), behave far better, and numerical libraries use Householder QR (Trefethen and Bau, Lectures 8–10 and 16; Golub and Van Loan, ch. 5).

For $Q \in \F^{m\times m}$ the following are equivalent: (i) $Q^*Q = I$; (ii) $\inner{Qx}{Qy} = \inner{x}{y}$ for all $x, y$; (iii) $\norm{Qx} = \norm{x}$ for all $x$. Such a $Q$ is unitary (orthogonal when real), and $Q^{-1} = Q^*$.

Proof. (i) $\Rightarrow$ (ii): $\inner{Qx}{Qy} = y^*Q^*Qx = y^*x$. (ii) $\Rightarrow$ (iii): take $y = x$. (iii) $\Rightarrow$ (ii): polarization () writes $\inner{x}{y}$ in terms of norms of $x \pm y$ and $x \pm iy$, which $Q$ preserves. (ii) $\Rightarrow$ (i): the $(j, i)$ entry of $Q^*Q - I$ is $\inner{Qe_i}{Qe_j} - \inner{e_i}{e_j} = 0$. Finally $Q^*Q = I$ makes $Q$ injective ($Qx = 0$ gives $x = Q^*Qx = 0$), hence invertible by the corollary on square matrices (chapter 1), and $Q^{-1} = Q^*$.

Let $\mathcal S$ be a subspace of an inner product space $V$. Then $\mathcal S^\perp$ is a subspace and $\mathcal S \cap \mathcal S^\perp = \{0\}$. If $\dim V = n$ is finite (for instance $V = \F^n$), then moreover $V = \mathcal S \oplus \mathcal S^\perp$, $\dim\mathcal S + \dim\mathcal S^\perp = n$, and $(\mathcal S^\perp)^\perp = \mathcal S$.

Proof. $\mathcal S^\perp$ is a subspace because $x \mapsto \inner{x}{s}$ is linear for each $s$, and a vector $x \in \mathcal S \cap \mathcal S^\perp$ has $\inner{x}{x} = 0$, so $x = 0$. Now let $\dim V = n$. Take an orthonormal basis $q_1, \dots, q_k$ of $\mathcal S$ (Gram–Schmidt on any basis), extend it to a basis of $V$, and apply Gram–Schmidt again; this leaves $q_1, \dots, q_k$ unchanged and gives an orthonormal basis $q_1, \dots, q_n$ of $V$. By , $x = \sum_i c_i q_i$ is orthogonal to $\mathcal S$ exactly when $c_i = \inner{x}{q_i} = 0$ for $i \le k$. So $\mathcal S^\perp = \spn(q_{k+1}, \dots, q_n)$, which gives the direct sum and the dimension count, and the same argument with the two halves of the basis exchanged gives $(\mathcal S^\perp)^\perp = \spn(q_1, \dots, q_k) = \mathcal S$. These are the facts chapter 1 used to prove the fundamental theorem of linear algebra.

Two warnings. Orthogonality belongs to the inner product, not to the vectors: $e_1 \perp e_2$ for the standard inner product on $\R^2$, but with $W = \begin{bmatrix}2 & 1\\ 1 & 2\end{bmatrix} \succ 0$ we get $\inner{e_1}{e_2}_W = e_2^\T W e_1 = 1$. And for a tall matrix with orthonormal columns, $\hat Q^\T$ is a left inverse but not a right inverse: $\hat Q = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{bmatrix}1\\ 1\end{bmatrix}$ has $\hat Q^\T\hat Q = 1$, yet $\hat Q\hat Q^\T = \tfrac12\begin{bmatrix}1 & 1\\ 1 & 1\end{bmatrix} \ne I$. That product is the orthogonal projector onto the line $x_1 = x_2$, the subject of the next section.

Orthogonal and oblique projection

Given a subspace $\mathcal S$ of an inner product space $V$ and a point $b \in V$, find the point of $\mathcal S$ closest to $b$.

$V$ is finite-dimensional with an inner product and its induced norm, for instance $\F^m$ with $\inner{x}{y} = y^*x$, and $\mathcal S$ is a subspace with an orthonormal basis $q_1, \dots, q_k$, which exists by .

Take a line, $\mathcal S = \spn(a)$ in $\R^m$ with $a \ne 0$. The function $f(t) = \norm{b - ta}^2$, which expands to $\norm{b}^2 - 2t\,a^\T b + t^2\norm{a}^2$, is minimized at $t^\star = a^\T b/\norm{a}^2$, and then $a^\T(b - t^\star a) = 0$: the error is perpendicular to the line. Extreme cases: if $b \in \mathcal S$ the closest point is $b$ itself, and if $b \perp \mathcal S$ it is $0$.

The closest point $p \in \mathcal S$ exists, is unique, and is characterized by $b - p \perp \mathcal S$.

For every $b \in V$ there is a unique $p \in \mathcal S$ with $\norm{b - p} \le \norm{b - s}$ for all $s \in \mathcal S$. It is the unique $p \in \mathcal S$ with $b - p \in \mathcal S^\perp$, and

$$p = \sum_{i=1}^k \inner{b}{q_i}\,q_i, \qquad \norm{b - s}^2 = \norm{b - p}^2 + \norm{p - s}^2 \quad \text{for all } s \in \mathcal S.$$

Proof. Define $p$ by the formula. For each $j$, $\inner{b - p}{q_j} = \inner{b}{q_j} - \inner{b}{q_j} = 0$, so $b - p \perp \mathcal S$. For $s \in \mathcal S$ write $b - s = (b - p) + (p - s)$; the second term lies in $\mathcal S$ and is therefore orthogonal to the first, and Pythagoras gives the identity. Hence $\norm{b - s} \ge \norm{b - p}$, with equality only for $s = p$. If $p' \in \mathcal S$ also has $b - p' \perp \mathcal S$, then $p - p' = (b - p') - (b - p)$ lies in $\mathcal S \cap \mathcal S^\perp = \{0\}$ ().

The map $b \mapsto p$ of is the orthogonal projector onto $\mathcal S$, written $P_{\mathcal S}$.

(i) $P_{\mathcal S}$ is linear, its range is $\mathcal S$, $P_{\mathcal S}s = s$ for every $s \in \mathcal S$, and $P_{\mathcal S}^2 = P_{\mathcal S}$. Now let $V = \F^m$ with the standard inner product. (ii) If the columns of $\hat Q \in \F^{m\times k}$ are an orthonormal basis of $\mathcal S$, then $P_{\mathcal S} = \hat Q\hat Q^*$. (iii) If $A \in \F^{m\times k}$ has full column rank and $\Range(A) = \mathcal S$, where $\Range(A)$ is the range (column space), then $A^*A$ is invertible and $P_{\mathcal S} = A(A^*A)^{-1}A^*$. (iv) $I - P_{\mathcal S} = P_{\mathcal S^\perp}$.

Proof. (i) The formula of is linear in $b$, because the inner product is linear in its first slot, and takes values in $\mathcal S$. For $s \in \mathcal S$ the point $p = s$ has $s - p = 0 \in \mathcal S^\perp$, so $P_{\mathcal S}s = s$ by the uniqueness in the theorem; hence the range is all of $\mathcal S$, and $P_{\mathcal S}(P_{\mathcal S}b) = P_{\mathcal S}b$. (ii) is the formula of the theorem in matrix form, $\sum_i q_i(q_i^* b) = \hat Q\hat Q^* b$. (iii) If $A^*Ax = 0$ then $\norm{Ax}^2 = x^*A^*Ax = 0$, so $x = 0$, and the square matrix $A^*A$ is invertible. Writing $p = Ay$, the condition $b - p \perp \Range(A)$ reads $A^*(b - Ay) = 0$, so $y = (A^*A)^{-1}A^*b$. An oblique projector is not a closest-point map. $P = \begin{bmatrix}1 & c\\ 0 & 0\end{bmatrix}$ with $c \ne 0$ satisfies $P^2 = P$, has range $\spn(e_1)$ and null space $\spn\big((-c, 1)^\T\big)$, and $P^\T \ne P$. This is a first sighting of a theme of chapter 6: similarity preserves eigenvalues, not geometry.

Least squares

Given $A \in \R^{m\times n}$ and $b \in \R^m$ with $b \notin \Range(A)$, typically because $m > n$ (more measurements than unknowns), find $x$ making the residual $b - Ax$ as small as possible.

Real data for definiteness; for complex $A$ replace $A^\T$ by $A^*$. The residual is measured in the Euclidean norm; changes that choice.

Characterize the minimizers, decide when the minimizer is unique, and compute it in a numerically stable way.

(i) One unknown, $A = a \in \R^m$: $\hat x = a^\T b/a^\T a$, the projection coefficient of . (ii) Fitting a constant, $a = \mathbf 1 = (1, \dots, 1)^\T$: $\hat x = \frac1m\sum_i b_i$, the mean. (iii) Square invertible $A$: $\hat x = A^{-1}b$ with zero residual. (iv) Fitting a line $y = c_0 + c_1 t$ to points $(t_i, y_i)$ whose abscissas $t_i$ are all equal: the two columns $\mathbf 1$ and $t = (t_1, \dots, t_m)^\T$ are parallel, and every line through the point $(t_1, \bar y)$ fits equally well, where $\bar y$ is the mean of the $y_i$. Uniqueness is lost exactly when the columns are dependent, as part 3 of confirms.

Let $A \in \R^{m\times n}$ and $b \in \R^m$.

  1. A vector $\hat x$ minimizes $\norm{Ax - b}$ if and only if it solves the normal equations $A^\T A\hat x = A^\T b$, that is, if and only if the residual $r = b - A\hat x$ lies in $\Null(A^\T)$.
  2. Minimizers always exist, and the fitted vector $A\hat x = P_{\Range(A)}b$ is the same for all of them.
  3. The minimizer is unique if and only if $A$ has full column rank, and then $\hat x = (A^\T A)^{-1}A^\T b$.

Proof by projection. As $x$ ranges over $\R^n$, $Ax$ ranges over $\Range(A)$, so the problem is the closest-point problem for the subspace $\Range(A)$. By , $\hat x$ is a minimizer exactly when $A\hat x = p := P_{\Range(A)}b$, and $p$ is the unique point of $\Range(A)$ with $b - p \perp \Range(A)$; for $A\hat x \in \Range(A)$ this orthogonality reads $A^\T(b - A\hat x) = 0$. Since $p \in \Range(A)$, the equation $A\hat x = p$ is solvable, and its solution set $\hat x_0 + \Null(A)$ (the solution-set theorem, chapter 1) is a single point exactly when $\Null(A) = \{0\}$, that is, when $A$ has full column rank. Then $A^\T A$ is invertible by .

Proof by calculus. The function

$$f(x) = \norm{Ax - b}^2 = x^\T A^\T A x - 2b^\T Ax + b^\T b$$

has gradient $\nabla f(x) = 2(A^\T Ax - A^\T b)$ and constant Hessian $2A^\T A$, which is positive semidefinite because $x^\T A^\T Ax = \norm{Ax}^2 \ge 0$. Expanding exactly, $f(x + h) = f(x) + \nabla f(x)^\T h + \norm{Ah}^2$ for all $h$. If $\nabla f(x) = 0$ this gives $f(x + h) \ge f(x)$, so $x$ is a minimizer; conversely, at a minimizer the gradient of a differentiable function vanishes. So the minimizers are the solutions of the normal equations. This route finds the equations but not their solvability, which the projection argument supplies.

In the language of chapter 1, $b$ splits uniquely as $b = p + r$ with $p \in \Range(A)$ and $r \in \Null(A^\T)$, the left null space (the fundamental theorem of linear algebra). Least squares solves the solvable part $Ax = p$ and discards $r$. That the normal equations are always consistent can also be read off $A^\T A$ directly: $\Null(A^\T A) = \Null(A)$ and $\Range(A^\T A) = \Range(A^\T)$, which contains $A^\T b$ (the subspaces of $A^\T A$, chapter 1).

How sensitive is the least-squares problem itself to its data? The answer depends on the size of the residual.

Let $A \in \R^{m\times n}$ have full column rank and let $b \in \R^m$ satisfy $P_{\Range(A)}b \ne 0$; let $\hat x$ be the least-squares solution and $y = A\hat x$. Define the angle $\theta \in [0, \pi/2)$ between $b$ and $\Range(A)$ by $\cos\theta = \norm{y}/\norm{b}$, and $\eta = \norm{A}\norm{\hat x}/\norm{y}$, which lies in $[1, \kappa(A)]$. For data $d$, here $b$ or $A$ with matrices measured in the induced 2-norm, the relative condition number of $\hat x$ is

$$\lim_{\delta \to 0}\ \sup\Big\{\frac{\norm{\delta\hat x}/\norm{\hat x}}{\norm{\delta d}/\norm{d}} : 0 < \norm{\delta d} \le \delta\Big\},$$

where $\delta\hat x$ is the change of $\hat x$ caused by the perturbation $\delta d$ of the data. It equals $\kappa(A)/(\eta\cos\theta)$ for $d = b$, and it is at most $\kappa(A) + \kappa(A)^2\tan\theta/\eta$ for $d = A$.

Proof: Trefethen and Bau, Numerical Linear Algebra, Lecture 18, Theorem 18.1. Idea: $1 \le \eta \le \kappa(A)$ because $\sigma_{\min}(A)\norm{\hat x} \le \norm{A\hat x} \le \norm{A}\norm{\hat x}$. The map $b \mapsto \hat x = (A^\T A)^{-1}A^\T b$ is linear with norm $1/\sigma_{\min}(A)$, and $\norm{b}/(\sigma_{\min}(A)\norm{\hat x}) = \kappa(A)/(\eta\cos\theta)$. Perturbing $A$ by $E$ changes $\hat x$, to first order, by $(A^\T A)^{-1}(E^\T r - A^\T E\hat x)$ with $r = b - y$; bounding the two terms by $\norm{E}\norm{r}/\sigma_{\min}(A)^2$ and $\norm{E}\norm{\hat x}/\sigma_{\min}(A)$ gives the two summands of the bound.

This is why least-squares problems are solved by QR, not by the normal equations. Let $\eps_{\rm mach} \approx 1.1\times 10^{-16}$ be the unit roundoff of double precision. Forming $A^\T A$ squares the condition number (), and solving the normal equations can introduce relative errors of order $\kappa(A)^2\eps_{\rm mach}$ even when the residual is zero; is an extreme instance. Householder QR is backward stable, so its relative error is of order $\eps_{\rm mach}$ times the condition numbers of (Trefethen and Bau, Lectures 15 and 19). Both are at most $2\kappa(A)$ when $\tan\theta \le 1/\kappa(A)$; the term in $\kappa(A)^2$ exceeds $\kappa(A)$ only for a sizeable residual, $\tan\theta > \eta/\kappa(A)$.

The normal equations can destroy information that QR keeps. Let $\eps = 10^{-8}$ and

$$A = \begin{bmatrix}1 & 1\\ \eps & 0\\ 0 & \eps\end{bmatrix},\qquad A^\T A = \begin{bmatrix}1 + \eps^2 & 1\\ 1 & 1 + \eps^2\end{bmatrix}.$$

$A$ has full column rank and $\kappa(A) = \sqrt{2 + \eps^2}/\eps \approx 1.4\times 10^8$, harmless in double precision. But $1 + \eps^2 = 1 + 10^{-16}$ rounds to $1$, so the computed $A^\T A$ is the singular matrix $\begin{bmatrix}1 & 1\\ 1 & 1\end{bmatrix}$ and the normal equations cannot be solved at all, whereas Householder QR recovers the solution $x = (1, 1)^\T$ of $Ax = (2, \eps, \eps)^\T$ to about 15 digits. The example is due to P. Läuchli (Numer. Math. 3, 1961).

Minimum-norm solutions and the pseudoinverse

If $A \in \R^{m\times n}$ has full row rank ($\rank A = m < n$), then $Ax = b$ has infinitely many solutions, an affine subspace of dimension $n - m$ (the solution-set theorem, chapter 1). Which one is smallest?

One equation in two unknowns, $a^\T x = c$ with $a \in \R^2$ nonzero and $c \in \R$. The solutions form a line perpendicular to $a$, and its point nearest the origin is the projection of $0$ onto that line, $x^\star = c\,a/\norm{a}^2$: a multiple of $a$, so a vector in the row space $\Range(a)$ of the $1\times 2$ matrix $a^\T$.

The minimum-norm solution is the unique solution that lies in the row space $\Range(A^\T)$.

If $A \in \R^{m\times n}$ has full row rank, then $AA^\T$ is invertible, and for every $b \in \R^m$

$$x^\star = A^\T(AA^\T)^{-1}b$$

is the unique solution of $Ax = b$ of minimum Euclidean norm. It is the only solution in $\Range(A^\T)$, every solution has the form $x^\star + z$ with $z \in \Null(A)$, and $\norm{x^\star + z}^2 = \norm{x^\star}^2 + \norm{z}^2$.

Proof. $AA^\T y = 0$ gives $\norm{A^\T y}^2 = 0$, and $A^\T$ has full column rank, so $y = 0$: $AA^\T$ is invertible. Then $Ax^\star = b$, and $x^\star = A^\T y$ with $y = (AA^\T)^{-1}b$ lies in $\Range(A^\T)$. Every solution differs from $x^\star$ by some $z \in \Null(A) = \Range(A^\T)^\perp$ (the fundamental theorem of linear algebra), so Pythagoras gives the norm identity, and the minimum is attained only at $z = 0$.

Least squares and least norm are mirror images. Least squares projects $b$ onto $\Range(A)$ in the output space (); least norm projects the origin onto the affine set of solutions in the input space, which keeps only the row-space component of any solution (). The full-rank formulas mirror each other, $(A^\T A)^{-1}A^\T$ for tall $A$ and $A^\T(AA^\T)^{-1}$ for wide $A$. One matrix does both jobs for every $A$ ().

For $A \in \F^{m\times n}$, a matrix $X \in \F^{n\times m}$ satisfying the four Penrose conditions

$$AXA = A,\qquad XAX = X,\qquad (AX)^* = AX,\qquad (XA)^* = XA$$

is a Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse of $A$. shows that there is exactly one, written $A^+$.

Every $A \in \F^{m\times n}$ has exactly one pseudoinverse $A^+$. It satisfies $AA^+ = P_{\Range(A)}$ and $A^+A = P_{\Range(A^*)}$; it vanishes on $\Null(A^*)$ and maps $\Range(A)$ one-to-one onto $\Range(A^*)$, inverting $A$ there: $A^+Ax = x$ for $x \in \Range(A^*)$. For every $b \in \F^m$ the vector $x^+ = A^+b$ is the minimum-norm least-squares solution: it minimizes $\norm{Ax - b}$, and among all minimizers it alone has the smallest norm. If $A$ has full column rank, $A^+ = (A^*A)^{-1}A^*$; if $A$ has full row rank, $A^+ = A^*(AA^*)^{-1}$; if $A$ is invertible, $A^+ = A^{-1}$.

Proof

Existence. By the fundamental theorem of linear algebra (chapter 1, with $A^*$ in place of $A^\T$), $A$ maps $\Range(A^*)$ one-to-one onto $\Range(A)$, $\Null(A) = \Range(A^*)^\perp$ and $\Null(A^*) = \Range(A)^\perp$. For $y \in \F^m$ let $Xy$ be the unique $x \in \Range(A^*)$ with $Ax = P_{\Range(A)}y$. Then $X$ is linear and $AX = P_{\Range(A)}$. For $x = x_r + x_n$ with $x_r \in \Range(A^*)$ and $x_n \in \Null(A)$ we have $Ax = Ax_r$, hence $XAx = x_r$, that is, $XA = P_{\Range(A^*)}$. Both products are orthogonal projectors, hence Hermitian (), which gives the last two conditions; and $AXA = P_{\Range(A)}A = A$, $XAX = P_{\Range(A^*)}X = X$ because $X$ maps into $\Range(A^*)$. By construction $Xy = 0$ for $y \in \Null(A^*) = \Range(A)^\perp$, and $XAx = x$ for $x \in \Range(A^*)$, so $X$ maps $\Range(A)$ onto $\Range(A^*)$, one-to-one because $AX$ is the identity on $\Range(A)$.

Uniqueness. If $X$ and $Y$ both satisfy the conditions, then

$$X = XAX = X(AX)^* = XX^*A^* = XX^*A^*Y^*A^* = X(AX)(AY) = XAY,$$ $$Y = YAY = (YA)^*Y = A^*Y^*Y = A^*X^*A^*Y^*Y = (XA)(YA)Y = XAY,$$

using $A^* = (AYA)^* = A^*Y^*A^*$ in the first line and $A^* = A^*X^*A^*$ in the second. So $X = Y$.

Minimum norm. By the minimizers of $\norm{Ax - b}$ are the solutions of $Ax = P_{\Range(A)}b = AA^+b$, which form the set $A^+b + \Null(A)$. Since $A^+b \in \Range(A^*) = \Null(A)^\perp$, Pythagoras gives $\norm{A^+b + z}^2 = \norm{A^+b}^2 + \norm{z}^2$, minimal only at $z = 0$. The three special formulas satisfy the four conditions by direct substitution, and uniqueness identifies each with $A^+$.

The conditions are from R. Penrose, "A generalized inverse for matrices", Proc. Cambridge Philos. Soc. 51 (1955) 406–413. In practice $A^+$ is computed from the SVD: if $A = U\Sigma V^*$ then $A^+ = V\Sigma^+U^*$, where $\Sigma^+$ is the transpose of $\Sigma$ with the nonzero singular values inverted (the pseudoinverse from the SVD, chapter 4).

Applied row by row, the theorem also solves least-squares problems whose unknown is a matrix.

Let $X \in \F^{n\times k}$ and $Y \in \F^{p\times k}$. The matrices $M \in \F^{p\times n}$ that minimize the Frobenius norm $\norm{MX - Y}_F$ are exactly the matrices $M = YX^+ + Z$ with $ZX = 0$, and $YX^+$ is the unique minimizer of smallest Frobenius norm.

Proof. Write $m_j^\T$ and $y_j^\T$ for row $j$ of $M$ and of $Y$. Row $j$ of $MX - Y$ is $m_j^\T X - y_j^\T$, so $\norm{MX - Y}_F^2 = \sum_j\norm{X^\T m_j - y_j}^2$ splits into $p$ separate least-squares problems with matrix $X^\T$, while $\norm{M}_F^2 = \sum_j\norm{m_j}^2$. By the minimizers of the $j$-th problem are the vectors $(X^\T)^+y_j + w$ with $X^\T w = 0$, and $(X^\T)^+y_j$ alone has the smallest norm. Transposing the four Penrose conditions shows $(X^\T)^+ = (X^+)^\T$, so the minimizers are $m_j^\T = y_j^\T X^+ + z_j^\T$ with $z_j^\T X = 0$, that is, $M = YX^+ + Z$ with $ZX = 0$, and the norm is smallest exactly for $Z = 0$.

Two familiar rules fail for $A^+$. (i) Continuity: $A_\eps = \diag(1, \eps)$ has $A_\eps^+ = \diag(1, 1/\eps)$ for $\eps \ne 0$ but $A_0^+ = \diag(1, 0)$, so $A_\eps^+$ blows up instead of converging as $\eps \to 0$. Here the rank drops in the limit, and shows that this is the only way continuity can fail, which is why numerical rank decisions (numerical rank, chapter 4) and regularization matter. (ii) Products: with $A = \begin{bmatrix}1 & 1\end{bmatrix}$ and $B = \begin{bmatrix}1\\ 0\end{bmatrix}$, $(AB)^+ = 1^+ = 1$, but $B^+A^+ = \begin{bmatrix}1 & 0\end{bmatrix}\tfrac12\begin{bmatrix}1\\ 1\end{bmatrix} = \tfrac12$.

Let $A_k \to A$ in $\F^{m\times n}$. Then $A_k^+ \to A^+$ if and only if $\rank A_k = \rank A$ for all sufficiently large $k$. If $\rank A_k \ne \rank A$ for infinitely many $k$, then $\norm{A_k^+}$ is unbounded.

Idea: let $r = \rank A$. A nonzero $r\times r$ minor of $A$ stays nonzero near $A$, so $\rank A_k \ge r$ for large $k$. For a matrix $B$ of rank $s \ge 1$ the SVD formula gives $\norm{B^+} = 1/\sigma_s(B)$, the reciprocal of the smallest nonzero singular value, and singular values are $1$-Lipschitz functions of the matrix (chapter 4). So $\rank A_k > r$ gives $\norm{A_k^+} \ge 1/\sigma_{r+1}(A_k) \ge 1/\norm{A_k - A}$. If the ranks agree, the $r$ nonzero singular values of $A_k$ stay away from $0$, and $A_k^+ \to A^+$. Proof: G. W. Stewart, "On the continuity of the generalized inverse", SIAM J. Appl. Math. 17 (1969) 33–45; Golub and Van Loan, ch. 5.

Let $A \in \R^{m\times n}$ be arbitrary and let $\mu > 0$ be a regularization parameter. The function $\norm{Ax - b}^2 + \mu\norm{x}^2$ has the unique minimizer

$$x_\mu = (A^\T A + \mu I)^{-1}A^\T b = A^\T(AA^\T + \mu I)^{-1}b,$$

and $x_\mu \to A^+b$ as $\mu \to 0^+$.

Proof. The objective is $\norm{\tilde Ax - \tilde b}^2$ with $\tilde A = \begin{bmatrix}A\\ \sqrt\mu\, I\end{bmatrix}$ and $\tilde b = \begin{bmatrix}b\\ 0\end{bmatrix}$. $\tilde A$ has full column rank, so by the minimizer is unique and solves $\tilde A^\T\tilde Ax = \tilde A^\T\tilde b$, which is $(A^\T A + \mu I)x = A^\T b$. The second form follows from $(A^\T A + \mu I)A^\T = A^\T(AA^\T + \mu I)$, both inverses existing because $x^\T(A^\T A + \mu I)x \ge \mu\norm{x}^2$. For the limit, write the SVD of chapter 4 as $A = \sum_i \sigma_i u_i v_i^\T$, summed over the singular values $\sigma_i > 0$, with orthonormal singular vectors $u_i$ and $v_i$. Then $A^\T b = \sum_i\sigma_i(u_i^\T b)\,v_i$, and $A^\T A + \mu I$ multiplies each $v_i$ by $\sigma_i^2 + \mu$; with $A^+b = \sum_i\sigma_i^{-1}(u_i^\T b)\,v_i$ from the pseudoinverse from the SVD (chapter 4),

$$x_\mu = \sum_i \frac{\sigma_i}{\sigma_i^2 + \mu}\,(u_i^\T b)\,v_i \;\longrightarrow\; \sum_i \frac{1}{\sigma_i}\,(u_i^\T b)\,v_i = A^+b \qquad (\mu \to 0^+).$$

Let $A \in \R^{m\times n}$, $b \in \R^m$, and let $x_\mu$ be the ridge solution of . Proof. In the notation of the proof of , let $\beta_i = u_i^\T b$ and $b_\perp = b - \sum_i\beta_iu_i$, which is orthogonal to every $u_i$. Then $b - Ax_\mu = \sum_i\frac{\mu}{\sigma_i^2 + \mu}\beta_iu_i + b_\perp$, and Pythagoras gives

$$\norm{x_\mu}^2 = \sum_i\Big(\frac{\sigma_i}{\sigma_i^2 + \mu}\Big)^2\beta_i^2,$$ $$\norm{Ax_\mu - b}^2 = \sum_i\Big(\frac{\mu}{\sigma_i^2 + \mu}\Big)^2\beta_i^2 + \norm{b_\perp}^2.$$

As $\mu$ grows, each coefficient of the first sum decreases to $0$ and each of the second increases, which is (ii). Since $\sigma_i^2 + \mu \ge 2\sigma_i\sqrt\mu$, the coefficients of the first sum are at most $1/(4\mu)$, and $\sum_i\beta_i^2 \le \norm{b}^2$, which gives (i) for the linear map $b \mapsto x_\mu$. The parameter $\mu$ trades fit against size, and it turns an ill-posed problem into a well-posed one: $x_\mu$ is unique and depends continuously on $A$ and $b$ (), whereas $A^+$ is not continuous across a change of rank (). Linear decoders that read out behaviour from the activity of many correlated neurons are often fitted this way. Heuristically, correlated activity makes $A^\T A$ nearly singular; whatever $A$ is, the penalty keeps the norm of the decoder weights below $\norm{b}/(2\sqrt\mu)$. The norm changes the answer

Replace the Euclidean norm by $\norm{\cdot}_1$ or $\norm{\cdot}_\infty$ in both problems: the smallest point of an affine set, and the best fit to data. Which conclusions of the previous sections survive?

$\R^n$ with a $p$-norm, $1 \le p \le \infty$, and the dual exponent $q$ of , $1/p + 1/q = 1$. The main tool is Hölder's inequality $\abs{a^\T x} \le \norm{a}_q\norm{x}_p$ from the same proposition, which generalizes Cauchy–Schwarz ($p = q = 2$).

The simplest affine set is a line $a^\T x = c$ in $\R^2$, with $a = (a_1, a_2)^\T \ne 0$ and $c \ne 0$. Grow the ball $\{x : \norm{x}_p \le t\}$ from $t = 0$ until it first touches the line; the touching point is the minimum-norm point. For $p = 2$ the disk touches at the foot of the perpendicular, $x = c\,a/\norm{a}_2^2$. For $p = \infty$ and $a_1a_2 \ne 0$ the square touches with a corner, at $x = c\,(\operatorname{sign}a_1, \operatorname{sign}a_2)^\T/\norm{a}_1$, where both coordinates have the same magnitude and $\operatorname{sign}$ is $+1$, $-1$ or $0$. For $p = 1$ and $\abs{a_1} \ne \abs{a_2}$ the diamond touches with a vertex on an axis, at $x = (c/a_k)\,e_k$ for the index $k$ with the larger $\abs{a_k}$: the minimizer is sparse, with a zero entry. (When $\abs{a_1} = \abs{a_2}$ a whole edge touches.)

Let $a \in \R^n$ be nonzero, $c \in \R$ and $1 \le p \le \infty$. Then

$$\min\{\norm{x}_p : a^\T x = c\} = \frac{\abs{c}}{\norm{a}_q}.$$

For $1 < p < \infty$ the minimizer is unique, with entries $x^\star_i = \dfrac{c}{\norm{a}_q^q}\operatorname{sign}(a_i)\abs{a_i}^{q-1}$. For $p = 1$ the minimizers include $(c/a_k)\,e_k$ for every $k$ with $\abs{a_k} = \norm{a}_\infty$; for $p = \infty$ they include $c\operatorname{sign}(a)/\norm{a}_1$, with $\operatorname{sign}$ applied entrywise.

Proof. For every feasible $x$, Hölder gives $\abs{c} = \abs{a^\T x} \le \norm{a}_q\norm{x}_p$, so no feasible point has norm below $\abs{c}/\norm{a}_q$. The proposed points are feasible and attain the bound. For $1 < p < \infty$: $a^\T x^\star = \frac{c}{\norm{a}_q^q}\sum_i \abs{a_i}^q = c$, and since $(q - 1)p = q$, $\norm{x^\star}_p = \frac{\abs{c}}{\norm{a}_q^q}\norm{a}_q^{q/p} = \abs{c}/\norm{a}_q$ (use $q/p - q = -1$). The cases $p = 1$ and $p = \infty$ are checked directly. Uniqueness for $1 < p < \infty$: the feasible set is convex and $x \mapsto \norm{x}_p^p$ is strictly convex, so the midpoint of two different minimizers would do strictly better.

In the plane the uniqueness question has a complete answer.

Let $a = (a_1, a_2)^\T \in \R^2$ be nonzero and $c \ne 0$, and consider the minimum-norm points of the line $a^\T x = c$. (i) For $\norm{\cdot}_1$ the minimizer is unique if and only if $\abs{a_1} \ne \abs{a_2}$, and it is then $(c/a_k)\,e_k$ with $\abs{a_k} = \norm{a}_\infty$, which has a zero entry. If $\abs{a_1} = \abs{a_2}$, the minimizers are the points of the segment from $(c/a_1, 0)$ to $(0, c/a_2)$. (ii) For $\norm{\cdot}_\infty$ the minimizer is unique if and only if $a_1a_2 \ne 0$. If $a_2 = 0$, the minimizers are the points $(c/a_1, s)$ with $\abs{s} \le \abs{c/a_1}$, and symmetrically if $a_1 = 0$.

Proof. Let $(p, q)$ be $(1, \infty)$ or $(\infty, 1)$. Every feasible $x$ satisfies

$$\abs{c} = \abs{a_1x_1 + a_2x_2} \le \abs{a_1}\abs{x_1} + \abs{a_2}\abs{x_2} \le \norm{a}_q\norm{x}_p,$$

so by it is a minimizer exactly when both inequalities are equalities. The first is an equality when each nonzero product $a_ix_i$ has the sign of $c$. (i) For $p = 1$ the second is an equality when $x_i = 0$ for each $i$ with $\abs{a_i} < \norm{a}_\infty$. If $\abs{a_1} \ne \abs{a_2}$ this forces one zero entry, and $a^\T x = c$ fixes the other. If $\abs{a_1} = \abs{a_2}$, only the sign condition remains: $a_1x_1 = \lambda c$ and $a_2x_2 = (1 - \lambda)c$ with $\lambda \in [0, 1]$, which is the segment. (ii) For $p = \infty$ the second is an equality when $\abs{x_i} = \norm{x}_\infty$ for each $i$ with $a_i \ne 0$. If $a_1a_2 \ne 0$, both entries have magnitude $\abs{c}/\norm{a}_1$ and signs fixed by the first condition, so $x = c\operatorname{sign}(a)/\norm{a}_1$. If $a_2 = 0$, the conditions reduce to $x_1 = c/a_1$ and $\abs{x_2} \le \abs{x_1}$.

The $\ell_1$ minimizer does not. Rotate the line $x_2 = 1$ clockwise by $30^\circ$; it becomes $\tfrac12 x_1 + \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}x_2 = 1$. The Euclidean norm is invariant under every orthogonal change of coordinates, and so are its minimizers (). The $\ell_1$ and $\ell_\infty$ norms are invariant only under permutations of the coordinates combined with sign changes:

A signed permutation matrix is a matrix $T \in \R^{n\times n}$ with $Te_k = \pm e_{\pi(k)}$ for every $k$, for some permutation $\pi$ of $\{1, \dots, n\}$; it preserves every $p$-norm. Conversely, if $T \in \R^{n\times n}$ satisfies $\norm{Tx}_1 = \norm{x}_1$ for all $x \in \R^n$, or $\norm{Tx}_\infty = \norm{x}_\infty$ for all $x \in \R^n$, then $T$ is a signed permutation matrix.

Proof

A signed permutation permutes the entries of $x$ and changes some of their signs, which leaves $\sum_i\abs{x_i}^p$ and $\max_i\abs{x_i}$ unchanged. Write $t_{ik}$ for the entries of $T$. Let $T$ preserve $\norm{\cdot}_1$. Then $\norm{Te_k}_1 = 1$, and for $j \ne k$ and either sign, $\norm{T(e_j \pm e_k)}_1 = 2 = \norm{Te_j}_1 + \norm{Te_k}_1$. Since $\abs{t_{ij} \pm t_{ik}} \le \abs{t_{ij}} + \abs{t_{ik}}$ for every $i$, equality for both signs forces $t_{ij}t_{ik} = 0$: the $n$ columns, all nonzero, have pairwise disjoint supports in $\{1, \dots, n\}$. So each column has a single nonzero entry, in a different row for each column, and $Te_k = \pm e_{\pi(k)}$ for a permutation $\pi$.

Now let $T$ preserve $\norm{\cdot}_\infty$, and let $\tau_i^\T$ be its $i$-th row. For the vector $x$ with entries $x_k = \operatorname{sign}(t_{ik})$, $\norm{\tau_i}_1 = \tau_i^\T x \le \norm{Tx}_\infty = \norm{x}_\infty \le 1$. For each $k$, $\norm{Te_k}_\infty = 1$, so some row has an entry $\pm1$ in column $k$, and by the bound just shown that row is $\pm e_k^\T$. Different $k$ give different rows, so the $n$ rows are $\pm e_1^\T, \dots, \pm e_n^\T$ in some order, and $T$ is a signed permutation matrix.

Sparsity is therefore a statement about a chosen basis. That is its cost and its use: in a basis where a signal is known to be sparse, $\ell_1$ minimization can find it, under conditions recalled at the end of this section.

For $A \in \R^{m\times n}$ with rows $a_i^\T$ and $b \in \R^m$, the $\ell_1$ fit (least absolute deviations) minimizes $\norm{Ax - b}_1 = \sum_i \abs{a_i^\T x - b_i}$, and the Chebyshev or minimax fit minimizes $\norm{Ax - b}_\infty = \max_i\abs{a_i^\T x - b_i}$.

Let $b_1, \dots, b_m \in \R$ and $x \in \R$. (i) $\sum_i(x - b_i)^2$ is minimized exactly at the mean $\bar b = \frac1m\sum_i b_i$. (ii) $\sum_i\abs{x - b_i}$ is minimized exactly at the medians, the $x$ with at most $m/2$ of the $b_i$ below $x$ and at most $m/2$ above $x$; they form a closed interval whose endpoints are data points, a single data point when $m$ is odd. (iii) $\max_i\abs{x - b_i}$ is minimized exactly at the midrange $\frac12(\max_i b_i + \min_i b_i)$. (iv) Let $m \ge 3$, and let $b_j$ be the largest or the smallest of the data. Then every median lies between the smallest and the largest of the other $m - 1$ points, whatever the value of $b_j$, while the mean and the midrange tend to $\pm\infty$ with $b_j$.

Proof. (i) $\sum_i(x - b_i)^2 = m(x - \bar b)^2 + \sum_i(b_i - \bar b)^2$. (ii) $f(x) = \sum_i\abs{x - b_i}$ is convex, so its local minimizers are global. Let $n_{<}$ count the $b_i < x$, and define $n_{\le}$, $n_{>}$, $n_{\ge}$ likewise. For small $h > 0$, $f(x + h) - f(x) = h\,(n_{\le} - n_{>})$ and $f(x - h) - f(x) = h\,(n_{\ge} - n_{<})$, so $x$ is a minimizer exactly when $n_{>} \le n_{\le}$ and $n_{<} \le n_{\ge}$, that is, $n_{>} \le m/2$ and $n_{<} \le m/2$. With $b_{(1)} \le \dots \le b_{(m)}$ the sorted data, this set is the interval $[b_{(\lceil m/2\rceil)}, b_{(\lfloor m/2\rfloor + 1)}]$. (iii) The farthest data point from $x$ is the largest or the smallest one, so the cost is $\max(x - \min_i b_i,\ \max_i b_i - x)$, which decreases until the two terms agree, at the midrange, and increases afterwards. (iv) Say $b_j$ is the largest, and let $\ell \le u$ be the smallest and the largest of the other points. A point $x > u$ has the $m - 1 > m/2$ other points below it, and a point $x < \ell$ has all $m$ points above it, so neither is a median. The mean is $b_j/m$ plus a quantity independent of $b_j$, and the midrange is $(b_j + \ell)/2$. The case of the smallest point is symmetric.

If $J$ had more than $m$ elements, the columns of $A$ indexed by $J$ would be dependent, so some $d \ne 0$ with $d_i = 0$ for $i \notin J$ would satisfy $Ad = 0$. For small $\abs{s}$ no entry of $x_0 + sd$ changes sign, so $\norm{x_0 + sd}_1$ is affine in $s$, hence constant because it is minimal at $s = 0$; then $x_0 \pm sd \in K$, contradicting extremality.

Part 1, $\ell_\infty$. If $t = 0$ then $x_0 = 0$ and every entry qualifies. Otherwise let $I$ be the set of $i$ with $\abs{x_{0,i}} = t$, and suppose $I$ had at most $n - m$ elements. The $m + \abs{I}$ homogeneous linear conditions $Ad = 0$ and $d_i = \operatorname{sign}(x_{0,i})\,\tau$ for $i \in I$, on $(d, \tau) \in \R^n\times\R$, would have a nonzero solution, with $d \ne 0$ because $I$ is nonempty. For small $\abs{s}$, $\norm{x_0 + sd}_\infty = t + s\tau$, so minimality forces $\tau = 0$, and then $x_0 \pm sd \in K$ contradicts extremality.

Part 2. Here $t > 0$ because $b \notin \Range(A)$. Repeat the previous argument with the residual $\rho = Ax_0 - b$ in place of $x_0$: if at most $n$ indices $i$ had $\abs{\rho_i} = t$, the conditions $a_i^\T d = \operatorname{sign}(\rho_i)\,\tau$ for these $i$ would have a solution with $d \ne 0$, along which $\norm{A(x_0 + sd) - b}_\infty = t + s\tau$ for small $\abs{s}$, and $x_0 \pm sd$ would again contradict extremality.

Part 3. For fixed $x$ the smallest feasible bound variables are $s_i = \abs{(Ax - b)_i}$ and $t = \norm{x}_\infty$ (likewise in the two problems not displayed), so each reformulation has the same minimum value and the same minimizers $x$ as the original problem.

Compressed sensing makes the preference of $\ell_1$ for sparse answers quantitative: under conditions on $A$ that suitable random matrices satisfy with high probability, the minimum-$\ell_1$ solution of an underdetermined system recovers a sufficiently sparse vector exactly (E. J. Candès, J. Romberg and T. Tao, IEEE Trans. Inf. Theory 52, 2006; D. L. Donoho, IEEE Trans. Inf. Theory 52, 2006). Adding a penalty $\lambda\norm{x}_1$ with weight $\lambda > 0$ to a least-squares cost gives the lasso (R. Tibshirani, J. R. Stat. Soc. B 58, 1996), and the same preference for few active causes underlies sparse coding models of visual receptive fields (B. A. Olshausen and D. J. Field, Nature 381, 1996).

  1. Project $b = (1, 2, 3)^\T$ onto $\mathcal S = \spn\big((1, 1, 1)^\T\big)$. Give the residual and check Pythagoras.

    Answer

    $p = \frac{6}{3}(1, 1, 1)^\T = (2, 2, 2)^\T$, the mean in every entry, and $r = (-1, 0, 1)^\T \perp (1, 1, 1)^\T$. Pythagoras: $\norm{b}^2 = 14 = 12 + 2 = \norm{p}^2 + \norm{r}^2$.

  2. For a unit vector $u \in \R^n$, is $H = I - 2uu^\T$ a projector? What is $\tfrac12(I + H)$?

    Answer

    $H^2 = I - 4uu^\T + 4u(u^\T u)u^\T = I \ne H$, so $H$ is not a projector; it is the reflection across $u^\perp$ (a Householder reflector, the building block of QR). $\tfrac12(I + H) = I - uu^\T$ is symmetric and idempotent, the orthogonal projector onto $u^\perp$.

  3. If $A$ has orthonormal columns, what are $A^+$, the least-squares solution and the fitted vector?

    Answer

    $A^\T A = I$, so by $A^+ = (A^\T A)^{-1}A^\T = A^\T$, $\hat x = A^\T b$ (the coefficients of $b$ in the orthonormal basis), and $A\hat x = AA^\T b = P_{\Range(A)}b$ by .

  4. Show that if $P^2 = P$ then $I - P$ is a projector with range $\Null(P)$ and null space $\Range(P)$. When is it an orthogonal projector?

    Answer

    $(I - P)^2 = I - 2P + P^2 = I - P$. $(I - P)b \in \Null(P)$ because $P(I - P) = 0$, and $(I - P)z = z$ for $z \in \Null(P)$, so the range is $\Null(P)$. $(I - P)b = 0$ means $b = Pb$, that is, $b \in \Range(P)$. By it is orthogonal exactly when $(I - P)^* = I - P$, that is, when $P^* = P$.

  5. For which lines $a^\T x = c$ in $\R^2$ with $c \ne 0$ is the minimum-$\ell_1$-norm point not unique, and what is the set of minimizers?

    Answer

    When $\abs{a_1} = \abs{a_2}$: the line is parallel to an edge of the diamond, and the minimizers are the segment between $(c/a_1, 0)$ and $(0, c/a_2)$, all with $\ell_1$ norm $\abs{c}/\abs{a_1} = \abs{c}/\norm{a}_\infty$, as and predict.
