Autonomous linear systems: flows, modes and stability

The system $\dot x = Ax$, in which a fixed matrix $A$ drives the state $x(t)$ and $\dot x$ is its time derivative, is the simplest dynamical system with structure: its solutions are built from exponentials of the eigenvalues along eigenvectors and generalized eigenvectors, so the eigenvalues and the Jordan structure decide what happens as time grows. This chapter constructs the matrix exponential, reads modes and phase portraits off the spectrum, and classifies planar flows up to linear changes of coordinates and hyperbolic flows up to continuous ones. It then certifies stability with quadratic Lyapunov functions, which keep working where eigenvalues stop being informative.

Linear flows and linear maps

Given a real matrix $A \in \R^{n\times n}$ and an initial state $x_0 \in \R^n$, describe every solution of the linear differential equation and of the linear recursion below, and decide what the solutions do as time grows.

Two settings, continuous time (CT) and discrete time (DT):

$$\text{CT:}\quad \dot x(t) = Ax(t),\ \ x(0) = x_0,\ \ t \in \R;\qquad\qquad \text{DT:}\quad x_{k+1} = Ax_k,\ \ k = 0, 1, 2, \dots$$

Both are autonomous (no input) and linear time-invariant (LTI): $A$ does not depend on time. The DT solution is $x_k = A^kx_0$. Inputs enter in chapter 8; time-varying matrices in .

If $x(t)$ and $y(t)$ solve $\dot x = Ax$, so does $ax(t) + by(t)$ for all scalars $a, b$: the solutions form a vector space, and the map $x_0 \mapsto x(t)$ is linear for each $t$. The same holds in DT.

Proof. Differentiation is linear: $\frac{\dd}{\dd t}(ax + by) = aAx + bAy = A(ax + by)$. Linearity of $x_0 \mapsto x(t)$ then follows from uniqueness (): the solution from $ax_0 + by_0$ must be $ax(t) + by(t)$.

The scalar case already separates CT from DT. $\dot x = ax$ has $x(t) = e^{at}x_0$, which decays ($a < 0$), stays put ($a = 0$) or grows ($a > 0$), and, for $x_0 \neq 0$, never changes sign or reaches 0 in finite time. $x_{k+1} = ax_k$ has $x_k = a^kx_0$, which can also alternate in sign ($a < 0$) or die in one step ($a = 0$).

Not every DT system is a sampled CT system. The scalar recursion $x_{k+1} = -x_k$ is not $x(kh)$ for any real $\dot x = ax$ and step $h > 0$, since $e^{ah} > 0$. In the plane it is: $-I = e^{hA}$ for $A = \frac{\pi}{h}\begin{bmatrix}0 & -1\\ 1 & 0\end{bmatrix}$, a rotation through $\pi$ in time $h$. And $A = 0$ sends every state to 0 in one step, whereas every flow map $e^{tA}$ is invertible ().

The matrix exponential

For $A \in \C^{n\times n}$, $e^{A} = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \dfrac{A^k}{k!}$. The series converges for every $A$ (), so $t \mapsto e^{tA}$ is defined for all real $t$.

Let $A \in \C^{n\times n}$, and let $\norm{A} = \max_{\norm{x} = 1}\norm{Ax}$ be the spectral norm, induced by the Euclidean norm $\norm{x}$. The series $\sum_{k\ge0}A^k/k!$ converges absolutely, with $\norm{e^{A}} \le e^{\norm{A}}$, and the series $\sum_{k\ge0}t^kA^k/k!$ converges uniformly for $t$ in every bounded interval.

Proof. The spectral norm is submultiplicative with $\norm{I} = 1$, so $\norm{A^k}/k! \le \norm{A}^k/k!$ and $\sum_k\norm{A^k}/k! \le e^{\norm{A}}$. An absolutely convergent series in the finite-dimensional space $\C^{n\times n}$ converges, and its sum obeys the same bound. For $\abs t \le T$ the terms $t^kA^k/k!$ have norms at most $T^k\norm{A}^k/k!$, the terms of a convergent series of constants, so the convergence is uniform (Weierstrass M-test).

For $A, B \in \C^{n\times n}$ and $s, t \in \R$:

  1. $\dfrac{\dd}{\dd t}e^{tA} = Ae^{tA} = e^{tA}A$;
  2. $e^{(s+t)A} = e^{sA}e^{tA}$ and $e^{0A} = I$, so $e^{tA}$ is invertible with inverse $e^{-tA}$;
  3. if $AB = BA$, then $e^{A+B} = e^Ae^B$;
  4. $e^{S^{-1}AS} = S^{-1}e^{A}S$ for invertible $S$;
  5. $\det e^{A} = e^{\tr A}$, where $\tr A$ is the trace (the sum of the diagonal entries), and the eigenvalues of $e^{A}$ are $e^{\lambda}$ for the eigenvalues $\lambda$ of $A$, with multiplicity.

Proof. (1) By the series converges uniformly for $t$ in bounded intervals, and so does the series of termwise derivatives, $\sum_{k\ge1}t^{k-1}A^k/(k-1)! = Ae^{tA}$; hence the series may be differentiated term by term, and $A$ commutes with every partial sum. (3) If $AB = BA$ the binomial theorem $(A + B)^m = \sum_k\binom{m}{k}A^kB^{m-k}$ holds, and the Cauchy product of the two absolutely convergent series is $e^Ae^B = \sum_m \frac{1}{m!}\sum_k\binom{m}{k}A^kB^{m-k} = e^{A+B}$. (2) is (3) for the commuting pair $sA$, $tA$. (4) $(S^{-1}AS)^k = S^{-1}A^kS$ term by term. (5) With a Schur form $A = UTU^*$ ($U$ unitary, $T$ upper triangular; Schur's theorem, chapter 3), $e^{A} = Ue^{T}U^*$ and $e^T$ is upper triangular with diagonal entries $e^{t_{ii}}$, where the $t_{ii} = \lambda_i$ are the eigenvalues of $A$; so the eigenvalues of $e^A$ are $e^{\lambda_i}$ and its determinant is $\prod_i e^{\lambda_i} = e^{\tr A}$.

For every $x_0 \in \C^n$ the problem $\dot x = Ax$, $x(0) = x_0$ has exactly one differentiable solution on $\R$, namely $x(t) = e^{tA}x_0$.

Proof. By part 1 of , $e^{tA}x_0$ is a solution. If $x(t)$ is any solution, then $\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\big(e^{-tA}x(t)\big) = -Ae^{-tA}x + e^{-tA}Ax = 0$, because $A$ commutes with $e^{-tA}$; hence $e^{-tA}x(t) = x_0$ for all $t$.

Without commutation the product rule fails. For $A = \begin{bmatrix}0 & 1\\ 0 & 0\end{bmatrix}$ and $B = \begin{bmatrix}0 & 0\\ 1 & 0\end{bmatrix}$ the series stop after two terms, so $e^A = \begin{bmatrix}1 & 1\\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}$ and $e^B = \begin{bmatrix}1 & 0\\ 1 & 1\end{bmatrix}$. Then $e^Ae^B = \begin{bmatrix}2 & 1\\ 1 & 1\end{bmatrix}$ and $e^Be^A = \begin{bmatrix}1 & 1\\ 1 & 2\end{bmatrix}$, while $(A + B)^2 = I$ gives $e^{A+B} = \cosh(1)\,I + \sinh(1)(A + B)$, approximately $\begin{bmatrix}1.543 & 1.175\\ 1.175 & 1.543\end{bmatrix}$. All three are different, although their determinants agree: by part 5 of and $\det(XY) = \det X\det Y$, each equals $e^{\tr A + \tr B} = 1$.

Liouville's formula gives the similarity invariant $\tr A$ a geometric meaning, as the exponential rate at which the flow changes volume (). Volume contraction ($\tr A < 0$) does not imply stability: the saddle $\diag(1, -2)$ has trace $-1$, so it contracts volume, and its eigenvalue $1$ makes it unstable.

Let $A \in \R^{n\times n}$ and $t \in \R$. Then $\det e^{tA} = e^{t\tr A} > 0$, so the flow map $e^{tA}$ preserves orientation and multiplies the $n$-dimensional volume of every region by $e^{t\tr A}$. Here $\tr A = \sum_i\partial(Ax)_i/\partial x_i$ is the divergence of the vector field $x \mapsto Ax$.

Proof. Part 5 of applied to $tA$ gives $\det e^{tA} = e^{\tr(tA)} = e^{t\tr A}$. A real matrix multiplies volumes by the absolute value of its determinant, and preserves orientation when the determinant is positive (the determinant theorem, chapter 1). Finally $\partial(Ax)_i/\partial x_i = a_{ii}$.

Let $J_k(\lambda) = \lambda I + N$, where $N = J_k(0)$ has ones on the superdiagonal and $N^k = 0$. Then

$$e^{tJ_k(\lambda)} = e^{\lambda t}\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\frac{t^j}{j!}N^j = e^{\lambda t}\begin{bmatrix}1 & t & \frac{t^2}{2} & \cdots & \frac{t^{k-1}}{(k-1)!}\\ & 1 & t & \ddots & \vdots\\ & & \ddots & \ddots & \frac{t^2}{2}\\ & & & 1 & t\\ & & & & 1\end{bmatrix}.$$

Proof. $\lambda I$ commutes with $N$, so $e^{tJ_k(\lambda)} = e^{\lambda t}e^{tN}$ by , and the series for $e^{tN}$ stops at $j = k - 1$; $N^j$ has ones on the $j$-th superdiagonal.

For complex $s$ with $\Re s > \alpha(A)$, where $\alpha(A) = \max_i\Re\lambda_i$ is the spectral abscissa,

$$\mathcal L\{e^{tA}\}(s) = \int_0^\infty e^{-st}e^{tA}\,\dd t = (sI - A)^{-1},\qquad\text{so}\qquad e^{tA} = \mathcal L^{-1}\big\{(sI - A)^{-1}\big\}\ \text{entrywise}.$$

Here $\mathcal L^{-1}$ is the inverse Laplace transform.

Proof. Put $M = A - sI$, which is invertible with all eigenvalues in the open left half-plane; since $sI$ commutes with $A$, part 3 of gives $e^{-st}e^{tA} = e^{tM}$. Then $\int_0^T e^{tM}\dd t = M^{-1}(e^{TM} - I)$ by part 1, and $e^{TM} \to 0$ as $T \to \infty$: in a Jordan basis its entries are $t^je^{\mu t}/j!$ with $\Re\mu < 0$ (). The limit is $-M^{-1} = (sI - A)^{-1}$. The inversion formula holds because a continuous function on $[0, \infty)$ is determined by its Laplace transform (Lerch's theorem).

For the oscillator $A = \begin{bmatrix}0 & 1\\ -1 & 0\end{bmatrix}$, $(sI - A)^{-1} = \frac{1}{s^2+1}\begin{bmatrix}s & 1\\ -1 & s\end{bmatrix}$, and inverting entrywise gives $e^{tA} = \begin{bmatrix}\cos t & \sin t\\ -\sin t & \cos t\end{bmatrix}$, a clockwise rotation. For $A = \begin{bmatrix}-1 & w\\ 0 & -2\end{bmatrix}$ (chapter 6) use interpolation () instead: $e^{tA} = c_0(t)I + c_1(t)A$ with $c_0 - c_1 = e^{-t}$ and $c_0 - 2c_1 = e^{-2t}$ (evaluate at the eigenvalues), so $c_1 = e^{-t} - e^{-2t}$ and $e^{tA} = \begin{bmatrix}e^{-t} & w(e^{-t} - e^{-2t})\\ 0 & e^{-2t}\end{bmatrix}$.

For every $t$ there are scalars $c_0(t), \dots, c_{n-1}(t)$ with $e^{tA} = \sum_{k=0}^{n-1}c_k(t)A^k$. If the eigenvalues $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n$ are distinct, the $c_k(t)$ solve the Vandermonde system $\sum_k c_k(t)\lambda_i^k = e^{\lambda_i t}$.

Proof. By the Cayley–Hamilton theorem and its corollary on powers (chapter 3), every power $A^k$ is a linear combination of $I, A, \dots, A^{n-1}$. The partial sums of the series therefore lie in the span of these $n$ matrices, a finite-dimensional and hence closed subspace, which then contains the limit. Applying both sides to an eigenvector $v_i$ gives $e^{\lambda_it}v_i = \sum_kc_k(t)\lambda_i^kv_i$.

Modes and invariant subspaces

Decompose a solution into pieces that evolve independently, each at its own rate, and identify the initial states that decay, grow or neither.

Let $A \in \C^{n\times n}$ be diagonalizable, $A = V\Lambda V^{-1}$, with $\Lambda = \diag(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n)$ and right eigenvectors $v_1, \dots, v_n$ as the columns of $V$. Write the rows of $V^{-1}$ as $w_1^*, \dots, w_n^*$, where $w^*$ is the conjugate transpose, and call $\xi = V^{-1}x$, with entries $\xi_i = w_i^*x$, the modal coordinates.

Under these assumptions the $w_i$ are left eigenvectors, $w_i^*A = \lambda_iw_i^*$, normalized against the right ones by $w_i^*v_j = \delta_{ij}$ (biorthogonality), where $\delta_{ij}$ is the Kronecker delta. The modal coordinates satisfy $\dot\xi = \Lambda\xi$, and

$$e^{tA} = \sum_{i=1}^n e^{\lambda_it}\,v_iw_i^*,\qquad x(t) = \sum_{i=1}^n \big(w_i^*x_0\big)\,e^{\lambda_it}\,v_i.$$

The matrices $P_i = v_iw_i^*$ satisfy $P_i^2 = P_i$, $P_iP_j = 0$ for $i \neq j$, $\sum_iP_i = I$ and $AP_i = P_iA = \lambda_iP_i$: they are projectors, oblique in general (for distinct eigenvalues, the spectral projectors of $A$).

Proof. Read row by row, $V^{-1}A = \Lambda V^{-1}$ gives $w_i^*A = \lambda_iw_i^*$, and $V^{-1}V = I$, entry by entry, gives $w_i^*v_j = \delta_{ij}$. Then $\dot\xi = V^{-1}Ax = V^{-1}AV\xi = \Lambda\xi$. Next, $e^{tA} = Ve^{t\Lambda}V^{-1}$ by part 4 of , and $e^{t\Lambda} = \diag(e^{\lambda_it})$; expand the product as a sum of rank-one terms. The projector identities are $w_i^*v_j = \delta_{ij}$, $VV^{-1} = \sum_iv_iw_i^* = I$, and $AP_i = (Av_i)w_i^* = \lambda_iP_i = v_i(w_i^*A) = P_iA$. Finally $P_i$ fixes $v_i$ and annihilates the $v_j$, $j \neq i$, which for distinct eigenvalues span the other eigenspaces.

In the modal coordinates the system is $n$ uncoupled scalar equations $\dot\xi_i = \lambda_i\xi_i$ (). The amplitudes $\xi_i(0) = w_i^*x_0$ are computed by the left eigenvectors, not by orthogonal projection onto the $v_i$; the two agree only for orthogonal eigenvectors:

Under the assumptions of , the amplitude $w_i^*x_0$ equals the coefficient $v_i^*x_0/\norm{v_i}^2$ of the orthogonal projection of $x_0$ onto the line spanned by $v_i$, for every $x_0 \in \C^n$ and every $i$, if and only if the eigenvectors $v_1, \dots, v_n$ are pairwise orthogonal. An eigenbasis with this property exists if and only if $A$ is normal, $AA^* = A^*A$; if the eigenvalues are distinct, every eigenbasis of a normal $A$ has it.

Proof. Agreement for every $x_0$ means $w_i = v_i/\norm{v_i}^2$ for every $i$; then $w_i^*v_j = 0$ for $i \neq j$ says $v_i^*v_j = 0$. Conversely, if the $v_i$ are pairwise orthogonal, the matrix with rows $v_i^*/\norm{v_i}^2$ maps each $v_j$ to the $j$-th standard basis vector, so it is a left inverse of $V$, hence equal to $V^{-1}$. An eigenbasis with orthogonal columns is $V = UD$ with $U$ unitary and $D$ positive diagonal, so $A = U\Lambda U^*$, which is normal; conversely a normal matrix has an orthonormal eigenbasis (the normal-matrix theorem, chapter 4). If the eigenvalues are distinct, each eigenspace is a line, so every eigenvector is a multiple of a column of that $U$.

Modal amplitudes can dwarf the state. Take $v_1 = (1, 0)^\T$, $v_2 = (1, \delta)^\T$ with small $\delta > 0$, eigenvalues $-1$ and $-2$, and $x_0 = (0, 1)^\T$. Then $x_0 = (v_2 - v_1)/\delta$: amplitudes $\mp 1/\delta$ for a unit initial state. The modes cancel at $t = 0$, but they decay at different rates, so the cancellation fails at once and $x_1(t) = (e^{-2t} - e^{-t})/\delta$ reaches $-1/(4\delta)$ at $t = \ln 2$. This is the matrix $\begin{bmatrix}-1 & -1/\delta\\ 0 & -2\end{bmatrix}$, the transient-growth example of chapter 6 with $w = -1/\delta$.

Let $A$ be real with an eigenvalue $\lambda = \sigma + i\omega$, $\omega \neq 0$, and eigenvector $v = a + ib$ with $a, b \in \R^n$; here $i$ is the imaginary unit, which engineering texts write $j$. Then $a$ and $b$ are linearly independent, span an $A$-invariant plane, and

$$A\begin{bmatrix}a & b\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}a & b\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\sigma & \omega\\ -\omega & \sigma\end{bmatrix},\qquad e^{t\begin{bmatrix}\sigma & \omega\\ -\omega & \sigma\end{bmatrix}} = e^{\sigma t}\begin{bmatrix}\cos\omega t & \sin\omega t\\ -\sin\omega t & \cos\omega t\end{bmatrix}.$$

In the coordinates of the basis $(a, b)$, solutions in this plane rotate through the angle $\omega t$ while they are scaled by $e^{\sigma t}$.

Proof. Separate real and imaginary parts of $A(a + ib) = (\sigma + i\omega)(a + ib)$: $Aa = \sigma a - \omega b$ and $Ab = \omega a + \sigma b$. If $a$ and $b$ were dependent, $v$ would be a complex multiple of a real vector, which would then be a real eigenvector of the real matrix $A$ for the non-real $\lambda$, impossible. The block equals $\sigma I + \omega K$ with $K = \begin{bmatrix}0 & 1\\ -1 & 0\end{bmatrix}$, $K^2 = -I$; the two terms commute, and $e^{\omega tK} = \cos(\omega t)I + \sin(\omega t)K$ by splitting the series into even and odd powers.

Jordan blocks produce secular terms. For $\dot x = J_2(0)x$, gives $x_1(t) = x_1(0) + t\,x_2(0)$ and $x_2(t) = x_2(0)$: a shear flow in which every point with $x_2(0) \neq 0$ drifts off linearly, although both eigenvalues are 0. For $J_k(\lambda)$ the solutions contain $t^je^{\lambda t}$ with $j < k$. Combining the real form of complex pairs with Jordan blocks gives the real Jordan form $A = SJ_\R S^{-1}$ with real $S$:

Let $A \in \R^{n\times n}$. There is a real invertible $S$ such that $J_\R = S^{-1}AS$ is block diagonal, each block being either a Jordan block $J_k(\lambda)$ for a real eigenvalue $\lambda$ or, for a pair of eigenvalues $\sigma \pm i\omega$ with $\omega > 0$, a real block of size $2k$,

$$\begin{bmatrix}C & I_2 & & \\ & C & \ddots & \\ & & \ddots & I_2\\ & & & C\end{bmatrix},\qquad C = \begin{bmatrix}\sigma & \omega\\ -\omega & \sigma\end{bmatrix},$$

with $I_2$ the $2\times 2$ identity. The blocks of size $2k$ for $\sigma \pm i\omega$ correspond one to one to the Jordan blocks $J_k(\sigma + i\omega)$ of $A$.

Proof sketch

Start from a Jordan basis of $\C^n$ (the Jordan form theorem, chapter 3). For a real eigenvalue the chains can be chosen real, because their construction only solves linear systems with the real matrices $(A - \lambda I)^j$. Since $A$ is real, the conjugate of a chain $v_1, \dots, v_k$ for $\lambda = \sigma + i\omega$, with $Av_1 = \lambda v_1$ and $Av_j = \lambda v_j + v_{j-1}$, is a chain for $\bar\lambda$. Writing $v_j = a_j + ib_j$ and separating real and imaginary parts as in gives $A\begin{bmatrix}a_j & b_j\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}a_j & b_j\end{bmatrix}C + \begin{bmatrix}a_{j-1} & b_{j-1}\end{bmatrix}$, with $a_0 = b_0 = 0$; and the $2k$ real vectors $a_j, b_j$ span the same complex space as the $2k$ vectors $v_j, \bar v_j$, so they are linearly independent.

Full statement and proof: Perko, ch. 1 (Jordan forms); Horn and Johnson, Matrix Analysis, 2nd ed., ch. 3.

For real $A$, let $E^s$ ($E^c$, $E^u$) be the set of real vectors in the sum of the generalized eigenspaces $\Null\big((A - \lambda I)^n\big)$ over the eigenvalues with $\Re\lambda < 0$ ($= 0$, $> 0$), where $\Null$ denotes the null space.

$\R^n = E^s \oplus E^c \oplus E^u$, each subspace is invariant under $A$ and under every $e^{tA}$, and $\dim E^s$ is the number of eigenvalues with negative real part counted with algebraic multiplicity (similarly for $E^c$, $E^u$). Moreover

$$E^s = \{x_0 : e^{tA}x_0 \to 0 \text{ as } t \to +\infty\},\qquad E^u = \{x_0 : e^{tA}x_0 \to 0 \text{ as } t \to -\infty\}.$$

Proof sketch. The splitting and invariance come from the real Jordan form (): generalized eigenspaces are invariant under $A$, hence under $e^{tA}$, which is a polynomial in $A$ (); they span $\C^n$, and complex conjugation maps the one for $\lambda$ onto the one for $\bar\lambda$, so their real vectors form real subspaces of the stated dimensions. In a Jordan basis, components in blocks with $\Re\lambda < 0$ decay like $t^je^{t\Re\lambda}$. In a block with $\Re\lambda \ge 0$, let $y_j$ be the last nonzero coordinate of $y$; then $(e^{tJ}y)_j = e^{\lambda t}y_j$, whose modulus does not decrease, so the solution does not tend to 0. Hence $x(t) \to 0$ exactly when the components outside $E^s$ vanish. Apply the same argument to $-A$ for $E^u$.

Planar flows: the trace–determinant atlas

Classify all flows $\dot x = Ax$ with $A \in \R^{2\times 2}$: up to linear changes of coordinates, and up to continuous ones.

With $\tau = \tr A$ and $\Delta = \det A$, the characteristic polynomial is $\lambda^2 - \tau\lambda + \Delta$, so (the $2\times 2$ eigenvalue theorem, chapter 3)

$$\lambda_\pm = \frac{\tau}{2} \pm \sqrt{\frac{\tau^2}{4} - \Delta},\qquad \lambda_+ + \lambda_- = \tau,\qquad \lambda_+\lambda_- = \Delta.$$

The pair $(\tau, \Delta)$ is a pair of similarity invariants (similarity invariants, chapter 3) that determines the eigenvalues; the parabola $\Delta = \tau^2/4$ separates real from complex eigenvalues. Region by region:

Let $A \in \R^{2\times 2}$ with $\tau = \tr A$ and $\Delta = \det A$.

  1. If $\Delta < \tau^2/4$, the eigenvalues are real and distinct, and each has an eigenvector line, invariant under the flow. They have opposite signs if $\Delta < 0$, the sign of $\tau$ if $\Delta > 0$, and they are $0$ and $\tau$ if $\Delta = 0$.
  2. If $\Delta = \tau^2/4$, the eigenvalue $\tau/2$ is double, and either $A = \frac\tau2 I$ or $A$ has exactly one eigenvector line.
  3. If $\Delta > \tau^2/4$, the eigenvalues are $\frac\tau2 \pm i\omega$ with $\omega = \sqrt{\Delta - \tau^2/4} > 0$, no line is invariant under $A$, and $e^{tA} = e^{\tau t/2}S\,U(\omega t)\,S^{-1}$ for a real invertible $S$, where $U(\theta) = \begin{bmatrix}\cos\theta & \sin\theta\\ -\sin\theta & \cos\theta\end{bmatrix}$. Hence $e^{-\tau t/2}x(t)$ runs around an ellipse centred at $0$ with period $2\pi/\omega$, and the polar angle of every solution $x(t) \neq 0$ changes monotonically, by $2\pi$ in each period; for $\tau = 0$ the orbits are these ellipses.
  4. Both eigenvalues have negative real part if and only if $\tau < 0 < \Delta$, and both have positive real part if and only if $\tau > 0$ and $\Delta > 0$. The flows of $\dot x = Ax$ and $\dot y = By$ on $\R^n$ are topologically conjugate if there is a homeomorphism $h: \R^n \to \R^n$ (a continuous bijection with continuous inverse) such that $h(e^{tA}x) = e^{tB}h(x)$ for all $x$ and $t$. The matrix $A$ is hyperbolic if no eigenvalue lies on the imaginary axis.

If $A, B \in \R^{n\times n}$ are hyperbolic, their flows are topologically conjugate if and only if $\dim E^s(A) = \dim E^s(B)$. There are exactly $n + 1$ classes; in the plane, sources, saddles and sinks.

Stability in continuous and discrete time

The equilibrium $0$ of $\dot x = Ax$ (or of $x_{k+1} = Ax_k$) is stable (in the sense of Lyapunov) if for every $\eps > 0$ there is $\delta > 0$ such that $\norm{x_0} < \delta$ (Euclidean norm) implies $\norm{x(t)} < \eps$ for all $t \ge 0$; asymptotically stable if it is stable and $x(t) \to 0$ for every $x_0$; exponentially stable if there are $M \ge 1$ and $\beta > 0$ with $\norm{x(t)} \le Me^{-\beta t}\norm{x_0}$ for all $x_0$ and $t \ge 0$. A matrix is Hurwitz if $\alpha(A) < 0$ and Schur stable if its spectral radius $\rho(A) = \max_i\abs{\lambda_i}$ is less than 1.

For linear systems these are properties of the matrices $e^{tA}$, and a local property is a global one:

Let $A \in \F^{n\times n}$, where $\F$ is $\R$ or $\C$, with states in $\F^n$.

  1. The equilibrium $0$ of $\dot x = Ax$ is stable if and only if $\sup_{t\ge0}\norm{e^{tA}} < \infty$.
  2. If $e^{tA}x_0 \to 0$ for every $x_0$ in some ball around $0$, then $e^{tA} \to 0$ as $t \to \infty$; hence $x(t) \to 0$ for every $x_0 \in \F^n$, and $0$ is asymptotically stable.

Lyapunov's method

Certify that $A$ is Hurwitz without computing eigenvalues, by exhibiting an energy that decreases along every trajectory. The certificate should also bound transients and survive situations, such as time variation, in which eigenvalues say nothing.

Try the Euclidean energy $V(x) = \norm{x}^2$. Along solutions, $\dot V = x^\T(A^\T + A)x$, which is negative for all $x \neq 0$ exactly when $A + A^\T \prec 0$ (negative definite), that is, when the numerical abscissa $\omega(A) = \lambda_{\max}\big(\frac12(A + A^\T)\big)$, the largest eigenvalue of the symmetric part of $A$, is negative (abscissas, chapter 6). For $A = \begin{bmatrix}-1 & w\\ 0 & -2\end{bmatrix}$ with $w > 2\sqrt2$ this fails although $A$ is Hurwitz: $\frac12(A + A^\T)$ has determinant $2 - w^2/4 < 0$, hence a positive eigenvalue, and solutions starting along the corresponding eigenvector initially move away from 0. The remedy is to weight the norm, $V(x) = x^\T Px$ with $P \succ 0$ (positive definite: $x^\T Px > 0$ for $x \neq 0$), for which $\dot V = x^\T(A^\T P + PA)x$.

For real $A$ and symmetric $Q$, the Lyapunov equation is $A^\T P + PA = -Q$, a linear equation for the symmetric matrix $P$. A quadratic $V(x) = x^\T Px$ with $P \succ 0$ is a Lyapunov function for $\dot x = Ax$ if $\dot V = -x^\T Qx < 0$ for all $x \neq 0$, that is, $Q \succ 0$.

For $A \in \R^{n\times n}$ the following are equivalent.

  1. $A$ is Hurwitz.
  2. For every $Q \succ 0$ the Lyapunov equation has a unique solution $P$, and $P \succ 0$; it is $P = \int_0^\infty e^{tA^\T}Qe^{tA}\,\dd t$.
  3. There is $P \succ 0$ with $A^\T P + PA \prec 0$.

Proof. (1 $\Rightarrow$ 2) By , $\norm{e^{tA}} \le Me^{-\beta t}$ with $\beta > 0$, so the integral converges. It is positive definite: $x^\T Px = \int_0^\infty \norm{Q^{1/2}e^{tA}x}^2\dd t > 0$ for $x \neq 0$, with $Q^{1/2}$ the positive definite square root of $Q$, because the integrand is continuous and positive at $t = 0$. It solves the equation:

$$A^\T P + PA = \int_0^\infty \frac{\dd}{\dd t}\big(e^{tA^\T}Qe^{tA}\big)\,\dd t = \big[e^{tA^\T}Qe^{tA}\big]_0^\infty = -Q.$$

It is unique: if $A^\T X + XA = 0$, then $\frac{\dd}{\dd t}(e^{tA^\T}Xe^{tA}) = e^{tA^\T}(A^\T X + XA)e^{tA} = 0$, so $e^{tA^\T}Xe^{tA} = X$ for all $t$, and letting $t \to \infty$ gives $X = 0$. (2 $\Rightarrow$ 3) Take $Q = I$. (3 $\Rightarrow$ 1) Let $Av = \lambda v$ with $v \in \C^n$ nonzero and put $Q = -(A^\T P + PA) \succ 0$. Since $A$ is real, $v^*A^\T = \bar\lambda v^*$, so

$$-v^*Qv = v^*(A^\T P + PA)v = (\bar\lambda + \lambda)\,v^*Pv = 2\Re\lambda\;v^*Pv.$$

As $v^*Pv > 0$ and $v^*Qv > 0$, $\Re\lambda < 0$.

The linear map $\mathcal L_A(P) = A^\T P + PA$ on $\R^{n\times n}$ has eigenvalues $\lambda_i + \lambda_j$, $1 \le i, j \le n$. Hence $A^\T P + PA = -Q$ has exactly one solution $P \in \R^{n\times n}$ for every $Q \in \R^{n\times n}$ if and only if $\lambda_i + \lambda_j \neq 0$ for all $i, j$ (possibly equal); this solution is symmetric whenever $Q$ is.

Proof sketch

Stacking the columns of $P$ into a vector, $\mathcal L_A$ becomes the $n^2\times n^2$ matrix $I \otimes A^\T + A^\T \otimes I$, with $\otimes$ the Kronecker product. Triangularizing $A^\T = UTU^*$ turns it into $(U \otimes U)(I \otimes T + T \otimes I)(U \otimes U)^*$, and $I \otimes T + T \otimes I$ is triangular with diagonal entries $\lambda_i + \lambda_j$. A linear map is invertible exactly when 0 is not an eigenvalue. If $Q$ is symmetric, $P^\T$ solves the same equation, so $P^\T = P$. The Kronecker form is also how this guide's runtime solves the equation, as a linear system in $n^2$ unknowns.

Let $A^\T P + PA = -Q$ with $P \succ 0$ and $Q \succ 0$, write $\lambda_{\max}$ and $\lambda_{\min}$ for the largest and smallest eigenvalues of a symmetric matrix, and let $\kappa(P) = \lambda_{\max}(P)/\lambda_{\min}(P)$. Then along every solution

$$V(x(t)) \le e^{-2\gamma t}V(x_0),\qquad \norm{x(t)} \le \sqrt{\kappa(P)}\,e^{-\gamma t}\norm{x_0},\qquad \gamma = \frac{\lambda_{\min}(Q)}{2\lambda_{\max}(P)}.$$

In particular $\sup_{t\ge0}\norm{e^{tA}} \le \sqrt{\kappa(P)}$ whenever $P \succ 0$ and $A^\T P + PA \preceq 0$ (negative semidefinite; in general $X \preceq Y$ means $Y - X \succeq 0$).

Proof. $\lambda_{\min}(P)\norm{x}^2 \le V(x) \le \lambda_{\max}(P)\norm{x}^2$ and $x^\T Qx \ge \lambda_{\min}(Q)\norm{x}^2$, so $\dot V = -x^\T Qx \le -2\gamma V$, and $e^{2\gamma t}V(x(t))$ is nonincreasing. Then $\lambda_{\min}(P)\norm{x(t)}^2 \le V(x(t)) \le e^{-2\gamma t}\lambda_{\max}(P)\norm{x_0}^2$. For the last claim, $V$ is nonincreasing when $A^\T P + PA \preceq 0$.

For $A = \begin{bmatrix}-1 & w\\ 0 & -2\end{bmatrix}$ and $Q = I$, write $P = \begin{bmatrix}p & q\\ q & r\end{bmatrix}$; the equation $A^\T P + PA = -I$ reads $-2p = -1$, $wp - 3q = 0$, $2wq - 4r = -1$, so

$$P = \begin{bmatrix}\frac12 & \frac w6\\ \frac w6 & \frac14 + \frac{w^2}{12}\end{bmatrix},\qquad \det P = \frac18 + \frac{w^2}{72} > 0.$$

For $w = 0$, $P = \diag(\frac12, \frac14)$ and the bound of gives $\gamma = 1 = -\alpha(A)$, the true rate. For $w = 10$, $\lambda(P) \approx 8.91$ and $0.170$, so $\kappa(P) \approx 52.5$: the bound $\sqrt{\kappa(P)} \approx 7.2$ is valid but loose against the true peak $2.56$ of $\norm{e^{tA}}$ (chapter 6), and $\gamma \approx 0.056$ is far below the true rate 1. Conversely, by every $P \succ 0$ with $A^\T P + PA \preceq 0$ has $\kappa(P) \ge \max_{t\ge0}\norm{e^{tA}}^2 \approx 6.57$: transient growth forces elongated Lyapunov ellipses.

Let $A \in \R^{n\times n}$ be Hurwitz and $Q \in \R^{n\times n}$ symmetric, not necessarily definite. The Lyapunov equation $A^\T P + PA = -Q$ has the unique solution $P = \int_0^\infty e^{tA^\T}Qe^{tA}\,\dd t$, which is symmetric, and every solution of $\dot x = Ax$ satisfies

$$x_0^\T Px_0 = \int_0^\infty x(t)^\T Qx(t)\,\dd t.$$

If $Q \succeq 0$, then $P \succeq 0$.

Proof. The proof of (1 $\Rightarrow$ 2) in shows the convergence of the integral, the equation and uniqueness without using $Q \succ 0$. The integrand is symmetric, and substituting $x(t) = e^{tA}x_0$ gives the cost formula, whose integrand $x^\T Qx$ is nonnegative when $Q \succeq 0$.

$P$ is a cost and a Gramian. By , $x_0^\T Px_0$ is the quadratic cost accumulated along the trajectory from $x_0$, a value function. With $Q = C^\T C$ for an output $y = Cx$, $C \in \R^{p\times n}$, the cost is the output energy $\int_0^\infty\norm{y(t)}^2\,\dd t$, and $P$ is the infinite-horizon limit of the observability Gramian $W_o(t_f)$ of chapter 9. The Riccati equation of chapter 11 is this equation with a quadratic term added for the control.

For $A \in \R^{n\times n}$: $\rho(A) < 1$ if and only if for some (equivalently, every) $Q \succ 0$ the equation $A^\T PA - P = -Q$ has a solution $P \succ 0$; it is then unique and equals $\sum_{k\ge0}(A^\T)^kQA^k$.

Proof sketch. If $\rho(A) < 1$ the series converges by , and substituting shows it solves the equation, since the sum telescopes; it satisfies $P \succeq Q \succ 0$, because the term $k = 0$ is $Q$ and the other terms are positive semidefinite. Conversely, if $Av = \lambda v$ with $v \neq 0$, then $-v^*Qv = v^*(A^\T PA - P)v = (\abs{\lambda}^2 - 1)\,v^*Pv$, which forces $\abs{\lambda} < 1$. Uniqueness follows as in , with $(A^\T)^kXA^k = X$ and $k \to \infty$.

The condition "$P \succ 0$ and $A^\T P + PA \prec 0$" is a linear matrix inequality (LMI) in $P$: its feasible set is a convex cone, which convex optimization can search. Unlike an eigenvalue test, the same inequality can be imposed for a whole family of matrices at once, and a common solution then serves every matrix in the convex hull of the family (); by in , it certifies stability of systems whose matrix varies continuously in time within that hull. Equivalences to remember (, ): Hurwitz $\Leftrightarrow$ exponential decay $\Leftrightarrow$ a quadratic Lyapunov function exists $\Leftrightarrow$ the Lyapunov equation with $Q \succ 0$ has a positive definite solution.

Let $A_1, \dots, A_m \in \R^{n\times n}$. The symmetric matrices $P$ with $P \succ 0$ and $A_i^\T P + PA_i \prec 0$ for $i = 1, \dots, m$ form a convex cone: with $P$ and $P'$ it contains $aP + bP'$ for all $a, b \ge 0$ not both $0$. For each such $P$ there is $c > 0$ with $A^\T P + PA \preceq -cP$ for every $A$ in the convex hull of $A_1, \dots, A_m$, that is, every $A = \sum_i\theta_iA_i$ with $\theta_i \ge 0$ and $\sum_i\theta_i = 1$.

Proof. The map $P \mapsto A_i^\T P + PA_i$ is linear, and sums and positive multiples of positive definite matrices are positive definite. Given $P$, let $Q_i = -(A_i^\T P + PA_i) \succ 0$ and $c = \min_i\lambda_{\min}\big(P^{-1/2}Q_iP^{-1/2}\big) > 0$, where $P^{-1/2}$ is the inverse of the positive definite square root of $P$. Then $Q_i \succeq cP$ for every $i$, and $A^\T P + PA = -\sum_i\theta_iQ_i \preceq -cP$.

Time-varying systems

A linear time-varying (LTV) system is $\dot x = A(t)x$ with $A: \R \to \R^{n\times n}$ continuous. Superposition still holds, with the proof of once uniqueness is known (), so the solutions are again described by a matrix.

The state-transition matrix $\Phi(t, t_0)$ is the solution of the matrix equation $\partial_t\Phi(t, t_0) = A(t)\Phi(t, t_0)$ (the derivative in $t$ with $t_0$ fixed) with $\Phi(t_0, t_0) = I$; it exists and is unique by .

Let $A: \R \to \R^{n\times n}$ be continuous and $t_0 \in \R$. The Peano–Baker series

$$\Phi(t, t_0) = I + \int_{t_0}^tA(s_1)\,\dd s_1 + \int_{t_0}^t\!\int_{t_0}^{s_1}A(s_1)A(s_2)\,\dd s_2\,\dd s_1 + \cdots,$$

whose $k$-th term is $\int_{t_0}^t\int_{t_0}^{s_1}\cdots\int_{t_0}^{s_{k-1}}A(s_1)\cdots A(s_k)\,\dd s_k\cdots\dd s_1$, converges uniformly for $t$ in bounded intervals, and its sum is the only solution of $\partial_t\Phi = A(t)\Phi$ with $\Phi(t_0, t_0) = I$. For every $x_0$, $x(t) = \Phi(t, t_0)x_0$ is the only solution of $\dot x = A(t)x$ with $x(t_0) = x_0$.

Proof sketch

On an interval $\abs{t - t_0} \le T$ let $a = \max\norm{A(s)}$. The $k$-th term has norm at most $a^k\abs{t - t_0}^k/k!$, the volume of the simplex of integration times $a^k$, so the series converges uniformly (Weierstrass M-test); so does the series of derivatives, whose $k$-th term is $A(t)$ times the $(k-1)$-th term of the series. Hence the sum is differentiable with $\partial_t\Phi = A(t)\Phi$, and $\Phi(t_0, t_0) = I$. If $x$ and $y$ solve $\dot x = A(t)x$ with $x(t_0) = y(t_0)$, then $e = x - y$ satisfies $\norm{e(t)} \le a\,\big|\int_{t_0}^t\norm{e(s)}\,\dd s\big|$, and Grönwall's inequality gives $e = 0$. Applied column by column, this gives the uniqueness of $\Phi$.

For all $t, s, r$:

  1. $\Phi(t, s)\Phi(s, r) = \Phi(t, r)$, and $\Phi(t, s)$ is invertible with $\Phi(t, s)^{-1} = \Phi(s, t)$;
  2. $\det\Phi(t, s) = \exp\int_s^t\tr A(\sigma)\,\dd\sigma$;
  3. if $A$ is constant, $\Phi(t, s) = e^{(t-s)A}$; if the matrices $A(t)$ commute with one another, $\Phi(t, s) = \exp\int_s^tA(\sigma)\,\dd\sigma$.

Proof. (1) As functions of $t$, both sides solve $\dot X = A(t)X$ and equal $\Phi(s, r)$ at $t = s$; by uniqueness () they agree. With $r = t$ this gives the inverse. (2) Jacobi's formula for the derivative of a determinant gives $\partial_t\det\Phi = \tr\big(\operatorname{adj}(\Phi)\,A\Phi\big) = \tr(A)\det\Phi$, with $\operatorname{adj}$ the adjugate ($\Phi\operatorname{adj}\Phi = \det\Phi\, I$); integrate. (3) If the $A(t)$ commute, $M(t) = \int_s^tA$ commutes with $\dot M(t) = A(t)$, so $\frac{\dd}{\dd t}e^{M(t)} = A(t)e^{M(t)}$.

The conjecture is false. Take

$$A(t) = \begin{bmatrix}-1 + \frac32\cos^2 t & 1 - \frac32\cos t\sin t\\ -1 - \frac32\sin t\cos t & -1 + \frac32\sin^2 t\end{bmatrix}.$$

For every $t$, $\tr A(t) = -\frac12$ and $\det A(t) = \frac12$, so the frozen eigenvalues are the constants $\frac{-1 \pm i\sqrt7}{4}$, with real part $-\frac14$. Yet $x(t) = e^{t/2}(-\cos t, \sin t)^\T$ is a solution (substitute), and $\norm{x(t)} = e^{t/2} \to \infty$.

The mechanism: $A(t) = U(t)A_0U(t)^\T$ with $A_0 = A(0) = \begin{bmatrix}\frac12 & 1\\ -1 & -1\end{bmatrix}$ and $U(t) = \begin{bmatrix}\cos t & \sin t\\ -\sin t & \cos t\end{bmatrix}$, a rotation by $-t$. In the rotating coordinates $y = U(t)^\T x$, the equation becomes $\dot y = (A_0 - K)y$ with $K = U^\T\dot U = \begin{bmatrix}0 & 1\\ -1 & 0\end{bmatrix}$, and $A_0 - K = \diag(\frac12, -1)$. So $\Phi(t, 0) = U(t)\diag(e^{t/2}, e^{-t})$: the rotation of the frame adds the skew term $-K$, and $A_0 - K$ is unstable although every frozen matrix is stable.

Periodic systems are decided by one matrix:

Let $A: \R \to \R^{n\times n}$ be continuous with $A(t + T) = A(t)$ for all $t$, for some period $T > 0$. Call $\Phi(T, 0)$ the monodromy matrix and its eigenvalues the Floquet multipliers. Then $\Phi(t + T, s + T) = \Phi(t, s)$ for all $t, s$, and $\Phi(kT + t, 0) = \Phi(t, 0)\Phi(T, 0)^k$ for all integers $k \ge 0$ and all $t$. Hence every solution of $\dot x = A(t)x$ tends to 0 as $t \to \infty$ if and only if all Floquet multipliers lie in the open unit disk, and every solution is bounded on $t \ge 0$ if and only if the powers $\Phi(T, 0)^k$ are bounded.

Proof. As a function of $t$, $\Phi(t + T, s + T)$ solves $\dot X = A(t + T)X = A(t)X$ and equals $I$ at $t = s$, so it is $\Phi(t, s)$ by . With part 1 of ,

$$\Phi(kT + t, 0) = \Phi(kT + t, kT)\,\Phi(kT, (k-1)T)\cdots\Phi(T, 0) = \Phi(t, 0)\,\Phi(T, 0)^k.$$

On $[0, T]$ the continuous functions $\norm{\Phi(t, 0)}$ and $\norm{\Phi(t, 0)^{-1}}$ are bounded by some $c$, so $\norm{\Phi(T, 0)^kx_0}/c \le \norm{x(kT + t)} \le c\,\norm{\Phi(T, 0)^kx_0}$ for $t \in [0, T]$. The solution therefore behaves like the iterates of $x_{k+1} = \Phi(T, 0)x_k$, and with gives both criteria.

This is a discrete-time test, not a frozen-time one. In the counterexample $T = \pi$ and $\Phi(\pi, 0) = -\diag(e^{\pi/2}, e^{-\pi})$, with a multiplier of modulus $e^{\pi/2} \approx 4.81$.

    Answer

  3. A real $3\times 3$ matrix has eigenvalues $-1$ and $2 \pm i$. Give $\dim E^s$ and $\dim E^u$. Is its flow topologically conjugate to that of $\diag(-1, 1, 1)$? To that of $\diag(-1, -1, 1)$?

    Answer

    $\dim E^s = 1$, $\dim E^u = 2$. Both matrices are hyperbolic; it is conjugate to $\diag(-1, 1, 1)$ (also $\dim E^s = 1$) and not to $\diag(-1, -1, 1)$ ().

  4. Let $A$ be normal and Hurwitz, so that $A$ commutes with $A^\T$. Show that $P = -(A + A^\T)^{-1}$ solves $A^\T P + PA = -I$. What does this say about the Lyapunov ellipses?

    Answer

    $A + A^\T$ commutes with $A$ and $A^\T$, so $A^\T P + PA = -(A^\T + A)(A + A^\T)^{-1} = -I$; and $A + A^\T \prec 0$ because, with $A = U\Lambda U^*$ for a unitary $U$ (the normal-matrix theorem, chapter 4), $A + A^\T = A + A^* = U(\Lambda + \bar\Lambda)U^*$ has the eigenvalues $2\Re\lambda_i < 0$. For symmetric Hurwitz $A$, $P = -\frac12A^{-1}$ has the same eigenvectors as $A$: the ellipses are aligned with the modes, and $\kappa(P) = \max_i\abs{\lambda_i}/\min_i\abs{\lambda_i} = \norm{A}\norm{A^{-1}}$.

  5. Is $x_{k+1} = Ax_k$ with $A = \begin{bmatrix}0.5 & 10\\ 0 & 0.5\end{bmatrix}$ asymptotically stable? What does $\norm{A^k}$ do for small $k$?

    Answer

    Yes: $\rho(A) = 0.5 < 1$ (). But $A^k = 0.5^kI + 10k\,0.5^{k-1}N$ with $N = J_2(0)$, so the $(1,2)$ entry is $10, 10, 7.5, 5, \dots$ for $k = 1, 2, 3, 4$: the powers first grow to about 10 before they decay, the discrete analogue of transient growth.
