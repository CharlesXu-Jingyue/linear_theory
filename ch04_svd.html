Symmetric matrices, quadratic forms and the SVD

Symmetric matrices are exactly the real matrices that a rotation of coordinates makes diagonal, and their quadratic forms are the energy functions of linear algebra. The singular value decomposition (SVD) extends the same picture to every matrix, square or not: rotate, stretch along orthogonal axes, rotate again. Keeping the largest stretches gives a best low-rank approximation, with an exactly known error.

Adjoints and the normal family

Chapter 3 diagonalizes a matrix as $A = V\Lambda V^{-1}$, with $\Lambda$ the diagonal matrix of eigenvalues and an arbitrary invertible $V$, which may be nearly singular. Identify the matrices that can be diagonalized by a change of basis that preserves lengths and angles.

Work in $\F^n$, where $\F$ is $\R$ or $\C$ (real or complex scalars), with the inner product $\inner{x}{y} = y^*x$, where $y^*$ is the conjugate transpose of $y$ (on $\R^n$ this is $x^\T y$, with $x^\T$ the transpose of $x$), and the norm $\norm{x} = \sqrt{\inner{x}{x}}$. A matrix $U$ with orthonormal columns, $U^*U = I$, preserves inner products: $\inner{Ux}{Uy} = y^*U^*Ux = y^*x = \inner{x}{y}$ (for square $U$ see the proposition on unitary matrices, chapter 2). A change of basis by such a $U$ is the "rigid" change of coordinates we are after.

For $A \in \F^{m\times n}$ the adjoint $A^*$ is the conjugate transpose, $(A^*)_{ij} = \overline{a_{ji}}$. For real $A$, $A^* = A^\T$.

A square matrix $A \in \C^{n\times n}$ is

  • Hermitian if $A^* = A$; a real Hermitian matrix is symmetric, $A^\T = A$;
  • skew-Hermitian if $A^* = -A$ (real case: skew-symmetric);
  • unitary if $A^*A = I$ (real case: orthogonal);
  • normal if $A^*A = AA^*$.

Let $A \in \F^{m\times n}$. (i) $A^*$ is the only matrix $M \in \F^{n\times m}$ with

$$\inner{Ax}{y} = \inner{x}{My}\qquad\text{for all } x \in \F^n,\ y \in \F^m.$$

(ii) $(AB)^* = B^*A^*$ for every $B \in \F^{n\times k}$, and $(A^*)^* = A$. (iii) Hermitian, skew-Hermitian and unitary matrices are normal; a unitary $A$ also satisfies $AA^* = I$.

Proof. (i) Both $\inner{Ax}{y} = y^*Ax$ and $\inner{x}{A^*y}$ equal $\sum_{i,j}\overline{y_i}\,a_{ij}x_j$, because $(A^*y)_j = \sum_i\overline{a_{ij}}\,y_i$. If $M$ and $M'$ both satisfy the identity, then $\inner{x}{(M - M')y} = 0$ for all $x$; taking $x = (M - M')y$ gives $(M - M')y = 0$ for every $y$. (ii) $\inner{ABx}{y} = \inner{Bx}{A^*y} = \inner{x}{B^*A^*y}$, and $\inner{A^*y}{x} = \overline{\inner{x}{A^*y}} = \overline{\inner{Ax}{y}} = \inner{y}{Ax}$; both claims follow from the uniqueness in (i). (iii) If $A^* = \pm A$, then $A^*$ commutes with $A$. If $A^*A = I$, the square matrix $A$ has the left inverse $A^*$, so it is invertible with $A^{-1} = A^*$ (chapter 2), and $AA^* = I = A^*A$.

Probes in the plane. Every symmetric $\begin{bmatrix}a & b\\ b & c\end{bmatrix}$, every rotation $R_\theta$ (angle $\theta$) and the skew-symmetric generator $\begin{bmatrix}0 & -1\\ 1 & 0\end{bmatrix}$ of rotations are normal (). So is the scaled rotation $\begin{bmatrix}a & -b\\ b & a\end{bmatrix}$, which is neither symmetric nor skew unless $a = 0$ or $b = 0$: normality is a strictly larger class. The shear $S = \begin{bmatrix}1 & 1\\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}$ is not normal: $S^\T S = \begin{bmatrix}1 & 1\\ 1 & 2\end{bmatrix}$ while $SS^\T = \begin{bmatrix}2 & 1\\ 1 & 1\end{bmatrix}$.

Normality can be tested on lengths alone. The test rests on a fact about the forms $x^*Mx$.

Let $M \in \F^{n\times n}$. (i) If $M$ is real, then $x^\T Mx = x^\T M_s x$ for every $x \in \R^n$, where $M_s = \tfrac12(M + M^\T)$ is the symmetric part of $M$. (ii) If $M$ is Hermitian (symmetric when $\F = \R$) and $x^*Mx = 0$ for every $x \in \F^n$, then $M = 0$. So a Hermitian matrix is determined by its form $x \mapsto x^*Mx$.

Proof. (i) The number $x^\T Mx$ equals its transpose $x^\T M^\T x$; average the two. (ii) Testing $x = e_j$ gives $m_{jj} = 0$, testing $x = e_j + e_k$ gives $m_{jk} + m_{kj} = 2\Re m_{jk} = 0$, and, when $\F = \C$, testing $x = e_j + i\,e_k$ gives $i(m_{jk} - m_{kj}) = -2\Im m_{jk} = 0$; in the real case the first two tests suffice. Here $e_j$ is the $j$-th standard basis vector and $i$ the imaginary unit (engineering texts write $j$).

$A \in \F^{n\times n}$ is normal if and only if $\norm{Ax} = \norm{A^*x}$ for every $x \in \F^n$.

Proof. $\norm{Ax}^2 - \norm{A^*x}^2 = x^*(A^*A - AA^*)x$, and $H = A^*A - AA^*$ is Hermitian. So the two lengths agree for every $x$ exactly when the form of $H$ vanishes identically, that is, by , when $H = 0$.

The spectral theorem

By the $2\times 2$ eigenvalue formula of chapter 3, the symmetric matrix $\begin{bmatrix}a & b\\ b & c\end{bmatrix}$ has eigenvalues

$$\lambda_\pm = \frac{a+c}{2} \pm \sqrt{\Big(\frac{a-c}{2}\Big)^2 + b^2}.$$

The discriminant is a sum of squares, so both eigenvalues are real, and they coincide only when $a = c$ and $b = 0$, that is, for a multiple of $I$. For $\begin{bmatrix}2 & 1\\ 1 & 2\end{bmatrix}$ the eigenvalues are $3$ and $1$ with eigenvectors $(1, 1)$ and $(1, -1)$: perpendicular. Contrast $\begin{bmatrix}1 & 1\\ 0 & 2\end{bmatrix}$, with eigenvalues $1, 2$ and eigenvectors $(1, 0)$ and $(1, 1)$ at $45^\circ$.

Every real symmetric matrix has real eigenvalues and an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors.

Let $A \in \C^{n\times n}$ be Hermitian. Then (i) $x^*Ax$ is real for every $x$; (ii) every eigenvalue of $A$ is real; (iii) eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal; (iv) if a subspace $S$ is invariant ($AS \subseteq S$), then so is its orthogonal complement $S^\perp$.

Proof. (i) $\overline{x^*Ax} = (x^*Ax)^* = x^*A^*x = x^*Ax$. (ii) If $Ax = \lambda x$ with $x \ne 0$, then $\lambda = x^*Ax / x^*x$ is real by (i). (iii) If $Ax = \lambda x$ and $Ay = \mu y$, then $\lambda\inner{x}{y} = \inner{Ax}{y} = \inner{x}{Ay} = \mu\inner{x}{y}$, using that $\mu$ is real; so $\lambda \ne \mu$ forces $\inner{x}{y} = 0$. (iv) For $y \in S^\perp$ and $s \in S$, $\inner{Ay}{s} = \inner{y}{As} = 0$ because $As \in S$.

$$[q_1\ W]^\T A\, [q_1\ W] = \begin{bmatrix}\lambda_1 & q_1^\T A W\\ W^\T A q_1 & W^\T A W\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}\lambda_1 & 0\\ 0 & A'\end{bmatrix},$$

If $A = Q\Lambda Q^\T$ is symmetric and $f$ is a real function defined on its eigenvalues, put $f(A) = Q\, f(\Lambda)\, Q^\T$ with $f(\Lambda) = \diag(f(\lambda_1),\dots,f(\lambda_n))$. This does not depend on the choice of $Q$, agrees with polynomials in $A$ and with power series in $A$ that converge at every eigenvalue, gives $A^k = Q\Lambda^kQ^\T$ and $e^{tA} = Qe^{t\Lambda}Q^\T$, and for $A$ with nonnegative eigenvalues defines the unique symmetric square root with nonnegative eigenvalues, $A^{1/2} = Q\Lambda^{1/2}Q^\T$.

Proof. Grouping equal eigenvalues, $Qf(\Lambda)Q^\T = \sum_\mu f(\mu)P_\mu$, where $\mu$ runs over the distinct eigenvalues and $P_\mu = \sum_{\lambda_i = \mu}q_iq_i^\T$ is the orthogonal projector onto the eigenspace $E_\mu = \Null(A - \mu I)$, which does not depend on $Q$. Since $Q^\T Q = I$, $A^k = Q\Lambda^kQ^\T$, which gives polynomials; for a power series $\sum_k c_kz^k$ converging at every $\lambda_i$, the partial sums $\sum_{k\le K}c_kA^k = Q\big(\sum_{k \le K}c_k\Lambda^k\big)Q^\T$ converge to $Qf(\Lambda)Q^\T$. For the square root, let $B$ be symmetric with nonnegative eigenvalues and $B^2 = A$. Then $B$ commutes with $A$, so it maps each $E_\mu$ into itself, and its restriction to $E_\mu$ is symmetric with nonnegative eigenvalues $\nu$ satisfying $\nu^2 = \mu$. By applied on $E_\mu$, $B = \sqrt\mu\, I$ there. The eigenspaces span $\R^n$, so $B = \sum_\mu\sqrt\mu\,P_\mu = A^{1/2}$.

$A \in \C^{n\times n}$ is normal if and only if $A = U\Lambda U^*$ for some unitary $U$ and diagonal $\Lambda$ (with complex entries, the eigenvalues).

Proof. If $A = U\Lambda U^*$, then $A^*A = U\bar\Lambda\Lambda U^* = U\Lambda\bar\Lambda U^* = AA^*$, where $\bar\Lambda$ is the entrywise conjugate. Conversely, take the Schur form $A = UTU^*$ (chapter 3), with $U$ unitary and $T$ upper triangular; $T = U^*AU$ is normal because $A$ is: $T^*T = U^*A^*AU = U^*AA^*U = TT^*$. Comparing the $(1,1)$ entries of $T^*T$ and $TT^*$ gives $\abs{t_{11}}^2 = \sum_j \abs{t_{1j}}^2$, so the first row of $T$ is zero off the diagonal. Then $T = \diag(t_{11}, T')$ with $T'$ normal and triangular, and induction makes $T$ diagonal.

Two hypotheses cannot be weakened. Real eigenvalues and diagonalizability do not give an orthonormal eigenbasis: $\begin{bmatrix}1 & 1\\ 0 & 2\end{bmatrix}$ has the eigenvectors $(1, 0)$ and $(1, 1)$. And over $\C$, the transpose symmetry $A^\T = A$ is the wrong condition: $A = \begin{bmatrix}1 & i\\ i & -1\end{bmatrix}$ satisfies $A^\T = A$ and $A^2 = 0$ with $A \ne 0$, so it is nilpotent and not diagonalizable at all: its only eigenvalue is $0$, and $A = V\,0\,V^{-1}$ would force $A = 0$. What matters is symmetry with respect to the inner product, $A^* = A$.

Equivalences and invariants. The spectral theorem has a trivial converse, and a complex matrix is normal exactly when it is unitarily diagonalizable (). So the eigenvalues, which unitary similarity $A \mapsto U^*AU$ preserves, should be all that distinguishes one normal matrix from another, and the special classes should be the normal matrices whose eigenvalues lie in special subsets of $\C$. Geometrically, a symmetric matrix is a set of independent scalings $\lambda_i$ along perpendicular axes $q_i$.

(i) A real $A \in \R^{n\times n}$ is symmetric if and only if $A = Q\Lambda Q^\T$ with $Q$ real orthogonal and $\Lambda$ real diagonal. (ii) Two real symmetric matrices are orthogonally similar ($B = Q^\T AQ$ with $Q$ real orthogonal) if and only if they have the same eigenvalues counted with multiplicity; likewise two normal matrices are unitarily similar if and only if they have the same eigenvalues counted with multiplicity. (iii) A normal $A \in \C^{n\times n}$ is Hermitian, positive semidefinite (Hermitian with $x^*Ax \ge 0$ for all $x$), skew-Hermitian, unitary, or an orthogonal projector ($A^2 = A = A^*$) if and only if all its eigenvalues lie, respectively, in $\R$, in $[0, \infty)$, on the imaginary axis $i\R$, on the unit circle $\{z : \abs{z} = 1\}$, or in $\{0, 1\}$.

Proof. (i) One direction is ; conversely $(Q\Lambda Q^\T)^\T = Q\Lambda^\T Q^\T = Q\Lambda Q^\T$. (ii) Similar matrices have the same eigenvalues with multiplicity (chapter 3). Conversely, write $A = U\Lambda U^*$ and $B = WMW^*$ with $U, W$ unitary () and $M = P^\T\Lambda P$ a reordering of $\Lambda$ by a permutation matrix $P$. Then $B = V^*AV$ with the unitary $V = UPW^*$; for real symmetric $A$ and $B$, makes $U$, $W$ and hence $V$ real orthogonal. (iii) Write $A = U\Lambda U^*$. Then $A^* = U\bar\Lambda U^*$, $A^*A = U\abs{\Lambda}^2U^*$ and $A^2 = U\Lambda^2U^*$, where $\bar\Lambda$ and $\abs{\Lambda}^2$ are taken entrywise, and $UDU^*$ with $D$ diagonal vanishes only if $D = 0$. So $A^* = A$, $A^* = -A$, $A^*A = I$ and $A^2 = A = A^*$ hold if and only if, for every $i$, $\bar\lambda_i = \lambda_i$, $\bar\lambda_i = -\lambda_i$, $\abs{\lambda_i} = 1$ and $\lambda_i^2 = \lambda_i = \bar\lambda_i$, the last meaning $\lambda_i \in \{0, 1\}$. Finally, for Hermitian $A$, $x^*Ax = \sum_i\lambda_i\abs{y_i}^2$ with $y = U^*x$ ranging over $\C^n$, which is $\ge 0$ for all $y$ exactly when every $\lambda_i \ge 0$ (test $y = e_i$).

Quadratic forms and definiteness

A symmetric matrix $A \in \R^{n\times n}$ defines the function $q(x) = x^\T A x$: energies, variances, quadratic costs and Lyapunov functions all have this form. Decide from $A$ whether $q$ is positive away from the origin, and describe its level sets.

For any real square $M$, $x^\T M x = x^\T \tfrac12(M + M^\T)\, x$, so only the symmetric part of a matrix enters its quadratic form, and the form determines that part (); we assume $A^\T = A$ throughout this section.

Principal axes. In the rotated coordinates $y = Q^\T x$ given by the spectral theorem, $A = Q\Lambda Q^\T$ becomes diagonal, the cross terms of $q$ disappear, and the shape of each level set can be read off from the signs of the eigenvalues: this is .

Let $A \in \R^{n\times n}$ be symmetric, $A = Q\Lambda Q^\T$ as in , and $y = Q^\T x$. Then

$$q(x) = y^\T \Lambda y = \sum_{i=1}^n \lambda_i\, y_i^2 .$$

If every $\lambda_i > 0$, the level set $q = 1$ is the ellipsoid with semi-axes of lengths $1/\sqrt{\lambda_i}$ along the $q_i$. For $n = 2$ and $\lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2$, the level set $q = 1$ is an ellipse if $\lambda_2 > 0$; a hyperbola whose asymptotes are the two lines that form the zero set $q = 0$ if $\lambda_1 > 0 > \lambda_2$; the pair of parallel lines $y_1 = \pm 1/\sqrt{\lambda_1}$ if $\lambda_1 > 0 = \lambda_2$; and empty if $\lambda_1 \le 0$.

Proof. $x^\T Ax = (Q^\T x)^\T\Lambda(Q^\T x)$. The orthogonal map $x \mapsto y$ preserves lengths and sends $q_i$ to $e_i$, so a set described in $y$ along the coordinate axes has the same shape along the $q_i$ in $x$. If all $\lambda_i > 0$, $\sum_i\lambda_iy_i^2 = 1$ is the ellipsoid with semi-axes $1/\sqrt{\lambda_i}$. For $n = 2$ with $\lambda_1 > 0 > \lambda_2$, $\lambda_1y_1^2 - \abs{\lambda_2}y_2^2 = 1$ is a hyperbola with asymptotes $y_2 = \pm\sqrt{\lambda_1/\abs{\lambda_2}}\,y_1$, which are exactly the solutions of $q = 0$. If $\lambda_1 > 0 = \lambda_2$ the equation is $\lambda_1y_1^2 = 1$, and if $\lambda_1 \le 0$ then $q \le 0$ everywhere.

A symmetric $A$ is positive definite, written $A \succ 0$, if $x^\T A x > 0$ for every $x \ne 0$, and positive semidefinite, $A \succeq 0$, if $x^\T Ax \ge 0$ for every $x$. It is negative (semi)definite if $-A$ is positive (semi)definite, and indefinite if $q$ takes both signs. For complex Hermitian $A$ the same definitions use $x^*Ax$.

For symmetric $A \in \R^{n\times n}$ the following are equivalent:

  1. $A \succ 0$;
  2. every eigenvalue of $A$ is positive;
  3. $A = B^\T B$ for some matrix $B$ with linearly independent columns (a Gram or energy form);
  4. $A = LL^\T$ with $L$ lower triangular with positive diagonal (the Cholesky factorization);
  5. $\Delta_k > 0$ for $k = 1,\dots,n$ (Sylvester's criterion).

Proof. (1)⇔(2): $q(x) = \sum_i\lambda_i y_i^2$ with $y = Q^\T x$ ranging over $\R^n$, and $\lambda_i = q(q_i)$. (2)⇒(3): $B = \Lambda^{1/2}Q^\T$ is invertible. (3)⇒(1): $x^\T B^\T Bx = \norm{Bx}^2 > 0$ because $Bx \ne 0$ for $x \ne 0$. (4)⇒(3): take $B = L^\T$. (1)⇒(5): $A^{(k)}$ is the matrix of $q$ restricted to $\spn(e_1,\dots,e_k)$, so it is positive definite and $\Delta_k$ is a product of positive eigenvalues. (5)⇒(4), by induction on $n$: write $A = \begin{bmatrix}a & b^\T\\ b & C\end{bmatrix}$ with $a = \Delta_1 > 0$. Then

$$A = \begin{bmatrix}\sqrt a & 0\\ b/\sqrt a & I\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0\\ 0 & S\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\sqrt a & b^\T/\sqrt a\\ 0 & I\end{bmatrix},\qquad S = C - \frac{bb^\T}{a}.$$

The same identity for the leading blocks gives $\Delta_{k+1}(A) = a\,\Delta_k(S)$, so the Schur complement $S$ satisfies (5), hence $S = L'L'^\T$ by induction, and $L = \begin{bmatrix}\sqrt a & 0\\ b/\sqrt a & L'\end{bmatrix}$ works.

Let $A \in \R^{n\times n}$ be symmetric. (i) $A$ is negative definite if and only if $(-1)^k\Delta_k > 0$ for $k = 1, \dots, n$: the leading minors alternate in sign, starting negative. (ii) For $n = 2$: $A \succ 0$ if and only if $\Delta_1 > 0$ and $\Delta_2 > 0$; $A$ is negative definite if and only if $\Delta_1 < 0 < \Delta_2$; and $A$ is indefinite if and only if $\Delta_2 < 0$. So when $\det A \ne 0$ the leading minors decide the class.

Proof. (i) $A$ is negative definite when $-A \succ 0$, and the $k$-th leading minor of $-A$ is $(-1)^k\Delta_k$; apply to $-A$. (ii) The first two statements are and (i) for $n = 2$. For the third, $\Delta_2 = \det A = \lambda_1\lambda_2$ (chapter 3) is negative exactly when the eigenvalues have strictly opposite signs, that is, when $q$ takes both signs. If $\Delta_2 \ne 0$, then either $\Delta_2 < 0$, or $\Delta_2 > 0$ and then $\Delta_1 = a_{11} \ne 0$, because $a_{11}a_{22} > a_{12}^2 \ge 0$; so exactly one of the three cases occurs.

For symmetric $A \in \R^{n\times n}$ the following are equivalent:

  1. $A \succeq 0$;
  2. every eigenvalue of $A$ is $\ge 0$;
  3. $A = B^\T B$ for some matrix $B$;
  4. every principal minor $\det A_{II}$ is $\ge 0$, where $A_{II}$ is the submatrix with rows and columns indexed by the same nonempty set $I \subseteq \{1, \dots, n\}$.

Proof. (1)⇔(2) as in , since $q(x) = \sum_i\lambda_iy_i^2$. (2)⇒(3): $B = \Lambda^{1/2}Q^\T$. (3)⇒(1): $x^\T B^\T Bx = \norm{Bx}^2 \ge 0$. (1)⇒(4): $A_{II}$ is the matrix of $q$ restricted to $\spn\{e_i : i \in I\}$, so it is positive semidefinite and its determinant is a product of nonnegative eigenvalues. (4)⇒(2): write column $j$ of $tI + A$ as $te_j + a_j$, with $a_j$ the $j$-th column of $A$, and expand the determinant by linearity in each column. The terms that take $a_j$ exactly for $j \in I$ contribute $t^{\,n - \abs{I}}\det A_{II}$, so

$$\det(tI + A) = t^n + \sum_{I \ne \emptyset} t^{\,n - \abs{I}}\det A_{II} \ge t^n > 0 \qquad\text{for } t > 0,$$

and no negative number $-t$ is an eigenvalue of $A$.

Variational characterization of eigenvalues

Eigenvalues are roots of the characteristic polynomial, a poor handle for estimates. Characterize the eigenvalues of a Hermitian matrix as values of optimization problems, which can be compared, restricted and perturbed.

For Hermitian $A \in \C^{n\times n}$ and $x \ne 0$, the Rayleigh quotient is $R_A(x) = \dfrac{x^*Ax}{x^*x}$. It is real by , and $R_A(cx) = \abs{c}^2x^*Ax/(\abs{c}^2x^*x) = R_A(x)$ for every scalar $c \ne 0$, so it is a function on the unit sphere.

In the plane, let $A$ have eigenvalues $\lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2$ with unit eigenvector $q_1 = (\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$. For the unit vector $x = (\cos\varphi, \sin\varphi)$,

$$R_A(x) = \lambda_1\cos^2(\varphi - \theta) + \lambda_2\sin^2(\varphi - \theta) = \frac{\lambda_1 + \lambda_2}{2} + \frac{\lambda_1 - \lambda_2}{2}\cos 2(\varphi - \theta).$$

The maximum $\lambda_1$ is attained at $\varphi = \theta$, the minimum $\lambda_2$ a quarter turn later, and every value in between is attained. In the extreme case $\lambda_1 = \lambda_2$ the quotient is constant: $A = \lambda_1 I$ and every direction is an eigenvector.

Let $A$ be Hermitian with eigenvalues $\lambda_1 \ge \dots \ge \lambda_n$. For every $x \ne 0$, $\lambda_n \le R_A(x) \le \lambda_1$, with $R_A(x) = \lambda_1$ if and only if $Ax = \lambda_1 x$, and $R_A(x) = \lambda_n$ if and only if $Ax = \lambda_n x$. For real symmetric $A$ the gradient is $\nabla R_A(x) = \frac{2}{x^\T x}\big(Ax - R_A(x)\,x\big)$, so the critical points of $R_A$ are exactly the eigenvectors, and the critical values are the eigenvalues.

Courant–Fischer converts spectral statements into optimization statements, and three properties of Hermitian spectra follow at once: eigenvalues are $1$-Lipschitz functions of the matrix in the spectral norm (), monotone in the Loewner order (), and interlace under compression to a subspace (, ). For a covariance matrix $C$, $R_C(x)$ is the variance of the data along the unit direction $x$, so $\lambda_1(C)$ is the largest variance of any one-dimensional projection and $q_1$ its direction (; see ). The singular value decomposition

A rectangular $A \in \R^{m\times n}$ has no eigenvalues, and a square non-normal one has no orthonormal eigenbasis (). Find orthonormal bases of the input space and of the output space in which every matrix becomes diagonal.

Look at images of the unit circle. $\diag(3, 1)$ gives an ellipse with axes $3$ and $1$ on the coordinate axes. A rotation gives the circle back. The shear $S = \begin{bmatrix}1 & 1\\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}$ gives a tilted ellipse whose axes are not the images of $e_1$ and $e_2$. A rank-one matrix $uv^\T$ flattens the circle onto a segment of the line through $u$. In each case the image is an ellipse, possibly degenerate, and the longest semi-axis has length $\max_{\norm{x} = 1}\norm{Ax}$.

Every matrix maps the unit sphere onto a possibly degenerate ellipsoid, and the vectors that land on its principal semi-axes are mutually orthogonal.

Matrix norms and low-rank approximation

Measure the size of a matrix, and find the matrix of rank at most $k$ that is closest to a given $A$. This is compression, denoising and dimensionality reduction in one question.

For $A \in \R^{m\times n}$, the spectral norm is the largest stretch $\norm{A}_2 = \max_{x\ne 0}\norm{Ax}/\norm{x}$, and the Frobenius norm is $\norm{A}_F = \big(\sum_{i,j}a_{ij}^2\big)^{1/2} = (\tr A^\T A)^{1/2}$, where $\tr$ is the trace (sum of the diagonal entries); the two expressions agree because the diagonal entries of $A^\T A$ are the squared lengths of the columns of $A$. For complex $A$, use $\abs{a_{ij}}^2$ and $A^*$ in place of $a_{ij}^2$ and $A^\T$. Chapter 5 treats induced norms in general.

$\norm{A}_2 = \sigma_1$ and $\norm{A}_F = \big(\sum_i \sigma_i^2\big)^{1/2}$. Both norms are unitarily invariant: $\norm{U'AV'} = \norm{A}$ for orthogonal $U'$, $V'$. If $A$ is square and invertible, $\norm{A^{-1}}_2 = 1/\sigma_n$. For complex $A$ the same holds with unitary $U'$, $V'$.

Proof. Orthogonal matrices preserve vector norms, so $\norm{U'AV'x} = \norm{A(V'x)}$ with $\norm{V'x} = \norm{x}$, which gives invariance of $\norm{\cdot}_2$, and

$$\tr\big((U'AV')^\T U'AV'\big) = \tr(V'^\T A^\T AV') = \tr(A^\T A)$$

gives invariance of $\norm{\cdot}_F$, using $\tr(XY) = \tr(YX)$. Hence both norms of $A$ equal those of $\Sigma$. For $\Sigma$, $\norm{\Sigma y}^2 = \sum_i\sigma_i^2y_i^2 \le \sigma_1^2\norm{y}^2$ with equality at $y = e_1$, and $\norm{\Sigma}_F^2 = \sum_i\sigma_i^2$. Finally $A^{-1} = V\Sigma^{-1}U^\T$ has largest singular value $1/\sigma_n$. The complex case is the same argument with $^*$ in place of $^\T$.

Extreme cases. For nonzero $u$ and $v$, the rank-one matrix $uv^\T = \frac{u}{\norm u}\,\big(\norm u\norm v\big)\,\frac{v^\T}{\norm v}$ has the single nonzero singular value $\norm{u}\norm{v}$, so both norms agree. The identity $I_n$ has $\norm{I}_2 = 1$ but $\norm{I}_F = \sqrt n$. The general comparison sits between these two extremes.

For $A \in \R^{m\times n}$ of rank $r$, $\norm{A}_2 \le \norm{A}_F \le \sqrt{r}\,\norm{A}_2$. Equality holds on the left if and only if $r \le 1$, and on the right if and only if all nonzero singular values of $A$ are equal, for instance for multiples of orthogonal matrices.

Proof. For $A = 0$ all three quantities vanish. Otherwise, by , $\norm{A}_F^2 = \sum_{i \le r}\sigma_i^2$ is a sum of $r \ge 1$ terms in $(0, \sigma_1^2]$ and $\norm{A}_2^2 = \sigma_1^2$. The sum is at least its first term $\sigma_1^2$, with equality exactly when there are no further terms, and at most $r\sigma_1^2$, with equality exactly when every term equals $\sigma_1^2$.

Let $A \in \R^{m\times n}$ have rank $r$ and SVD $A = \sum_{i\le r}\sigma_iu_iv_i^\T$. For $0 \le k < r$ let $A_k = \sum_{i=1}^k \sigma_iu_iv_i^\T$. Then

$$\min_{\rank B \le k}\norm{A - B}_2 = \norm{A - A_k}_2 = \sigma_{k+1},\qquad \min_{\rank B \le k}\norm{A - B}_F = \norm{A - A_k}_F = \Big(\sum_{i=k+1}^r\sigma_i^2\Big)^{1/2}.$$

Proof for the spectral norm. $A - A_k = \sum_{i>k}\sigma_iu_iv_i^\T$ is an SVD with largest singular value $\sigma_{k+1}$. Now let $\rank B \le k$. Then $\dim\Null(B) \ge n - k$, and $\spn(v_1,\dots,v_{k+1})$ has dimension $k + 1$, so the two subspaces share a unit vector $x = \sum_{i\le k+1}c_iv_i$. For it,

$$\norm{(A - B)x} = \norm{Ax} = \Big(\sum_{i \le k+1}\sigma_i^2c_i^2\Big)^{1/2} \ge \sigma_{k+1}.$$
Proof for the Frobenius norm

Set $\sigma_l(M) = 0$ for $l > \min(m, n)$. The argument above shows $\norm{M - C}_2 \ge \sigma_{l+1}(M)$ for every $M \in \R^{m\times n}$, every $l \ge 0$ and every $C$ with $\rank C \le l$ (trivially when $\sigma_{l+1}(M) = 0$), and $\norm{M - M_l}_2 = \sigma_{l+1}(M)$ for the truncated SVD $M_l$ (with $M_l = M$ when $l \ge \rank M$). This gives Weyl's inequality for singular values: for $X, Y \in \R^{m\times n}$ and $i, j \ge 1$, the matrix $X_{i-1} + Y_{j-1}$ has rank at most $i + j - 2$, so

$$\sigma_{i+j-1}(X + Y) \le \norm{(X - X_{i-1}) + (Y - Y_{j-1})}_2 \le \sigma_i(X) + \sigma_j(Y).$$

With $X = A - B$, $Y = B$ and $j = k+1$, so that $\sigma_{k+1}(B) = 0$, it gives $\sigma_{i+k}(A) \le \sigma_i(A - B)$ for every $i$. Squaring and summing,

$$\norm{A - B}_F^2 = \sum_i\sigma_i(A-B)^2 \ge \sum_{i}\sigma_{i+k}(A)^2 = \norm{A - A_k}_F^2.$$

In the spectral norm the minimizer of need not be unique. For $A = \diag(3, 1)$ and $k = 1$, every $B = \diag(3 + t, 0)$ with $\abs{t} \le 1$ has rank one, $A - B = \diag(-t, 1)$, and $\norm{A - B}_2 = \max(\abs t, 1) = 1 = \sigma_2$.

For square $A \in \R^{n\times n}$, $\min\{\norm{E}_2 : A + E \text{ singular}\} = \sigma_n$, attained by $E = -\sigma_nu_nv_n^\T$. If $A$ is invertible, the distance relative to $\norm{A}_2 = \sigma_1$ is $\sigma_n/\sigma_1 = 1/\kappa(A)$, the reciprocal of the condition number of .

Proof. If $A$ is singular, $\sigma_n = 0$ and $E = 0$ works. Otherwise $r = n$, and $A + E$ is singular exactly when $\rank(A + E) \le n - 1$; apply with $k = n - 1 < r$ and $B = A + E$, whose minimizer $B = A_{n-1}$ gives $E = A_{n-1} - A = -\sigma_nu_nv_n^\T$.

The condition number of a square invertible matrix is $\kappa(A) = \norm{A}_2\norm{A^{-1}}_2 = \sigma_1/\sigma_n$ (). The numerical rank of $A$ at tolerance $\tau > 0$ is the number of singular values larger than $\tau$.

Let $A \in \R^{m\times n}$, $\tau > 0$, and let $k$ be the numerical rank of $A$ at tolerance $\tau$. Then $k = \min\{\rank B : \norm{A - B}_2 \le \tau\}$, and the truncated SVD $A_k$ attains the minimum.

Proof. With $\sigma_l = 0$ for $l > \rank A$, $\norm{A - A_k}_2 = \sigma_{k+1} \le \tau$ (; $A_k = A$ if $k = \rank A$). If $k \ge 1$ and $\rank B \le k - 1$, then $\norm{A - B}_2 \ge \sigma_k > \tau$ by with $k - 1$ in place of $k$.

For $A = \begin{bmatrix}1 & 10\\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}$, $A^\T A = \begin{bmatrix}1 & 10\\ 10 & 101\end{bmatrix}$ has trace $102$ and determinant $1$, so $\sigma_{1,2}^2 = 51 \pm\sqrt{2600}$: $\sigma_1 \approx 10.099$, $\sigma_2 = 1/\sigma_1 \approx 0.0990$ and $\kappa(A) \approx 102$. A perturbation of spectral norm $\sigma_2 \approx 0.099$ makes $A$ singular (), although $\det A = 1$. Chapter 1 warned that a small determinant does not mean "nearly singular"; this is the converse warning.

Invariants and equivalences. Two $m\times n$ matrices are unitarily equivalent, $B = U'AV'$, if and only if they have the same singular values, and every unitarily invariant quantity, including both norms above, is a function of the singular values (): the SVD is the normal form $\Sigma$ of this action, as in the table of chapter 1. For data, the same factorization diagonalizes the covariance: principal components are an SVD.

Let $X \in \R^{N\times d}$, with $N \ge 2$, have centred columns ($N$ samples of $d$ variables, each variable with mean zero), let $X = U\Sigma V^\T$ be an SVD, and let $C = X^\T X/(N-1)$ be the sample covariance. Then

$$C = V\,\frac{\Sigma^\T\Sigma}{N-1}\,V^\T,$$

so the right singular vectors $v_i$ are orthonormal eigenvectors of $C$ (the principal axes), with variances $\lambda_i(C) = \sigma_i^2/(N-1)$, where $\sigma_i = 0$ for $i > \min(N, d)$. For a unit vector $x$, the sample variance of the projected data $Xx$ is $R_C(x)$, largest for $x = v_1$, and the coordinates of the samples along $v_i$ (the principal component scores) are $Xv_i = \sigma_iu_i$ for $i \le \min(N, d)$.

Proof. $X^\T X = V\Sigma^\T U^\T U\Sigma V^\T = V\Sigma^\T\Sigma V^\T$, and $\Sigma^\T\Sigma$ is diagonal with entries $\sigma_i^2$. The entries of $Xx$ have mean zero because the columns of $X$ do, so their sample variance is $\norm{Xx}^2/(N-1) = x^\T Cx$, which is $R_C(x)$ for unit $x$; its maximum over unit vectors is $\lambda_1(C)$, attained at $v_1$ (). Finally $XV = U\Sigma$ column by column.

Heuristically, a population recording "is low-dimensional" when its singular values fall quickly to a noise floor, so that a few principal components carry most of the variance; makes such a floor visible.

Eigenvalues versus singular values

Eigenvalues decide what $A^k$ and $e^{tA}$ do in the long run; singular values decide how much $A$ can amplify a vector in one application. Determine what each set says about the other.

Three extreme cases. For a normal matrix the singular values are the moduli of the eigenvalues (part (4) of below). An orthogonal matrix $Q$ has all singular values equal to $1$, since $Q^\T Q = I$, and eigenvalues on the unit circle (). The nilpotent $N = \begin{bmatrix}0 & M\\ 0 & 0\end{bmatrix}$, with $M > 0$, has both eigenvalues $0$ but $\sigma_1 = M$: arbitrarily large gain with zero spectrum.

Let $A \in \C^{n\times n}$ have eigenvalues ordered so that $\abs{\lambda_1} \ge \dots \ge \abs{\lambda_n}$, and singular values $\sigma_1 \ge \dots \ge \sigma_n$. Then

  1. $\sigma_n \le \abs{\lambda_j} \le \sigma_1$ for every $j$; in particular the spectral radius $\rho(A) = \abs{\lambda_1}$ satisfies $\rho(A) \le \norm{A}_2$;
  2. $\prod_j\abs{\lambda_j} = \abs{\det A} = \prod_j\sigma_j$;
  3. $\sum_j\abs{\lambda_j}^2 \le \sum_j\sigma_j^2 = \norm{A}_F^2$ (Schur's inequality), with equality if and only if $A$ is normal;
  4. $A$ is normal if and only if $\sigma_j = \abs{\lambda_j}$ for every $j$.

Proof. (1) For a unit eigenvector $x$, $\abs{\lambda} = \norm{Ax}$, and $\sigma_n \le \norm{Ax} \le \sigma_1$ for every unit $x$, by expanding $x$ in the right singular vectors. (2) $\det A = \prod_j\lambda_j$, and $\abs{\det A} = \abs{\det U}\,\det\Sigma\,\abs{\det V^*} = \prod_j\sigma_j$. (3) With the Schur form $A = UTU^*$, $\norm{A}_F^2 = \norm{T}_F^2 = \sum_j\abs{\lambda_j}^2 + \sum_{j<k}\abs{t_{jk}}^2$ (as in the departure-from-normality proposition of chapter 3), with equality exactly when $T$ is diagonal, that is, when $A$ is normal (). (4) If $A = Q\Lambda Q^*$ is normal, write $\lambda_j = \abs{\lambda_j}e^{i\theta_j}$ and $D = \diag(e^{i\theta_j})$ (with $\theta_j = 0$ when $\lambda_j = 0$); then $A = (QD)\,\abs{\Lambda}\,Q^*$ is an SVD after reordering, so the singular values are the $\abs{\lambda_j}$. Conversely, equal lists give equality in (3).

With the orderings of , $\prod_{j=1}^k\abs{\lambda_j} \le \prod_{j=1}^k\sigma_j$ for $k = 1,\dots,n$, with equality for $k = n$.

Idea: apply part (1) of to the $k$-th compound matrix (the action of $A$ on $k$-dimensional volumes), whose eigenvalues are products of $k$ eigenvalues of $A$ and whose largest singular value is $\sigma_1\cdots\sigma_k$. Eigenvalues do not measure gain. $A = \begin{bmatrix}1 & 10\\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}$ has eigenvalues $1, 1$ and singular values $\approx 10.1$ and $0.099$ (): the products agree, as part (2) of requires, while the singular values differ from the eigenvalues by a factor of about $10$ in each direction. Its powers $A^k = \begin{bmatrix}1 & 10k\\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}$ have $\norm{A^k}_2 \ge \abs{e_1^\T A^ke_2} = 10k$, unbounded although $\rho(A) = 1$. For the transient behaviour of $e^{tA}$, chapter 6 replaces eigenvalues by pseudospectra and by the numerical abscissa.

Eigenvalues of non-normal matrices can be very sensitive (chapter 6). Singular values behave better, for a reason that the next proposition makes precise: they are the eigenvalues of a Hermitian matrix built from $A$, to which Weyl's inequality applies.

Let $A \in \F^{m\times n}$, $p = \min(m, n)$, and let $H(A) = \begin{bmatrix}0 & A\\ A^* & 0\end{bmatrix} \in \F^{(m+n)\times(m+n)}$ be its Hermitian dilation. (i) $H(A)$ is Hermitian with eigenvalues $\pm\sigma_1(A), \dots, \pm\sigma_p(A)$ and $\abs{m - n}$ zeros, and the stacked vector $(u_j, \pm v_j)/\sqrt2 \in \F^{m+n}$ is a unit eigenvector for $\pm\sigma_j(A)$, $j \le p$. (ii) For every $E \in \F^{m\times n}$ and every $j \le p$, $\abs{\sigma_j(A + E) - \sigma_j(A)} \le \norm{E}_2$, whether or not $A$ is normal.

Proof. (i) From $Av_j = \sigma_ju_j$ and $A^*u_j = \sigma_jv_j$, $H(A)(u_j, \pm v_j) = \pm\sigma_j(u_j, \pm v_j)$. If $m > n$, the vectors $(u_j, 0)$ with $j > n$ are null vectors of $H(A)$, because $\norm{A^*u_j}^2 = u_j^*AA^*u_j = (\Sigma\Sigma^\T)_{jj} = 0$; if $n > m$, so are the $(0, v_j)$ with $j > m$, because $\norm{Av_j}^2 = (\Sigma^\T\Sigma)_{jj} = 0$. These $m + n$ vectors are orthonormal, so they form a complete eigenbasis and the list of eigenvalues is complete. (ii) By (i), the $j$-th largest eigenvalue of $H(A)$ is $\sigma_j(A)$ for $j \le p$, and $\norm{H(E)}_2 = \sigma_1(E) = \norm{E}_2$ by and . Since $H(A + E) = H(A) + H(E)$, gives the bound.

Self-check

  1. Show that a real skew-symmetric matrix $K$ has purely imaginary eigenvalues and that $e^{tK}$ is orthogonal for every real $t$.

    Answer

    $iK$ is Hermitian: $(iK)^* = -iK^\T = iK$. Its eigenvalues are real by , so those of $K$ are $-i$ times real numbers (as predicts for skew-Hermitian matrices). Transposing the exponential series term by term gives $(e^{tK})^\T = e^{tK^\T} = e^{-tK}$, and since $-tK$ commutes with $tK$, $(e^{tK})^\T e^{tK} = e^{-tK}e^{tK} = I$ (chapter 7). Moreover $\det e^{tK} = e^{t\tr K} = 1$, because the diagonal of $K$ is zero, so skew-symmetric generators produce rotations.

  2. Is $A = \begin{bmatrix}2 & 3\\ 3 & 4\end{bmatrix}$ positive definite? Answer with Sylvester's criterion, then check with the eigenvalues.

    Answer

    No: $\Delta_1 = 2 > 0$ but $\Delta_2 = 8 - 9 = -1 < 0$, so $A$ is indefinite (). The eigenvalues are $3 \pm\sqrt{10} \approx 6.16$ and $-0.16$, with product $-1$.

  3. Find the singular values of $A = \begin{bmatrix}3 & 0\\ 4 & 5\end{bmatrix}$, then $\norm{A}_2$, $\norm{A}_F$, $\kappa(A)$ and the distance from $A$ to the nearest singular matrix.

    Answer

    $A^\T A = \begin{bmatrix}25 & 20\\ 20 & 25\end{bmatrix}$ has eigenvalues $45$ and $5$, with $v_1 = (1,1)/\sqrt2$ and $v_2 = (1,-1)/\sqrt2$. So $\sigma_1 = 3\sqrt5 \approx 6.71$, $\sigma_2 = \sqrt5 \approx 2.24$, $\norm{A}_2 = 3\sqrt5$, $\norm{A}_F = \sqrt{50}$ (also $\sqrt{9 + 16 + 25}$), $\kappa(A) = 3$, and the distance to singularity is $\sigma_2 = \sqrt5$ (). Check: $\sigma_1\sigma_2 = 15 = \det A$, and $u_1 = Av_1/\sigma_1 = (1, 3)/\sqrt{10}$, $u_2 = (3, -1)/\sqrt{10}$.

  4. A symmetric $3\times 3$ matrix has eigenvalues $5$, $-7$ and $2$. Give its singular values, $\norm{A}_2$, a best rank-one approximation, and the errors of that approximation in both norms.

    Answer

    Singular values $7, 5, 2$ (moduli, reordered; ), so $\norm{A}_2 = 7$. A best rank-one approximation in both norms () is $-7\,qq^\T$ with $q$ the unit eigenvector for $-7$: ordering is by $\abs{\lambda}$, not by $\lambda$. Errors: $\sigma_2 = 5$ in the spectral norm and $\sqrt{25 + 4} = \sqrt{29}$ in the Frobenius norm.

  5. A real symmetric $3\times 3$ matrix has eigenvalues $4, 1, -2$. Where can the eigenvalues of its upper-left $2\times 2$ block lie? Can its $(1,1)$ entry be $5$?

    Answer

    By , the block's eigenvalues $\mu_1 \ge \mu_2$ satisfy $\mu_1 \in [1, 4]$ and $\mu_2 \in [-2, 1]$. The $(1,1)$ entry is $R_A(e_1) \in [-2, 4]$ (), so it cannot be $5$; a non-symmetric matrix can violate this, as shows.
