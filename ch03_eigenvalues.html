Eigenvalues, diagonalization and Jordan form

An eigenvector is a direction that a matrix only stretches. With enough of them the matrix splits into independent scalar modes and its powers can be read off the eigenvalues; without enough, Jordan blocks take over and polynomial factors appear beside the geometric ones. In both cases the spectral radius decides whether $A^k$ decays.

Eigenvalues and eigenvectors

Find the directions that a square matrix maps into themselves, and the factor by which it stretches each one.

Let $A \in \C^{n\times n}$, where $\C$ is the complex numbers. A scalar $\lambda \in \C$ is an eigenvalue of $A$ if $Av = \lambda v$ for some nonzero $v \in \C^n$, called an eigenvector for $\lambda$. The eigenspace $E_\lambda = \Null(A - \lambda I)$, with $\Null$ the null space and $I$ the identity, consists of the eigenvectors for $\lambda$ together with $0$. The spectrum $\Lambda(A)$ is the set of eigenvalues. A real matrix is treated as a complex one, so its eigenvalues may be nonreal.

$\lambda$ is an eigenvalue of $A$ if and only if $\det(\lambda I - A) = 0$, where $\det$ is the determinant. The characteristic polynomial $p_A(\lambda) = \det(\lambda I - A)$ is monic of degree $n$, so by the fundamental theorem of algebra

$$p_A(\lambda) = \prod_{i=1}^n (\lambda - \lambda_i)$$

for complex numbers $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n$, the eigenvalues listed with multiplicity. If $A$ is real, its nonreal eigenvalues come in conjugate pairs $\lambda$, $\bar\lambda$ with conjugate eigenvectors $v$, $\bar v$, where the bar is complex conjugation.

Proof. $Av = \lambda v$ with $v \ne 0$ says $\Null(\lambda I - A) \ne \{0\}$, which holds exactly when $\lambda I - A$ is singular, that is, when its determinant vanishes (the determinant theorem, chapter 1). In the permutation expansion of $\det(\lambda I - A)$, every term is a product of $n$ entries, one from each column, and only the product of the diagonal entries $\prod_i(\lambda - a_{ii})$ has degree $n$ in $\lambda$, with leading term $\lambda^n$. For real $A$, conjugating $Av = \lambda v$ gives $A\bar v = \bar\lambda\bar v$; moreover $p_A$ has real coefficients, so conjugating its factorization gives $p_A(\lambda) = \prod_i(\lambda - \bar\lambda_i)$, and by uniqueness of the factorization the list $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n$ is closed under conjugation, multiplicities included.

$\tr A = \sum_i \lambda_i$ and $\det A = \prod_i \lambda_i$, where $\tr A = \sum_i a_{ii}$ is the trace.

Proof. Expanding the product, and evaluating at $\lambda = 0$,

$$p_A(\lambda) = \lambda^n - \Big(\sum_i \lambda_i\Big)\lambda^{n-1} + \dots + (-1)^n\prod_i \lambda_i, \qquad p_A(0) = \det(-A) = (-1)^n\det A.$$

This gives the determinant. A permutation other than the identity moves at least two indices, so its term in the expansion misses at least two diagonal factors and has degree at most $n - 2$; hence the coefficient of $\lambda^{n-1}$ comes from $\prod_i(\lambda - a_{ii})$ alone and equals $-\tr A$.

The rotation $R_\theta$ has $p(\lambda) = \lambda^2 - 2\cos\theta\,\lambda + 1$, with roots $\cos\theta \pm i\sin\theta = e^{\pm i\theta}$, where $i$ is the imaginary unit (engineering texts write $j$); indeed $R_\theta(1, -i)^\T = e^{i\theta}(1, -i)^\T$.

Two lessons, from the rotation and the shear: a real matrix may have no real eigenvalue, and an $n\times n$ matrix can have fewer than $n$ independent eigenvectors.

If $B = S^{-1}AS$ with $S$ invertible, then $p_B = p_A$. Hence similar matrices have the same eigenvalues with the same multiplicities, the same trace and the same determinant, and $v$ is an eigenvector of $A$ exactly when $S^{-1}v$ is an eigenvector of $B$ for the same eigenvalue. Also $\rank(B - \lambda I)^k = \rank(A - \lambda I)^k$ for every $\lambda \in \C$ and every $k \ge 1$; in particular, the eigenspaces of $A$ and $B$ for each eigenvalue have the same dimension. Moreover, if $Av = \lambda v$ then $f(A)v = f(\lambda)v$ for every polynomial $f$, and $A^{-1}v = \lambda^{-1}v$ when $A$ is invertible.

Proof. By multiplicativity of the determinant,

$$\det(\lambda I - S^{-1}AS) = \det\big(S^{-1}(\lambda I - A)S\big) = \det(\lambda I - A),$$

and $B(S^{-1}v) = S^{-1}Av$. Next, $(B - \lambda I)^k = S^{-1}(A - \lambda I)^kS$, and for every square matrix $M$, $\Range(S^{-1}MS) = S^{-1}\Range(M)$ because $S$ is onto, while $S^{-1}$ is injective; so $S^{-1}MS$ and $M$ have the same rank, and the eigenspace dimensions $n - \rank(A - \lambda I)$ agree by rank–nullity (chapter 1). From $Av = \lambda v$, induction gives $A^kv = \lambda^kv$, and linear combinations give $f(A)v = f(\lambda)v$; if $A$ is invertible then $\lambda \ne 0$ and applying $A^{-1}$ to $Av = \lambda v$ gives the last claim.

Eigenvalues neither add nor multiply. $A = \begin{bmatrix}0 & 1\\ 0 & 0\end{bmatrix}$ and $B = \begin{bmatrix}0 & 0\\ 1 & 0\end{bmatrix}$ both have spectrum $\{0\}$, yet $A + B = \begin{bmatrix}0 & 1\\ 1 & 0\end{bmatrix}$ has spectrum $\{1, -1\}$ and $AB = \begin{bmatrix}1 & 0\\ 0 & 0\end{bmatrix}$ has spectrum $\{1, 0\}$. For commuting matrices, in contrast, the eigenvalues of $A + B$ and of $AB$ are sums and products of eigenvalues of $A$ and of $B$ under a suitable pairing ().

Let $M = \begin{bmatrix}M_{11} & M_{12}\\ 0 & M_{22}\end{bmatrix}$ with square diagonal blocks $M_{11}$ and $M_{22}$. Then $\det M = \det M_{11}\,\det M_{22}$ and $p_M = p_{M_{11}}\,p_{M_{22}}$, and the same holds for block lower triangular $M$. In particular, the eigenvalues of a triangular matrix, listed with multiplicity, are its diagonal entries.

Proof. Factor $M = \begin{bmatrix}I & 0\\ 0 & M_{22}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}M_{11} & M_{12}\\ 0 & I\end{bmatrix}$ and use multiplicativity of the determinant (chapter 1). In the permutation expansion of the first factor, a nonzero term must fix every index of the identity block, because those rows have a single nonzero entry, on the diagonal; so the expansion reduces to that of $\det M_{22}$. Likewise the second factor has determinant $\det M_{11}$. Applied to $\lambda I - M = \begin{bmatrix}\lambda I - M_{11} & -M_{12}\\ 0 & \lambda I - M_{22}\end{bmatrix}$ this gives $p_M = p_{M_{11}}p_{M_{22}}$; transposition, which leaves determinants unchanged, handles the lower triangular case; and splitting off one diagonal entry at a time gives $p_T(\lambda) = \prod_i(\lambda - t_{ii})$ for a triangular $T$.

For $A \in \C^{m\times n}$ and $B \in \C^{n\times m}$,

$$\lambda^n\,p_{AB}(\lambda) = \lambda^m\,p_{BA}(\lambda).$$

So $AB$ and $BA$ have the same nonzero eigenvalues with the same algebraic multiplicities, and $p_{AB} = p_{BA}$ when $m = n$.

Proof. With $S = \begin{bmatrix}I_m & A\\ 0 & I_n\end{bmatrix}$, whose inverse is $\begin{bmatrix}I_m & -A\\ 0 & I_n\end{bmatrix}$, block multiplication gives

$$S^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}AB & 0\\ B & 0\end{bmatrix}S = \begin{bmatrix}0 & 0\\ B & BA\end{bmatrix}.$$

Both block matrices are block lower triangular, so by and their common characteristic polynomial is $p_{AB}(\lambda)\,\lambda^n = \lambda^m\,p_{BA}(\lambda)$.

(i) For $A \in \C^{m\times n}$, with $A^*$ its conjugate transpose, $A^*A$ and $AA^*$ have the same nonzero eigenvalues with the same algebraic multiplicities; for real $A$ these are $A^\T A$ and $AA^\T$. (ii) For $u, v \in \C^n$, $p_{uv^\T}(\lambda) = \lambda^{n-1}(\lambda - v^\T u)$: the eigenvalues of $uv^\T$ are $v^\T u$ and $0$, the latter repeated $n - 1$ times.

Proof. Apply to the pair $A$, $A^*$, and to the pair $u \in \C^{n\times 1}$, $v^\T \in \C^{1\times n}$, for which it gives $\lambda\,p_{uv^\T}(\lambda) = \lambda^n\,p_{v^\T u}(\lambda) = \lambda^n(\lambda - v^\T u)$.

In chapter 4 the square roots of the eigenvalues of $A^*A$ are the singular values of $A$, so the nonzero eigenvalues in (i) are the squared nonzero singular values.

$AB$ and $BA$ need not be similar. With $A = \begin{bmatrix}1 & 0\\ 0 & 0\end{bmatrix}$ and $B = \begin{bmatrix}0 & 1\\ 0 & 0\end{bmatrix}$, $AB = \begin{bmatrix}0 & 1\\ 0 & 0\end{bmatrix} \ne 0$ while $BA = 0$. Both have characteristic polynomial $\lambda^2$, but only the zero matrix is similar to the zero matrix. The difference sits in the eigenvalue $0$: $\Null(AB) = \spn(e_1)$ is one-dimensional, while $\Null(BA) = \C^2$.

The $2\times 2$ anatomy

Read the whole eigenstructure of a real $2\times 2$ matrix from two numbers.

Let $A = \begin{bmatrix}a & b\\ c & d\end{bmatrix}$ be real, with trace $\tau = a + d$ and determinant $\Delta = ad - bc$. Then $p_A(\lambda) = \lambda^2 - \tau\lambda + \Delta$, and

$$\lambda_\pm = \frac{\tau}{2} \pm \sqrt{D},\qquad D = \frac{\tau^2}{4} - \Delta = \frac{(a - d)^2}{4} + bc.$$

If $D > 0$ the eigenvalues are real and distinct; if $D = 0$ there is one real eigenvalue $\tau/2$ of algebraic multiplicity two; if $D < 0$ they are the conjugate pair $\tau/2 \pm i\sqrt{-D}$.

Proof. Expand the determinant and simplify the discriminant:

$$\det(\lambda I - A) = (\lambda - a)(\lambda - d) - bc = \lambda^2 - \tau\lambda + \Delta,\qquad \frac{\tau^2}{4} - \Delta = \frac{(a + d)^2}{4} - ad + bc = \frac{(a - d)^2}{4} + bc.$$

The quadratic formula gives the roots.

Let $A \in \R^{2\times 2}$ have trace $\tau$, determinant $\Delta$, discriminant $D = \tau^2/4 - \Delta$ and eigenvalues $\lambda_\pm = \tau/2 \pm \sqrt D$. (i) If $\Delta < 0$, then $D > 0$ and $\lambda_- < 0 < \lambda_+$. (ii) If $\Delta > 0$ and $D \ge 0$, both eigenvalues are real, nonzero and of the sign of $\tau$. (iii) If $\Delta = 0$, the eigenvalues are $0$ and $\tau$. (iv) Both eigenvalues have negative real part if and only if $\tau < 0$ and $\Delta > 0$.

Proof. By , $\lambda_+ + \lambda_- = \tau$ and $\lambda_+\lambda_- = \Delta$. (i) $D = \tau^2/4 - \Delta > 0$, so the eigenvalues are real, and their product is negative. (ii) They are real because $D \ge 0$, nonzero and of one sign because their product is positive, and their sum is $\tau$. (iii) $p_A(\lambda) = \lambda(\lambda - \tau)$. (iv) If $D < 0$, both eigenvalues have real part $\tau/2$, and $\Delta = \lambda_+\bar\lambda_+ = \abs{\lambda_+}^2 > 0$ holds automatically. If $D \ge 0$, two real numbers are both negative exactly when their sum is negative and their product positive.

For $x = (x_1, x_2)^\T \in \R^2$,

$$\det\begin{bmatrix}x & Ax\end{bmatrix} = c\,x_1^2 + (d - a)\,x_1x_2 - b\,x_2^2,$$

which vanishes for $x \ne 0$ exactly when $Ax$ is parallel to $x$, that is, when $x$ spans a real eigendirection. Unless $A$ is a multiple of $I$ (when the form vanishes identically), there are two real eigendirections if $D > 0$, and the form changes sign across each of them; exactly one if $D = 0$, and the form has the same sign on both sides of it; and none if $D < 0$.

Proof. Expand: $x_1(cx_1 + dx_2) - x_2(ax_1 + bx_2)$. The determinant of two vectors vanishes exactly when they are dependent, and $x \ne 0$. A nonzero binary quadratic form $\alpha x_1^2 + \beta x_1x_2 + \gamma x_2^2$ with $\beta^2 - 4\alpha\gamma > 0$ is a product of two non-proportional real linear forms, so it vanishes on two lines and changes sign across each; with $\beta^2 - 4\alpha\gamma = 0$ it is a nonzero multiple of the square of one linear form, so it vanishes on one line without changing sign; with $\beta^2 - 4\alpha\gamma < 0$ it vanishes only at the origin. Here $\beta^2 - 4\alpha\gamma = (d - a)^2 + 4bc = 4D$. The form vanishes identically exactly when $b = c = 0$ and $a = d$.

Let $A \in \R^{n\times n}$ have an eigenvalue $\lambda = \alpha + i\beta$ with $\beta \ne 0$, and let $v = u + iw$ with $u, w \in \R^n$ be an eigenvector for $\lambda$. Then $u$ and $w$ are linearly independent, and $Aw = \alpha w + \beta u$, $Au = -\beta w + \alpha u$, so $A$ maps the real plane $\spn(w, u)$ into itself. For $n = 2$, with $S = \begin{bmatrix}w & u\end{bmatrix}$,

$$S^{-1}AS = \begin{bmatrix}\alpha & -\beta\\ \beta & \alpha\end{bmatrix} = r\,R_\phi,$$

where $r = \sqrt{\alpha^2 + \beta^2} = \abs{\lambda}$ and $\phi$ is the argument of $\lambda$. So a real $2\times 2$ matrix with nonreal eigenvalues is a rotation by $\phi$ combined with a scaling by $r$, seen in a skewed basis.

Proof. Separating real and imaginary parts of $A(u + iw) = (\alpha + i\beta)(u + iw)$ gives $Aw = \alpha w + \beta u$ and $Au = -\beta w + \alpha u$, which for $n = 2$ are the two columns of the displayed matrix in the basis $(w, u)$. If $u$ and $w$ were dependent, $v$ would be a complex multiple of a real vector $y \ne 0$, and $Ay = \lambda y$ would have a real left side and a nonreal right side.

Diagonalization and modes

When does $A \in \C^{n\times n}$ decouple into $n$ independent scalar modes, that is, when does $\C^n$ have a basis of eigenvectors of $A$?

Eigenvectors $v_1, \dots, v_k$ for distinct eigenvalues $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_k$ are linearly independent.

Proof. Suppose not, and let $j$ be the smallest index with $v_j \in \spn(v_1, \dots, v_{j-1})$, say $v_j = \sum_{i<j} c_iv_i$; then $v_1, \dots, v_{j-1}$ are independent. Applying $A$ gives $\lambda_jv_j = \sum_{i<j} c_i\lambda_iv_i$, and subtracting $\lambda_j$ times the first relation gives $0 = \sum_{i<j} c_i(\lambda_i - \lambda_j)v_i$. Independence forces $c_i(\lambda_i - \lambda_j) = 0$, so every $c_i = 0$ because the eigenvalues are distinct. Then $v_j = 0$, which is impossible for an eigenvector.

The algebraic multiplicity $m_a(\lambda)$ of an eigenvalue is its multiplicity as a root of $p_A$; the geometric multiplicity is $m_g(\lambda) = \dim E_\lambda$. The matrix $A$ is diagonalizable if $A = V\Lambda V^{-1}$ with $V$ invertible and $\Lambda = \diag(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n)$ diagonal. Without an argument, $\Lambda$ denotes this diagonal matrix; $\Lambda(A)$ is the spectrum.

For every eigenvalue, $1 \le m_g(\lambda) \le m_a(\lambda)$.

Proof. Let $g = m_g(\lambda) \ge 1$. Take a basis $v_1, \dots, v_g$ of $E_\lambda$, extend it to a basis of $\C^n$, and let $S$ have these vectors as columns. Then $S^{-1}AS = \begin{bmatrix}\lambda I_g & X\\ 0 & Y\end{bmatrix}$ for some blocks $X$, $Y$, so by and $p_A(z) = (z - \lambda)^g\,p_Y(z)$, and $(z - \lambda)^g$ divides $p_A$.

For $A \in \C^{n\times n}$ the following are equivalent: (i) $A$ is diagonalizable; (ii) $\C^n$ has a basis of eigenvectors of $A$; (iii) $m_g(\lambda) = m_a(\lambda)$ for every eigenvalue. In particular, $n$ distinct eigenvalues suffice.

Proof. (i) $\Leftrightarrow$ (ii): $AV = V\Lambda$ says that column $j$ of $V$ is an eigenvector for $\Lambda_{jj}$, and $V$ is invertible exactly when its columns form a basis. (ii) $\Leftrightarrow$ (iii): by , a sum of vectors from eigenspaces of distinct eigenvalues vanishes only if each term does, so bases of the eigenspaces together form an independent list of length $\sum_\lambda m_g(\lambda)$, and eigenvectors span $\C^n$ exactly when this length is $n$. Since $\sum_\lambda m_a(\lambda) = n$ and $m_g \le m_a$ termwise (), the length is $n$ exactly when $m_g = m_a$ for every eigenvalue.

Let $u, v \in \R^n$ be nonzero, with $n \ge 2$. The eigenvectors of $uv^\T$ for the eigenvalue $0$ are the nonzero vectors of $v^\perp$, so $m_g(0) = n - 1$, and $uv^\T$ is diagonalizable if and only if $v^\T u \ne 0$. If $v^\T u = 0$, then $(uv^\T)^2 = 0$: the matrix is nilpotent, that is, some power of it vanishes.

Proof. $uv^\T x = (v^\T x)\,u$ vanishes exactly when $v^\T x = 0$, because $u \ne 0$, and this null space of the rank-one row $v^\T$ has dimension $n - 1$ by rank–nullity. By , if $v^\T u \ne 0$ the eigenvalues are $v^\T u$ with $m_a = 1$ and $0$ with $m_a = n - 1$, so $m_g = m_a$ for both and applies; if $v^\T u = 0$, then $m_a(0) = n > m_g(0)$. Finally $(uv^\T)^2 = u\,(v^\T u)\,v^\T$.

Modes. A nonzero row vector $w^*$ with $w^*A = \lambda w^*$ is a left eigenvector of $A$ for $\lambda$. In a basis of eigenvectors the recursion $x_{k+1} = Ax_k$ splits into scalar recursions, and the left eigenvectors read off the coordinates.

Let $A = V\Lambda V^{-1}$ be diagonalizable, with $V = \begin{bmatrix}v_1 & \cdots & v_n\end{bmatrix}$, and let $w_1^*, \dots, w_n^*$ be the rows of $V^{-1}$. Then (i) $w_i^*v_j = \delta_{ij}$, the Kronecker delta ($1$ if $i = j$ and $0$ otherwise), and $w_i^*A = \lambda_iw_i^*$, so $w_i^*$ is a left eigenvector for $\lambda_i$; (ii) for every $k \ge 0$,

$$A^k = V\Lambda^kV^{-1} = \sum_{i=1}^n \lambda_i^k\,v_iw_i^*,\qquad x_k = A^kx_0 = \sum_{i=1}^n (w_i^*x_0)\,\lambda_i^k\,v_i;$$

(iii) in the coordinates $y = V^{-1}x$ the recursion $x_{k+1} = Ax_k$ becomes the $n$ uncoupled scalar recursions $y_{i,k+1} = \lambda_i\,y_{i,k}$; (iv) $v_iw_i^*$ is the projector onto $\spn(v_i)$ along $\spn\{v_j : j \ne i\}$, in general an oblique one.

Let $A = V\Lambda V^{-1}$ be diagonalizable, let $f$ be a complex function defined on $\Lambda(A)$, and put $f(\Lambda) = \diag\big(f(\lambda_1), \dots, f(\lambda_n)\big)$. Then $Vf(\Lambda)V^{-1} = p(A)$ for every polynomial $p$ with $p(\lambda) = f(\lambda)$ for all $\lambda \in \Lambda(A)$, and such polynomials exist. Hence $f(A) = Vf(\Lambda)V^{-1}$ depends only on $A$ and on the values of $f$ on $\Lambda(A)$, not on the choice of $V$, and for a polynomial $f$ it is the usual $f(A)$.

Proof. The Lagrange polynomial interpolating $f$ at the distinct eigenvalues is such a $p$. Since every $\lambda_i$ lies in $\Lambda(A)$, $f(\Lambda) = p(\Lambda)$, and $Vp(\Lambda)V^{-1} = p(A)$ because $V\Lambda^kV^{-1} = A^k$ for every $k$ () and both sides are linear in $p$. The matrix $p(A)$ involves only $A$ and $p$.

Chapter 7 takes $f(\lambda) = e^{\lambda t}$ (the modal expansion of $e^{tA}$).

The recursion $F_{k+1} = F_k + F_{k-1}$, $F_0 = 0$, $F_1 = 1$, is $\begin{bmatrix}F_{k+1}\\ F_k\end{bmatrix} = A^k\begin{bmatrix}1\\ 0\end{bmatrix}$ with $A = \begin{bmatrix}1 & 1\\ 1 & 0\end{bmatrix}$. Its eigenvalues are the roots $\varphi = (1 + \sqrt5)/2$ and $\psi = (1 - \sqrt5)/2$ of $\lambda^2 = \lambda + 1$, with eigenvectors $(\lambda, 1)^\T$. Expanding $(1, 0)^\T = \big((\varphi, 1)^\T - (\psi, 1)^\T\big)/\sqrt5$ gives Binet's formula $F_k = (\varphi^k - \psi^k)/\sqrt5$. Because $\abs{\psi} < 1$, $F_k$ is the integer nearest to $\varphi^k/\sqrt5$, and the ratio $F_{k+1}/F_k$ approaches the dominant eigenvalue $\varphi$ with error shrinking like $\abs{\psi/\varphi}^k \approx 0.38^k$, a first instance of power iteration ().

Equal characteristic polynomials do not imply similarity. $I$ and $\begin{bmatrix}1 & 1\\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}$ both have $p(\lambda) = (\lambda - 1)^2$, but $S^{-1}IS = I$ for every invertible $S$, so the only matrix similar to $I$ is $I$ itself. The shear has $m_g(1) = 1 < 2 = m_a(1)$, which no change of basis can repair, since $m_g$ is a similarity invariant ().

Schur triangularization

If $A$ has no eigenvector basis, or only a badly conditioned one, what is the simplest form reachable by a change of basis that preserves lengths and angles?

Let $A \in \C^{n\times n}$, let $U$ be unitary ($U^*U = I$, where $U^*$ is the conjugate transpose) and $B = U^*AU$. Then (i) $\norm{B} = \norm{A}$ in the induced 2-norm $\norm{M} = \max_{x \ne 0}\norm{Mx}/\norm{x}$; (ii) $B^*B = BB^*$ if and only if $A^*A = AA^*$; (iii) $v \mapsto U^*v$ maps the eigenvectors of $A$ onto those of $B$, for the same eigenvalues, and preserves the inner product $\inner{x}{y} = y^*x$, hence angles between eigenvectors; if $A = V\Lambda V^{-1}$, then $B = (U^*V)\Lambda(U^*V)^{-1}$ and $\kappa(U^*V) = \kappa(V)$; (iv) $A$ and $B$ have the same singular values.

Proof. Unitary matrices preserve inner products and lengths (chapter 2). (i) $\norm{Bx} = \norm{AUx}$, $\norm{Ux} = \norm{x}$, and $Ux$ runs over all unit vectors as $x$ does. (ii) $B^*B - BB^* = U^*(A^*A - AA^*)U$. (iii) $B(U^*v) = U^*Av = \lambda\,U^*v$, every eigenvector $w$ of $B$ is $U^*(Uw)$ with $Uw$ an eigenvector of $A$, and $\inner{U^*x}{U^*y} = \inner{x}{y}$ for all $x, y$. As in (i), $\norm{U^*V} = \norm{V}$ and $\norm{(U^*V)^{-1}} = \norm{V^{-1}U} = \norm{V^{-1}}$. (iv) $B^*B = U^*(A^*A)U$ has the same eigenvalues as $A^*A$ (), and the singular values are their square roots.

Schur forms are what eigenvalue software computes. The QR algorithm applies a sequence of unitary similarities to drive $A$ toward triangular form (Trefethen and Bau, Numerical Linear Algebra, Lectures 24–29), and it is backward stable: the computed triangular factor is an exact Schur factor of a matrix $A + E$ with $\norm{E}$ of the order of the unit roundoff (the relative precision of the floating-point arithmetic) times $\norm{A}$ (Golub and Van Loan, Matrix Computations, 4th ed., ch. 7). Heuristically, unitary changes of basis are safe because they do not amplify perturbations: $\norm{UEU^*} = \norm{E}$ by . A perturbation $E$ in the coordinates of a diagonalization $A = V\Lambda V^{-1}$ becomes $VEV^{-1}$ instead, and $\norm{VEV^{-1}} \le \kappa(V)\norm{E}$, where $\kappa(V)$ can be huge ().

When diagonalization fails: Jordan form

What replaces the eigenvector basis when $m_g(\lambda) < m_a(\lambda)$ for some eigenvalue?

The smallest defective matrix is $J_2(\lambda) = \begin{bmatrix}\lambda & 1\\ 0 & \lambda\end{bmatrix} = \lambda I + N$ with $N = \begin{bmatrix}0 & 1\\ 0 & 0\end{bmatrix}$. Here $(J_2 - \lambda I)e_1 = 0$ but $(J_2 - \lambda I)e_2 = e_1$: $e_2$ is not an eigenvector, yet $(J_2 - \lambda I)^2e_2 = 0$. Because $\lambda I$ and $N$ commute and $N^2 = 0$, the binomial theorem gives $J_2(\lambda)^k = \lambda^kI + k\lambda^{k-1}N$: beside the geometric term $\lambda^k$ stands $k\lambda^{k-1}$, a polynomial times a geometric factor.

A nonzero $v$ with $(A - \lambda I)^jv = 0$ for some $j \ge 1$ is a generalized eigenvector for $\lambda$, and $G_\lambda = \Null\big((A - \lambda I)^n\big)$ is the generalized eigenspace. The Jordan block of size $k$ is $J_k(\lambda) = \lambda I_k + N_k$, where $N_k$ has ones on the first superdiagonal and zeros elsewhere. A Jordan chain of length $k$ is a list $s_1, \dots, s_k$ with $s_1 \ne 0$, $(A - \lambda I)s_1 = 0$ and $(A - \lambda I)s_j = s_{j-1}$ for $j = 2, \dots, k$.

Let $A \in \C^{n\times n}$ and $\lambda \in \C$. (i) The vectors $s_1, \dots, s_k$ of a Jordan chain for $\lambda$ are linearly independent and lie in $G_\lambda$; $A$ maps their span into itself and acts there, in the basis $s_1, \dots, s_k$, as $J_k(\lambda)$. (ii) Every generalized eigenvector for $\lambda$ lies in $G_\lambda$.

Proof. (i) By induction, $(A - \lambda I)^{j-1}s_j = s_1$ and $(A - \lambda I)^js_j = 0$. If $\sum_j c_js_j = 0$ with some $c_j \ne 0$, let $j_0$ be the largest such index; applying $(A - \lambda I)^{j_0 - 1}$ leaves $c_{j_0}s_1 = 0$, which is impossible. Hence $k \le n$ and $(A - \lambda I)^ns_j = 0$ for every $j$. The relations $As_1 = \lambda s_1$ and $As_j = \lambda s_j + s_{j-1}$ are the columns of $J_k(\lambda)$. (ii) The null spaces $\mathcal N_j = \Null\big((A - \lambda I)^j\big)$ increase with $j$, and once $\mathcal N_j = \mathcal N_{j+1}$ they stay constant: if $(A - \lambda I)^{j+2}x = 0$, then $(A - \lambda I)x \in \mathcal N_{j+1} = \mathcal N_j$, so $x \in \mathcal N_{j+1}$. Each strict inclusion raises the dimension, which is at most $n$, so $\mathcal N_j = \mathcal N_n = G_\lambda$ for every $j \ge n$.

Every $A \in \C^{n\times n}$ is similar to a block-diagonal matrix

$$J = S^{-1}AS = \diag\big(J_{k_1}(\mu_1), \dots, J_{k_r}(\mu_r)\big),$$

where each $\mu_j$ is an eigenvalue of $A$ (repetitions allowed), and $J$ is unique up to the order of the blocks. For each eigenvalue $\lambda$: the number of blocks with $\mu_j = \lambda$ is $m_g(\lambda)$, their sizes add up to $m_a(\lambda) = \dim G_\lambda$, and the number of them of size at least $k$ is $\rank(A - \lambda I)^{k-1} - \rank(A - \lambda I)^k$. Moreover $\C^n$ is the direct sum $\bigoplus_{\lambda \in \Lambda(A)} G_\lambda$ of the generalized eigenspaces, and the columns of $S$ belonging to one block form a Jordan chain.

Proof of the block count and of uniqueness

Rank is a similarity invariant (), so compute it on $J$. For a block $J_m(\mu)$ with $\mu \ne \lambda$, $J_m(\mu) - \lambda I$ is invertible and contributes $m$ to $\rank(J - \lambda I)^k$ for every $k$. For a block $J_m(\lambda)$, $(J_m(\lambda) - \lambda I)^k = N_m^k$ has ones on the $k$-th superdiagonal, so its rank is $\max(m - k, 0)$, which drops by one from $k - 1$ to $k$ exactly when $m \ge k$. Summing over blocks, $\rank(A - \lambda I)^{k-1} - \rank(A - \lambda I)^k$ counts the $\lambda$-blocks of size at least $k$. These numbers, which depend only on $A$, determine the block sizes, which proves uniqueness; $k = 1$ gives $n - \rank(A - \lambda I) = m_g(\lambda)$ blocks, by rank–nullity.

For $A, B \in \C^{n\times n}$ the following are equivalent: (i) $A$ and $B$ are similar; (ii) they have the same Jordan blocks, counted with multiplicity; (iii) $\rank(A - \lambda I)^k = \rank(B - \lambda I)^k$ for every $\lambda \in \C$ and every $k \ge 1$. In particular, two diagonalizable matrices are similar if and only if they have the same characteristic polynomial.

Eigenvalues are continuous in the entries (); eigenvectors are not. For $A(\eps) = \begin{bmatrix}1 & 1\\ \eps & 1\end{bmatrix}$, $p(\lambda) = (\lambda - 1)^2 - \eps$, so the eigenvalues $1 \pm \sqrt\eps$ (equal to $1 \pm i\sqrt{-\eps}$ for $\eps < 0$) are continuous at $\eps = 0$ but with infinite slope: a perturbation of size $10^{-8}$ moves them by $10^{-4}$. Normalizing them to unit length, $V^\T V = \frac{1}{1 + \eps}\begin{bmatrix}1 + \eps & 1 - \eps\\ 1 - \eps & 1 + \eps\end{bmatrix}$ has eigenvalues $\frac{2}{1 + \eps}$ and $\frac{2\eps}{1 + \eps}$ for $0 < \eps \le 1$, and $\kappa(V)$ is the square root of their ratio, the ratio of the largest to the smallest singular value of $V$ (chapter 4), so

$$\kappa(V) = \abs{\eps}^{-1/2} \qquad (0 < \abs{\eps} \le 1),$$

with the same value for $\eps < 0$, where the computation uses the complex eigenvectors $(1, \pm i\sqrt{-\eps})^\T$. Eigenvectors can jump even for symmetric matrices: $I + \eps\begin{bmatrix}\cos(2/\eps) & \sin(2/\eps)\\ \sin(2/\eps) & -\cos(2/\eps)\end{bmatrix}$ tends to $I$, with eigenvalues $1 \pm \eps$ and eigenvectors at the angle $1/\eps$, which spin without limit as $\eps \to 0$.

Powers and the spectral radius

Describe $A^k$ and $x_k = A^kx_0$ as $k \to \infty$: whether they decay, how fast, and in which direction $x_k$ ends up pointing.

The spectral radius of $A \in \C^{n\times n}$ is $\rho(A) = \max\{\abs{\lambda} : \lambda \in \Lambda(A)\}$.

(i) Diagonal $A = \diag(\lambda_1, \lambda_2)$: $A^k = \diag(\lambda_1^k, \lambda_2^k) \to 0$ exactly when both $\abs{\lambda_i} < 1$. (ii) The Jordan block $J_2(\lambda)$: the corner entry $k\lambda^{k-1}$ also tends to $0$ when $\abs{\lambda} < 1$, since geometric decay beats polynomial growth, but $J_2(\lambda)^k$ is not bounded by any constant times $\abs{\lambda}^k$. (iii) The rotation $R_\theta$: every power is a rotation, $\rho = 1$, with neither growth nor decay.

$A^k \to 0$ exactly when $\rho(A) < 1$, and the rate is $\rho(A)^k$ up to factors that grow at most polynomially.

For $A \in \C^{n\times n}$, $A^k \to 0$ as $k \to \infty$ if and only if $\rho(A) < 1$.

Proof. If $\rho(A) \ge 1$, take an eigenvector $Av = \lambda v$ with $\abs{\lambda} \ge 1$; then $\norm{A^kv} = \abs{\lambda}^k\norm{v} \not\to 0$. If $\rho(A) < 1$, write $A^k = SJ^kS^{-1}$ with the Jordan form. By each entry of $J^k$ is $0$ or $\binom{k}{j}\lambda^{k-j}$ with $\abs{\lambda} \le \rho < 1$ and $j < n$, and $\binom{k}{j}\abs{\lambda}^{k-j} \le k^j\rho^{k-j} \to 0$.

For any norm on $\C^n$ and its induced matrix norm $\norm{M} = \max_{x \ne 0}\norm{Mx}/\norm{x}$, $\rho(A)^k \le \norm{A^k}$ for all $k$. If $A = V\Lambda V^{-1}$ is diagonalizable, then in the induced 2-norm

$$\rho(A)^k \le \norm{A^k} \le \kappa(V)\,\rho(A)^k,\qquad \kappa(V) = \norm{V}\norm{V^{-1}}.$$

Proof. For an eigenvector with $\abs{\lambda} = \rho(A)$, $\norm{A^kv} = \rho(A)^k\norm{v}$. For the upper bound, $\norm{A^k} = \norm{V\Lambda^kV^{-1}} \le \norm{V}\,\norm{\Lambda^k}\,\norm{V^{-1}}$, and the 2-norm of a diagonal matrix is its largest entry in modulus, $\rho(A)^k$.

By , the entries of $J^k$ in a block $J_p(\mu)$ are $\binom{k}{j}\mu^{k-j}$ with $0 \le j < p$, and $\binom{k}{j} \le k^j$. Upper bound: if $\abs\mu = \rho$, then $p \le m$ and $\binom{k}{j}\rho^{k-j} \le k^{m-1}\rho^k\max(1, \rho^{1-m})$; if $0 < \abs\mu < \rho$, the ratio of $k^j\abs\mu^{k-j}$ to $k^{m-1}\rho^k$ tends to $0$ and is therefore bounded; if $\mu = 0$, the entries vanish for $k \ge p$, so the ratio is again bounded. Lower bound: a block $J_m(\mu)$ with $\abs\mu = \rho$ has the corner entry $\binom{k}{m-1}\mu^{k-m+1}$, and $\binom{k}{m-1} \ge \big(k/(m-1)\big)^{m-1}$ for $k \ge m - 1 \ge 1$; for $m = 1$ use the diagonal entry $\mu^k$. A real $2\times 2$ matrix with trace $\tau$ and determinant $\Delta$ has $\rho(A) < 1$ if and only if

$$\abs{\tau} - 1 < \Delta < 1.$$

Proof. If the eigenvalues are a nonreal pair, $\abs{\lambda}^2 = \lambda\bar\lambda = \Delta$, so $\rho < 1$ means $\Delta < 1$; and then $\abs{\tau} < 2\sqrt{\Delta} \le 1 + \Delta$ holds automatically ($D < 0$ and $(1 - \sqrt\Delta)^2 \ge 0$). If they are real, both lie in $(-1, 1)$ exactly when $p_A(1) = 1 - \tau + \Delta > 0$, $p_A(-1) = 1 + \tau + \Delta > 0$, and the two roots are not both beyond $1$ or both beyond $-1$; given the first two conditions, the excluded cases are those with $\Delta = \lambda_+\lambda_- > 1$. Let $A = V\Lambda V^{-1}$ be diagonalizable with eigenvalues ordered so that $\abs{\lambda_1} > \abs{\lambda_2} \ge \dots \ge \abs{\lambda_n}$, and let $x_0$ satisfy $c_1 = w_1^*x_0 \ne 0$. Then $x_k = A^kx_0 \ne 0$ for all $k$, and the angle $\theta_k$ between $x_k$ and the line $\spn(v_1)$, defined by $\sin\theta_k = \min_{c \in \C}\norm{x_k - cv_1}/\norm{x_k}$, satisfies

$$\sin\theta_k \le C\,\Big|\frac{\lambda_2}{\lambda_1}\Big|^k$$

for a constant $C$ depending on $x_0$ and $V$ but not on $k$.

Proof. By the modal expansion (), $x_k = \lambda_1^k\,(c_1v_1 + \eta_k)$ with the error term $\eta_k = \sum_{i\ge2} c_i(\lambda_i/\lambda_1)^kv_i$ and $c_i = w_i^*x_0$, so $\norm{\eta_k} \le \beta\,\abs{\lambda_2/\lambda_1}^k$ with $\beta = \sum_{i\ge2}\abs{c_i}\norm{v_i}$. Here $\lambda_1 \ne 0$ and the $v_i$ are independent, so $x_k \ne 0$. The distance from $c_1v_1 + \eta_k$ to $\spn(v_1)$ is at most $\norm{\eta_k}$, and $\norm{c_1v_1 + \eta_k} \ge \abs{c_1}\norm{v_1} - \norm{\eta_k}$, which exceeds $\frac12\abs{c_1}\norm{v_1}$ for large $k$; for those $k$, $\sin\theta_k \le 2\beta\abs{\lambda_2/\lambda_1}^k/(\abs{c_1}\norm{v_1})$. Since $\sin\theta_k \le 1$, enlarging the constant covers the finitely many remaining $k$.

In practice $x_k$ is rescaled to unit length at each step, which avoids overflow and, since $A$ is linear, changes neither the directions nor the angles $\theta_k$. Power iteration can fail when two different eigenvalues share the largest modulus. For the rotation $R_\theta$ with $\theta$ not an integer multiple of $\pi$, both eigenvalues have modulus $1$ and $x_k = R_{k\theta}x_0$ keeps turning without settling.
