Beyond $\R^n$: norms, operators and Hilbert space

Signals, trajectories and input histories are vectors in infinite-dimensional spaces. This chapter sorts the facts of $\R^n$ into those that survive there (orthogonal projection, orthonormal expansion, minimum-norm solutions) and those that fail (equivalence of norms, compactness of the unit ball, spectra made only of eigenvalues). It ends with the two results that the control chapters use most: Fourier modes diagonalize shift-invariant operators, exactly through the discrete Fourier transform (DFT) in the cyclic case and as generalized eigenfunctions for convolutions on the line, and minimum-energy steering is a projection.

Normed and Banach spaces

In $\R^n$ every norm gives the same notions of convergence, every subspace is closed and the unit ball is compact (, , ). Decide which of these facts survive when the vectors are functions or infinite sequences.

A norm on a vector space $X$ over $\F$ ($\R$ or $\C$) is a function $\norm{\cdot}: X \to [0, \infty)$ with $\norm{x} = 0$ only for $x = 0$, $\norm{\alpha x} = \abs{\alpha}\norm{x}$, and $\norm{x + y} \le \norm{x} + \norm{y}$. A sequence $(x_n)$ is Cauchy if $\norm{x_n - x_m} \to 0$ as $n, m \to \infty$. A normed space in which every Cauchy sequence converges is complete, or a Banach space.

The sequence spaces $\ell^p$ for $1 \le p < \infty$, the sequences $x = (x_1, x_2, \dots)$ with $\norm{x}_p = (\sum_k\abs{x_k}^p)^{1/p} < \infty$, and $\ell^\infty$, the bounded sequences with $\norm{x}_\infty = \sup_k\abs{x_k}$; the space $C[a,b]$ of continuous functions on an interval $[a,b]$, $a < b$, with the sup norm $\norm{f}_\infty = \max_t\abs{f(t)}$; and, for $1 \le p < \infty$, $L^p[a,b]$, the Lebesgue-measurable functions with $\norm{f}_p = (\int_a^b\abs{f}^p\,\dd t)^{1/p} < \infty$, where two functions that agree except on a set of measure zero ("almost everywhere") are identified. Continuous functions on $[a,b]$ are bounded and measurable, so they lie in every $L^p[a,b]$, and $\norm{f}_p$ is also a norm on $C[a,b]$, since a continuous $f$ with $\int_a^b\abs{f}^p\,\dd t = 0$ vanishes. All of these spaces are complete (), and among them only $\ell^2$ and $L^2[a,b]$ carry an inner product that induces the norm ().

(i) For $1 \le p \le \infty$, $\norm{\cdot}_p$ is a norm on $\ell^p$, and $\ell^p$ is a Banach space. (ii) $C[a,b]$ with the sup norm is a Banach space. (iii) For $1 \le p < \infty$, $L^p[a,b]$ is a Banach space, and $C[a,b]$ is dense in it. So $L^p[a,b]$ is the completion of $(C[a,b], \norm{\cdot}_p)$: a Banach space that contains it as a dense subspace.

Proof of (ii). If $(f_n)$ is Cauchy in the sup norm, then $(f_n(t))$ is Cauchy in $\F$ for every $t$; let $f(t)$ be its limit. Given $\eps > 0$, choose $N$ with $\norm{f_n - f_m}_\infty \le \eps$ for $n, m \ge N$; letting $m \to \infty$ gives $\abs{f_n(t) - f(t)} \le \eps$ for all $t$ and all $n \ge N$, so $f_n \to f$ uniformly. A uniform limit of continuous functions is continuous, since $\abs{f(t) - f(s)} \le 2\eps + \abs{f_N(t) - f_N(s)}$; so $f \in C[a,b]$ and $\norm{f_n - f}_\infty \to 0$.

Folland, Real Analysis, 2nd ed., ch. 6. Density in (iii): simple functions are dense in $L^p$, a measurable subset of $[a,b]$ differs from a finite union of intervals by a set of small measure, and the indicator function of an interval is an $L^p$ limit of continuous piecewise-linear functions; Folland, ch. 7. Luenberger, Optimization by Vector Space Methods, ch. 2, surveys all of these spaces.

The functions $f_n(t) = t^n$ on $[0, 1]$ have

$$\norm{f_n}_\infty = 1,\qquad \norm{f_n}_1 = \int_0^1 t^n\,\dd t = \frac{1}{n+1},\qquad \norm{f_n}_2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2n+1}}.$$

So $f_n \to 0$ in the $L^1$ and $L^2$ norms but not uniformly, and no constant $c$ satisfies $\norm{f}_\infty \le c\norm{f}_1$ for all $f \in C[0,1]$. Which sequences converge now depends on the norm.

On a finite-dimensional vector space any two norms $\norm{\cdot}_a$, $\norm{\cdot}_b$ are equivalent: there are constants $0 < c \le C$ with $c\norm{x}_a \le \norm{x}_b \le C\norm{x}_a$ for every $x$. On $\F^n$, for example,

$$\norm{x}_\infty \le \norm{x}_2 \le \norm{x}_1 \le \sqrt n\,\norm{x}_2 \le n\norm{x}_\infty .$$

Proof of the chain. $\abs{x_k} \le \norm{x}_2$ for every $k$; $\norm{x}_1^2 = \sum_{j,k}\abs{x_j}\abs{x_k} \ge \sum_k\abs{x_k}^2 = \norm{x}_2^2$; Cauchy–Schwarz with the vector of ones gives $\norm{x}_1 \le \sqrt n\,\norm{x}_2$; and $\norm{x}_2^2 \le n\norm{x}_\infty^2$.

Idea of the general statement: in coordinates, a norm is continuous and positive on the compact unit sphere of $\norm{\cdot}_2$, so it is bounded above and below there. Proof: Kreyszig, ch. 2; for $\F^n$ also the norm-equivalence theorem of chapter 2.

Completeness depends on the norm. With the sup norm $C[-1,1]$ is complete (); with the $L^2$ norm it is not: the ramps $g_n(t) = \max(-1, \min(1, nt))$ are continuous, form a Cauchy sequence in $L^2$ (for $m > n$, $g_m$ and $g_n$ differ only on $\abs{t} \le 1/n$, where $\abs{g_m - g_n} \le 1$, so $\norm{g_m - g_n}_2^2 \le 2/n$), and converge in $L^2$ to the sign function, which has no continuous representative. So the Cauchy sequence $(g_n)$ has no limit in $C[-1,1]$; by , the completion of $(C[a,b], \norm{\cdot}_2)$ is $L^2[a,b]$.

A subset $M$ of a normed space $X$ is closed if it contains the limit of every convergent sequence of its elements, and dense if every $x \in X$ is such a limit.

Let $V$ be a finite-dimensional normed space. (i) $V$ is complete. Hence every finite-dimensional subspace of a normed space $X$ is closed in $X$, and every subspace of a finite-dimensional normed space is closed. (ii) For every normed space $Y$ and every linear map $T: V \to Y$ there is a constant $C$ with $\norm{Tx} \le C\norm{x}$ for all $x \in V$.

Proof. Choose a basis $v_1, \dots, v_n$ of $V$ and write $x = \sum_j\xi_jv_j$ with coordinate vector $\xi \in \F^n$. The maps $x \mapsto \norm{\xi}_2$ and $x \mapsto \norm{\xi}_1$ are norms on $V$, so by there are constants $0 < c \le C'$ with $c\norm{\xi}_2 \le \norm{x} \le C'\norm{\xi}_2$, and a constant $c_1$ with $\norm{\xi}_1 \le c_1\norm{x}$. (i) If $(x_k)$ is Cauchy in $V$, its coordinate vectors are Cauchy in $\F^n$, which is complete, so they converge to some $\xi$, and then $x_k \to \sum_j\xi_jv_j$. If $V \subseteq X$ and $x_k \in V$ converge to $x \in X$, then $(x_k)$ is Cauchy, so it has a limit in $V$, which is $x$ because limits are unique. (ii) $\norm{Tx} = \norm{\sum_j\xi_jTv_j} \le \big(\max_j\norm{Tv_j}\big)\norm{\xi}_1$, which is at most $c_1\max_j\norm{Tv_j}\,\norm{x}$.

The polynomials form a subspace of $L^2[0,1]$ that is dense but not closed. Dense: by the Weierstrass approximation theorem polynomials approximate every continuous function uniformly, hence in $L^2$ (on $[0,1]$, $\norm{g}_2 \le \norm{g}_\infty$), and continuous functions are dense in $L^2[0,1]$ (). Not closed: a closed dense subspace contains every limit, so it would be all of $L^2[0,1]$; but $e^t$ is not a polynomial, even after a change on a set of measure zero, because two continuous functions that agree almost everywhere agree everywhere and no derivative of $e^t$ vanishes. The same holds for the finitely supported sequences inside $\ell^2$: the truncations $x^{(N)} = (x_1, \dots, x_N, 0, 0, \dots)$ of any $x \in \ell^2$ converge to $x$, since $\norm{x - x^{(N)}}_2^2 = \sum_{k > N}\abs{x_k}^2 \to 0$, while $(1, \frac12, \frac13, \dots) \in \ell^2$ is not finitely supported.

The closed unit ball $\{x : \norm{x} \le 1\}$ of a normed space $X$ is compact if and only if $\dim X < \infty$.

Proof in a Hilbert space. If $\dim X < \infty$, the ball is closed and bounded in coordinates, by , hence compact by the Heine–Borel theorem. Conversely, an infinite-dimensional Hilbert space contains an orthonormal sequence $(e_k)$ (apply Gram–Schmidt, chapter 2, to any independent sequence), and $\norm{e_j - e_k}^2 = 2$ for $j \ne k$, so no subsequence is Cauchy and none converges. For general normed spaces the same conclusion follows from Riesz's lemma; Kreyszig, ch. 2.

Three finite-dimensional conveniences rest on one fact, compactness of the unit sphere: all norms are equivalent (), every subspace is closed (), and every bounded sequence has a convergent subsequence (). In infinite dimension each must be replaced by a hypothesis that one states and checks: a chosen norm, a closed subspace, a compact operator ().

Bounded operators and induced norms

Extend the matrix norm of chapter 4 to linear maps between normed spaces, decide when such a map is continuous, and find when $I - T$ can be inverted by a series.

A linear map $T: X \to Y$ between normed spaces is bounded if $\norm{Tx} \le C\norm{x}$ for some constant $C$ and all $x$. The smallest such $C$ is the operator norm

$$\norm{T} = \sup_{x \ne 0}\frac{\norm{Tx}}{\norm{x}} = \sup_{\norm{x} = 1}\norm{Tx}.$$

We write $B(X, Y)$ for the set of bounded linear maps $X \to Y$, and $B(X) = B(X, X)$.

Let $X$, $Y$, $Z$ be normed spaces. (i) The operator norm is a norm on $B(X, Y)$, and $\norm{Tx} \le \norm{T}\norm{x}$ for every $x$. (ii) If $Y$ is a Banach space, so is $B(X, Y)$. (iii) If $T \in B(X, Y)$ and $S \in B(Y, Z)$, then $ST \in B(X, Z)$ and $\norm{ST} \le \norm{S}\norm{T}$; in particular $\norm{T^k} \le \norm{T}^k$ for $T \in B(X)$ and $k \ge 1$.

Proof. (i) $\norm{(S + T)x} \le (\norm{S} + \norm{T})\norm{x}$ and $\norm{\alpha Tx} = \abs\alpha\norm{Tx}$ give the triangle inequality and homogeneity, and $\norm{T} = 0$ forces $Tx = 0$ for every $x$. (ii) If $(T_n)$ is Cauchy in $B(X, Y)$, then $\norm{T_nx - T_mx} \le \norm{T_n - T_m}\norm{x}$, so $(T_nx)$ is Cauchy in $Y$; call its limit $Tx$. The map $T$ is linear. Given $\eps > 0$, choose $N$ with $\norm{T_n - T_m} \le \eps$ for $n, m \ge N$; letting $m \to \infty$ in $\norm{T_nx - T_mx} \le \eps\norm{x}$ gives $\norm{(T_n - T)x} \le \eps\norm{x}$. So $T = T_n - (T_n - T)$ is bounded and $\norm{T_n - T} \le \eps$ for $n \ge N$. (iii) $\norm{STx} \le \norm{S}\norm{Tx} \le \norm{S}\norm{T}\norm{x}$.

For a linear $T: X \to Y$ the following are equivalent: $T$ is bounded; $T$ is continuous; $T$ is continuous at $0$.

Proof. If $T$ is bounded, $\norm{Tx - Ty} \le \norm{T}\norm{x - y}$, so $T$ is Lipschitz. Continuity at $0$ is a special case of continuity. If $T$ is continuous at $0$, there is $\delta > 0$ with $\norm{Tx} \le 1$ whenever $\norm{x} \le \delta$; scaling gives $\norm{Tx} \le \norm{x}/\delta$ for every $x$.

Differentiation is unbounded. On $C^1[0,1]$ (continuously differentiable functions) with the sup norm, $D f = f'$ is linear, and $f_n(t) = \sin(n\pi t)$ has $\norm{f_n}_\infty = 1$ but $\norm{Df_n}_\infty = n\pi$. In finite dimension this cannot happen: every linear map on a finite-dimensional normed space is bounded (). Unbounded operators such as $D$ are one reason why differential equations posed on function spaces need more care than the matrix equation $\dot x = Ax$ of chapter 7.

For $A \in \C^{m\times n}$ with entries $a_{ij}$, the norms induced by $\norm{\cdot}_1$, $\norm{\cdot}_2$ and $\norm{\cdot}_\infty$ on both sides are

$$\norm{A}_1 = \max_j\sum_{i}\abs{a_{ij}},\qquad \norm{A}_2 = \sigma_1(A),\qquad \norm{A}_\infty = \max_i\sum_{j}\abs{a_{ij}},$$

the largest column sum, the largest singular value and the largest row sum of absolute values. Consequently $\norm{A^*}_1 = \norm{A}_\infty$ and $\norm{A^*}_2 = \norm{A}_2$, where $A^*$ is the conjugate transpose.

Proof. For $\norm{\cdot}_1$,

$$\norm{Ax}_1 = \sum_i\Big\lvert\sum_j a_{ij}x_j\Big\rvert \le \sum_j\abs{x_j}\sum_i\abs{a_{ij}} \le \Big(\max_j\sum_i\abs{a_{ij}}\Big)\norm{x}_1,$$

with equality at $x = e_{j^*}$, the $j^*$-th standard basis vector, for a column $j^*$ of largest sum. $\norm{\cdot}_\infty$: $\abs{(Ax)_i} \le \sum_j\abs{a_{ij}}\abs{x_j} \le \big(\sum_j\abs{a_{ij}}\big)\norm{x}_\infty$, with equality for the row $i^*$ of largest sum at $x_j = \overline{a_{i^*j}}/\abs{a_{i^*j}}$, the bar denoting complex conjugation (and $x_j = 1$ where $a_{i^*j} = 0$), which has $\norm{x}_\infty = 1$. The spectral norm is the norms-from-singular-values theorem of chapter 4. Finally, row $i$ of $A$, conjugated, is column $i$ of $A^*$, so the largest row sum of $A$ is the largest column sum of $A^*$; and with the standard inner product $\inner{x}{y} = y^*x$, both $\norm{A}_2$ and $\norm{A^*}_2$ equal the maximum of $\abs{\inner{Ax}{y}} = \abs{\inner{x}{A^*y}}$ over unit vectors $x$ and $y$, by Cauchy–Schwarz with equality at $y = Ax/\norm{Ax}_2$ (and at $x = A^*y/\norm{A^*y}_2$).

For $A = \begin{bmatrix}1 & 2\\ 3 & 4\end{bmatrix}$: $\norm{A}_1 = \max(4, 6) = 6$, $\norm{A}_\infty = \max(3, 7) = 7$, $\norm{A}_2 = \sigma_1 \approx 5.465$ (from $A^\T A$, which has trace $30$ and determinant $4$; $A^\T$ is the transpose), and the spectral radius $\rho(A) = \max\abs{\lambda} = (5 + \sqrt{33})/2 \approx 5.372$. Every norm exceeds $\rho(A)$, and $\norm{A}_\infty \ne \norm{A}_1$, which can happen only because $A \ne A^\T$: by , $\norm{A}_\infty = \norm{A^*}_1$, and here $A^* = A^\T$.

Let $A \in \F^{n\times n}$, let $\norm{\cdot}$ be a norm on $\F^n$, and let $\norm{A}$ be the operator norm it induces. Then $\rho(A) \le \norm{A}$.

Proof. If $Ax = \lambda x$ with $x \ne 0$, then $\abs{\lambda}\norm{x} = \norm{Ax} \le \norm{A}\norm{x}$. (If $A$ is real, $\lambda$ is complex and the norm is defined only on $\R^n$, apply this argument to $\norm{\cdot}_2$ on $\C^n$ and to $A^k$: $\abs{\lambda}^k \le \norm{A^k}_2 \le c\norm{A^k} \le c\norm{A}^k$ for every $k$, where the constant $c$ comes from the equivalence of norms on $\R^{n\times n}$ () and the last step is ; take $k$-th roots and let $k \to \infty$.)

Let $A \in \F^{n\times n}$. For every $\eps > 0$ there is a norm on $\F^n$ whose induced norm satisfies $\norm{A} \le \rho(A) + \eps$. Hence $\rho(A)$ is the infimum of $\norm{A}$ over all induced norms, and $\rho(A) < 1$ if and only if $\norm{A} < 1$ for some induced norm.

Proof

By Schur triangularization (chapter 3), $A = UTU^*$ with $U$ unitary and $T$ upper triangular with the eigenvalues of $A$ on its diagonal. For $t > 0$ let $D_t = \diag(1, t, \dots, t^{n-1})$. The matrix $D_t^{-1}TD_t$ has entries $t^{l-j}T_{jl}$ for $l \ge j$ and $0$ below the diagonal, so its largest column sum of absolute values is at most $\rho(A) + \eps$ once $t$ is small enough. With $S = UD_t$, the norm $\norm{x}_S = \norm{S^{-1}x}_1$ on $\C^n$ induces $\norm{A}_S = \norm{S^{-1}AS}_1$ (substitute $x = Sy$), and $\norm{S^{-1}AS}_1 = \norm{D_t^{-1}TD_t}_1 \le \rho(A) + \eps$ by . For real $A$, restrict $\norm{\cdot}_S$ to $\R^n$, which can only lower the induced norm. Together with this gives the infimum. If $\rho(A) < 1$, the choice $\eps = (1 - \rho(A))/2$ gives an induced norm with $\norm{A} < 1$; the converse is again. The infimum need not be attained: $A = \begin{bmatrix}0 & 1\\ 0 & 0\end{bmatrix}$ has $\rho(A) = 0$, but $\norm{A} > 0$ in every norm because $A \ne 0$.

Let $X$ be a Banach space and $T \in B(X)$ with $\norm{T} < 1$. Then $I - T$ has a bounded inverse,

$$(I - T)^{-1} = \sum_{k=0}^\infty T^k,\qquad \norm{(I - T)^{-1}} \le \frac{1}{1 - \norm{T}}.$$

Proof. $\norm{T^k} \le \norm{T}^k$, so the partial sums $S_N = \sum_{k\le N}T^k$ form a Cauchy sequence in $B(X)$, which is complete because $X$ is (); let $S$ be the limit. Then $(I - T)S_N = I - T^{N+1}$, which tends to $I$, and likewise $S_N(I - T)$, so $S$ is a two-sided inverse, and $\norm{S} \le \sum_k\norm{T}^k = 1/(1 - \norm{T})$.

Let $X$ be a Banach space. If $A \in B(X)$ has a bounded inverse and $E \in B(X)$ satisfies $\norm{E} < 1/\norm{A^{-1}}$, then $A + E$ has a bounded inverse with $\norm{(A + E)^{-1}} \le \norm{A^{-1}}/(1 - \norm{A^{-1}}\norm{E})$.

Proof. $A + E = A(I + A^{-1}E)$ and $\norm{A^{-1}E} \le \norm{A^{-1}}\norm{E} < 1$; apply to $T = -A^{-1}E$.

For invertible matrices $A \in \F^{n\times n}$ in the spectral norm the threshold is sharp: $1/\norm{A^{-1}}_2 = \sigma_n$, the smallest singular value (chapter 4), and the distance-to-singularity corollary of chapter 4 exhibits a perturbation $E$ of exactly that size that makes $A + E$ singular.

$\norm{T} < 1$ is sufficient, not necessary. $T = \begin{bmatrix}0 & 10\\ 0 & 0\end{bmatrix}$ has $\norm{T} = 10$, yet $T^2 = 0$ and $(I - T)^{-1} = I + T$; for matrices the exact condition for convergence is $\rho(T) < 1$ (). Invertibility of $I - T$ does not need convergence: for the scalar $T = -2$ the series $\sum(-2)^k$ diverges while $(1 + 2)^{-1} = 1/3$ exists. Completeness cannot be dropped: on the finitely supported sequences with the $\ell^2$ norm, a normed space that is not complete (), $T(x_1, x_2, \dots) = (0, x_1/2, x_2/2, \dots)$ has $\norm{T} = 1/2$, but the only sequence with $(I - T)x = e_1 = (1, 0, 0, \dots)$ is $x = (1, \frac12, \frac14, \dots)$, which is not finitely supported, so $I - T$ is not onto.

For $T \in \F^{n\times n}$ the series $\sum_{k\ge0}T^k$ converges if and only if $\rho(T) < 1$, and then its sum is $(I - T)^{-1}$.

Proof. If $\rho(T) < 1$, gives an induced norm with $\norm{T} < 1$, and $\F^n$ is complete, so applies. Conversely, if the series converges, its terms tend to $0$; for an eigenpair $Tx = \lambda x$ with $x \in \C^n$ nonzero, $T^kx = \lambda^kx \to 0$ forces $\abs\lambda < 1$.

For a linear rate network $\tau\dot r = -r + Wr + u$ with firing rates $r(t) \in \R^n$, connectivity $W \in \R^{n\times n}$, time constant $\tau > 0$ and constant input $u$, the steady state is $r^* = (I - W)^{-1}u$ whenever $1$ is not an eigenvalue of $W$. When $\rho(W) < 1$ it expands as $\sum_k W^ku$ (), a sum over paths of length $k$ through the network. Stability of the dynamics only needs the eigenvalues of $W$ to have real part below $1$ (the eigenvalue test of chapter 7), which is weaker because $\Re\lambda \le \abs\lambda$: the inhibitory self-loop $W = -2$ gives the stable $\tau\dot r = -3r + u$ with $r^* = u/3$, while the path expansion diverges.

Hilbert spaces and the projection theorem

Least squares in chapter 2 rested on one picture: the closest point of a subspace is the foot of the perpendicular (the projection theorem of chapter 2). Find what that picture needs in infinite dimension.

An inner product on $X$ is a map $\inner{\cdot}{\cdot}: X \times X \to \F$ that is linear in the first argument, satisfies $\inner{y}{x} = \overline{\inner{x}{y}}$, and has $\inner{x}{x} > 0$ for $x \ne 0$. It induces the norm $\norm{x} = \inner{x}{x}^{1/2}$ (the triangle inequality is a corollary of Cauchy–Schwarz, chapter 2). A Hilbert space is an inner product space that is complete in this norm. The main examples, complete by , are $\ell^2$ with $\inner{x}{y} = \sum_k x_k\overline{y_k}$ and $L^2[a,b]$ with $\inner{f}{g} = \int_a^b f(t)\overline{g(t)}\,\dd t$; the sum and the integral converge absolutely because $2\abs{x\bar y} \le \abs{x}^2 + \abs{y}^2$.

Cauchy–Schwarz, $\abs{\inner{x}{y}} \le \norm{x}\norm{y}$, and the parallelogram law, $\norm{x + y}^2 + \norm{x - y}^2 = 2\norm{x}^2 + 2\norm{y}^2$, hold in every inner product space, with the dimension-free proofs of chapter 2 (Cauchy–Schwarz, the parallelogram law).

The parallelogram law characterizes norms that come from inner products (Jordan–von Neumann, chapter 2), and it fails in $\ell^p$ and $L^p[a,b]$ for every $p \ne 2$ and for the sup norm. In $\ell^p$, $x = e_1$ and $y = e_2$ (the first two standard unit sequences) give $\norm{x+y}_p^2 + \norm{x-y}_p^2 = 2\cdot 2^{2/p}$, which equals $2\norm{x}_p^2 + 2\norm{y}_p^2 = 4$ only for $p = 2$; for $p = 1$ it is $8$, and in $\ell^\infty$ the left side is $2$. In $L^p[a,b]$ the same computation applies to the indicator functions of two disjoint intervals of equal length, scaled to norm $1$. In $C[0,1]$ with the sup norm, $f = 1$ and $g(t) = t$ give $\norm{f + g}_\infty^2 + \norm{f - g}_\infty^2 = 4 + 1 = 5 \ne 4$. These Banach spaces have no notion of angle, and their closest points behave differently ().

Let $H$ be a Hilbert space, $K \subseteq H$ a nonempty closed convex set and $x \in H$. There is exactly one $k^* \in K$ with $\norm{x - k^*} = \inf_{k\in K}\norm{x - k}$. If $K = M$ is a closed subspace, $k^* = P_Mx$ is characterized by $x - k^* \perp M$ (orthogonal to every element of $M$), the map $P_M$ is a bounded linear projector with $P_M^2 = P_M$ and $\norm{P_M} \le 1$, and $H = M \oplus M^\perp$: every $x \in H$ is uniquely $m + z$ with $m \in M$ and $z$ in the orthogonal complement $M^\perp = \{z \in H : \inner{z}{m} = 0 \text{ for all } m \in M\}$.

Proof. Let $d$ be the infimum and $k_n \in K$ with $\norm{x - k_n} \to d$. The parallelogram law applied to $x - k_n$ and $x - k_m$ gives

$$\norm{k_n - k_m}^2 = 2\norm{x - k_n}^2 + 2\norm{x - k_m}^2 - 4\Big\lVert x - \tfrac{k_n + k_m}{2}\Big\rVert^2 \le 2\norm{x - k_n}^2 + 2\norm{x - k_m}^2 - 4d^2,$$

because the midpoint lies in $K$ by convexity. The right side tends to $0$, so $(k_n)$ is Cauchy; it converges by completeness, and the limit $k^*$ lies in $K$ because $K$ is closed. Applied to two minimizers, the same inequality shows that they coincide. For a subspace $M$ and a unit vector $m \in M$, put $z = x - k^*$; the point $k^* + \inner{z}{m}m \in M$ has distance $\big(\norm{z}^2 - \abs{\inner{z}{m}}^2\big)^{1/2}$ from $x$, so minimality forces $\inner{z}{m} = 0$. Conversely, if $x - k \perp M$ for some $k \in M$, Pythagoras gives $\norm{x - k'}^2 = \norm{x - k}^2 + \norm{k - k'}^2$ for every $k' \in M$, so $k = P_Mx$. Linearity of $P_M$ follows from the characterization, since $\alpha P_Mx + \beta P_My \in M$ and $\alpha x + \beta y - (\alpha P_Mx + \beta P_My) \perp M$. For $m \in M$, $P_Mm = m$, so $P_M^2 = P_M$; Pythagoras gives $\norm{x}^2 = \norm{P_Mx}^2 + \norm{x - P_Mx}^2$, so $\norm{P_M} \le 1$; and $x = P_Mx + (x - P_Mx)$ with $x - P_Mx \in M^\perp$, uniquely because $M \cap M^\perp = \{0\}$.

A sequence $(e_k)$ in $H$ is orthonormal if $\inner{e_j}{e_k} = \delta_{jk}$ (the Kronecker delta: $1$ if $j = k$, else $0$). It is an orthonormal basis if its span is dense in $H$. The numbers $\inner{x}{e_k}$ are the Fourier coefficients of $x$.

Let $(e_k)$ be orthonormal in $H$ and $x \in H$. Then $\sum_k\abs{\inner{x}{e_k}}^2 \le \norm{x}^2$ (Bessel's inequality), and the series $\sum_k\inner{x}{e_k}e_k$ converges to the projection of $x$ onto the closed span of $(e_k)$, the closure of its span. The following are equivalent: $(e_k)$ is an orthonormal basis; $x = \sum_k\inner{x}{e_k}e_k$ for every $x$; $\norm{x}^2 = \sum_k\abs{\inner{x}{e_k}}^2$ for every $x$ (Parseval's identity).

Adjoints and spectra of operators

For a matrix, $A - \lambda I$ fails to be invertible exactly when $\lambda$ is an eigenvalue (square matrices, chapter 1). For an operator, $T - \lambda I$ can be injective without being invertible (). Find what replaces the list of eigenvalues, and which operators still have an orthonormal eigenbasis.

For Hilbert spaces $H_1$, $H_2$ and $T \in B(H_1, H_2)$, the adjoint $T^*: H_2 \to H_1$ is the map with $\inner{Tx}{y} = \inner{x}{T^*y}$ for all $x \in H_1$, $y \in H_2$; it exists by . The main case is $H_1 = H_2 = H$, and then, as for matrices, $T$ is self-adjoint if $T^* = T$, unitary if $T^*T = TT^* = I$, and normal if $T^*T = TT^*$.

Let $H_1$, $H_2$, $H_3$ be Hilbert spaces and $T \in B(H_1, H_2)$. (i) There is exactly one map $T^*: H_2 \to H_1$ with $\inner{Tx}{y} = \inner{x}{T^*y}$ for all $x$, $y$; it is linear and bounded, with $\norm{T^*} = \norm{T}$. (ii) $(T^*)^* = T$, and $(ST)^* = T^*S^*$ for $S \in B(H_2, H_3)$. (iii) $\Range(T)^\perp = \Null(T^*)$, where $\Range(T)$ is the range of $T$ and $\Null(T^*)$ the null space of $T^*$; hence $T$ has dense range if and only if $T^*$ is injective.

Proof. (i) For fixed $y$, $x \mapsto \inner{Tx}{y}$ is linear with $\abs{\inner{Tx}{y}} \le \norm{T}\norm{y}\norm{x}$, so gives exactly one vector $T^*y$ with $\inner{Tx}{y} = \inner{x}{T^*y}$ for all $x$, and $\norm{T^*y} \le \norm{T}\norm{y}$. Uniqueness makes $y \mapsto T^*y$ linear, and $\norm{T^*} \le \norm{T}$. (ii) $\inner{T^*y}{x} = \overline{\inner{x}{T^*y}} = \overline{\inner{Tx}{y}} = \inner{y}{Tx}$ for all $x$, $y$, so $(T^*)^* = T$ by uniqueness, and then $\norm{T} = \norm{(T^*)^*} \le \norm{T^*}$. Likewise $\inner{STx}{z} = \inner{Tx}{S^*z} = \inner{x}{T^*S^*z}$. (iii) $y \perp \Range(T)$ means $\inner{x}{T^*y} = \inner{Tx}{y} = 0$ for all $x$, that is $T^*y = 0$. The closure of $\Range(T)$ is a closed subspace with the same orthogonal complement, since the inner product is continuous, so by it is all of $H_2$ exactly when $\Null(T^*) = \{0\}$.

Let $k$ be a measurable function on $[a,b]^2$ with $\norm{k}_2^2 = \int_a^b\!\int_a^b\abs{k(t,s)}^2\,\dd s\,\dd t < \infty$. Then $(Kf)(t) = \int_a^b k(t, s)f(s)\,\dd s$ defines $K \in B(L^2[a,b])$ with $\norm{K} \le \norm{k}_2$, and its adjoint is $(K^*g)(s) = \int_a^b\overline{k(t,s)}\,g(t)\,\dd t$: transpose and conjugate the kernel.

Proof. By Cauchy–Schwarz in $s$, $\abs{(Kf)(t)}^2 \le \int_a^b\abs{k(t,s)}^2\,\dd s\;\norm{f}^2$ for almost every $t$; integrating in $t$ gives $\norm{Kf} \le \norm{k}_2\norm{f}$. For $f, g \in L^2[a,b]$, Cauchy–Schwarz on the square gives $\iint\abs{k(t,s)f(s)g(t)}\,\dd s\,\dd t \le \norm{k}_2\norm{f}\norm{g} < \infty$, so Fubini's theorem allows the exchange of the integrals in

$$\inner{Kf}{g} = \int_a^b\!\!\int_a^b k(t,s)f(s)\overline{g(t)}\,\dd s\,\dd t = \int_a^b f(s)\,\overline{\int_a^b\overline{k(t,s)}\,g(t)\,\dd t}\;\dd s .$$

Let $X$ be a complex Banach space and $T \in B(X)$. The spectrum $\Lambda(T)$ is the set of $\lambda \in \C$ for which $T - \lambda I$ does not have a bounded inverse defined on all of $X$. The eigenvalues form the point spectrum.

Let $X \ne \{0\}$ be a complex Banach space and $T \in B(X)$. (i) If $\dim X < \infty$, $\Lambda(T)$ is exactly the set of eigenvalues of $T$. (ii) $\Lambda(T)$ is closed and contained in the disk $\abs\lambda \le \norm{T}$. (iii) $\Lambda(T)$ is nonempty, and its largest modulus, the spectral radius $\rho(T)$, satisfies Gelfand's formula $\rho(T) = \lim_{n\to\infty}\norm{T^n}^{1/n}$.

Proof of (i) and (ii). (i) In finite dimension, $T - \lambda I$ is invertible exactly when it is injective (rank–nullity, chapter 1, applied in coordinates), and every inverse is bounded by . (ii) If $\abs\lambda > \norm{T}$, then $T - \lambda I = -\lambda(I - T/\lambda)$ is invertible by . If $T - \lambda_0 I$ is invertible, so is $T - \lambda I$ for $\abs{\lambda - \lambda_0} < 1/\norm{(T - \lambda_0I)^{-1}}$, by ; so the complement of $\Lambda(T)$ is open.

The shifts on $\ell^2$ break both finite-dimensional rules at once. The right shift is an isometry, $\norm{S_Rx} = \norm{x}$, hence injective, but it is not surjective: nothing maps to $e_1$. It has no eigenvalues ($S_Rx = \lambda x$ forces $x_1 = 0$, then $x_2 = 0$, and so on). Yet $\Lambda(S_R)$ is the whole closed unit disk: $S_R$ itself is not onto; for $0 < \abs\lambda < 1$, solving $(S_R - \lambda I)x = e_1$ forces $x_k = -\lambda^{-k}$, which is not square summable, so $S_R - \lambda I$ is not onto either; and $\Lambda(S_R)$ is closed and lies in $\abs\lambda \le \norm{S_R} = 1$ (). The left shift is surjective but not injective, and every $\lambda$ with $\abs\lambda < 1$ is an eigenvalue:

$$S_L(1, \lambda, \lambda^2, \dots) = (\lambda, \lambda^2, \dots) = \lambda\,(1, \lambda, \lambda^2, \dots),\qquad \sum_k\abs{\lambda}^{2k} < \infty .$$

Its spectrum is also the closed unit disk, by the same closure and norm argument: $\norm{S_L} = 1$, since $S_L$ drops $x_1$ and preserves the norm of sequences with $x_1 = 0$.

A spectrum with no eigenvalues at all: $(Mf)(t) = t\,f(t)$ on $L^2[0,1]$ is self-adjoint with $\norm{M} = 1$. If $tf(t) = \lambda f(t)$ almost everywhere, then $f = 0$ almost everywhere, so there are no eigenvalues. For $\lambda \notin [0,1]$ the inverse is multiplication by the bounded function $1/(t - \lambda)$. For $\lambda \in [0, 1]$ the unit vectors $f_n = \sqrt n\,\mathbb 1_{[\lambda, \lambda + 1/n]}$ (indicator functions, with the interval moved inside $[0,1]$ near $\lambda = 1$) satisfy $\norm{(M - \lambda I)f_n} \le 1/n$, so no bounded inverse exists, although $M - \lambda I$ is injective with dense range (its adjoint is $M - \lambda I$ itself, which is injective; ). Hence $\Lambda(M) = [0, 1]$: $M$ is the continuous analogue of a diagonal matrix, with a "diagonal" that is a whole interval.

Let $X$ be a complex Banach space, $T \in B(X)$ and $\lambda \in \Lambda(T)$. Exactly one of the following holds: (i) $T - \lambda I$ is not injective, so $\lambda$ is an eigenvalue; (ii) $T - \lambda I$ is injective with dense range, and its inverse, defined on that range, is unbounded; (iii) $T - \lambda I$ is injective and its range is not dense. If $X$ is a Hilbert space, (iii) holds exactly when $T - \lambda I$ is injective and $\bar\lambda$ is an eigenvalue of $T^*$.

$$Tx = \sum_k\sigma_k\inner{x}{v_k}\,u_k\qquad\text{for every } x \in H_1 .$$

For every integer $r \ge 0$, the truncation $T_r x = \sum_{k\le r}\sigma_k\inner{x}{v_k}u_k$ satisfies

$$\norm{T - T_r} = \min\{\norm{T - F} : F \in B(H_1, H_2),\ \rank F \le r\} = \sigma_{r+1},$$

where $\sigma_{r+1} = 0$ if there are at most $r$ singular values $\sigma_k$.

Proof. $T^*T \in B(H_1)$ is self-adjoint, and compact because $T^*$ is continuous and maps the compact closure of the image of the ball onto a compact set. Since $\inner{T^*Tx}{x} = \norm{Tx}^2 \ge 0$, its nonzero eigenvalues are positive; by write them $\sigma_1^2 \ge \sigma_2^2 \ge \dots$, with orthonormal eigenvectors $v_k$, and put $u_k = Tv_k/\sigma_k$. Then $\inner{u_j}{u_k} = \inner{T^*Tv_j}{v_k}/(\sigma_j\sigma_k) = \delta_{jk}$. For $x \in H_1$, the expansion of shows that $x_0 = x - \sum_k\inner{x}{v_k}v_k$ has $T^*Tx_0 = 0$, so $\norm{Tx_0}^2 = \inner{T^*Tx_0}{x_0} = 0$, and continuity of $T$ gives the expansion of $Tx$. The terms with $k > r$ make up $T - T_r$, so $\norm{(T - T_r)x}^2 = \sum_{k>r}\sigma_k^2\abs{\inner{x}{v_k}}^2 \le \sigma_{r+1}^2\norm{x}^2$, with equality at $x = v_{r+1}$. If $\rank F \le r$ and $\sigma_{r+1} > 0$, the restriction of $F$ to the $(r+1)$-dimensional space $\spn(v_1, \dots, v_{r+1})$ has a nontrivial null space, so some unit vector $x$ in it has $Fx = 0$ and $\norm{(T - F)x} = \norm{Tx} \ge \sigma_{r+1}$. This is the argument of Eckart–Young–Mirsky in chapter 4.

Shift invariance and the Fourier basis

Filters, convolutional layers and time-invariant systems all commute with shifts. Find one basis that diagonalizes every such operator at once.

Work in $\C^N$ with indices $0, \dots, N-1$ taken modulo $N$, and let $e_m$ be the $m$-th standard basis vector of $\C^N$. The cyclic shift $P$ is $(Px)_j = x_{j-1}$. A matrix is circulant if $C_{jl} = c_{(j - l) \bmod N}$ for a vector $c$, its first column; we write $C = \operatorname{circ}(c)$. Then $C = \sum_m c_mP^m$, because $(P^m)_{jl} = 1$ when $j - l \equiv m \pmod N$ and $0$ otherwise, and $Cx$ is the circular convolution $(c \circledast x)_j = \sum_l c_{j-l}\,x_l$.

For $N = 4$,

$$\operatorname{circ}(c_0, c_1, c_2, c_3) = \begin{bmatrix}c_0 & c_3 & c_2 & c_1\\ c_1 & c_0 & c_3 & c_2\\ c_2 & c_1 & c_0 & c_3\\ c_3 & c_2 & c_1 & c_0\end{bmatrix}:$$

each row is the previous one shifted right by one place. The simplest circulant is $P$ itself, and its eigenvectors are easy to guess: if $\varphi_j = e^{2\pi ijk/N}$, then $(P\varphi)_j = \varphi_{j-1} = e^{-2\pi ik/N}\varphi_j$. Every circulant is a polynomial in $P$, so it should share these eigenvectors.

Let $\omega = e^{-2\pi i/N}$. The discrete Fourier transform (DFT) of $x \in \C^N$ is $\hat x = Fx$, with the matrix $F_{jk} = \omega^{jk}$, $j, k = 0, \dots, N-1$. The Fourier modes $\varphi_k \in \C^N$, $(\varphi_k)_j = \omega^{-jk} = e^{2\pi ijk/N}$, are the columns of $F^*$.

The DFT matrix satisfies $F^*F = FF^* = NI$. Hence $U = F/\sqrt N$ is unitary, $x = \frac1N F^*\hat x$ for every $x \in \C^N$, and the Fourier modes $\varphi_0, \dots, \varphi_{N-1}$ form an orthogonal basis of $\C^N$ with $\norm{\varphi_k}_2 = \sqrt N$.

Proof. Orthogonality is a geometric sum: $(F^*F)_{kl} = \sum_j\omega^{j(l-k)}$ equals $N$ for $k = l$, and for $k \ne l$ it equals $(1 - \omega^{N(l-k)})/(1 - \omega^{l-k}) = 0$, because $\omega^{l-k} \ne 1$ and $\omega^N = 1$. A square matrix with a left inverse has it as a right inverse, so $FF^* = NI$ as well. The modes are the columns of $F^*$, and $(F^*)^*F^* = FF^* = NI$ gives their orthogonality and norms.

Let $C = \operatorname{circ}(c)$ and $\hat c = Fc$. Then $C\varphi_k = \hat c_k\,\varphi_k$ for every $k$; equivalently

$$C = \tfrac1N F^*\diag(\hat c)\,F = U^*\diag(\hat c)\,U,\qquad U = F/\sqrt N \text{ unitary}.$$

Here $\diag(\hat c)$ is the diagonal matrix with entries $\hat c_0, \dots, \hat c_{N-1}$, and $U$ is unitary by . Consequently all $N\times N$ circulants commute, every circulant is normal, its eigenvalues are the DFT $\hat c$ of its first column, $\norm{C}_2 = \max_k\abs{\hat c_k}$, and $C$ is invertible exactly when no $\hat c_k$ vanishes.

Proof. Substituting $m = j - l$,

$$(C\varphi_k)_j = \sum_l c_{j-l}\,\omega^{-lk} = \sum_m c_m\,\omega^{-(j-m)k} = \omega^{-jk}\sum_m c_m\omega^{mk} = \hat c_k\,(\varphi_k)_j .$$

So $CF^* = F^*\diag(\hat c)$, and $F^*$ has inverse $F/N$ (). Diagonal matrices commute, and a unitarily diagonalizable matrix is normal (chapter 4) with singular values $\abs{\hat c_k}$ (eigenvalues and singular values, chapter 4).

For $c, x \in \C^N$, $\widehat{c \circledast x} = \hat c \odot \hat x$, where $\odot$ is the entrywise product.

Proof. $F(Cx) = \tfrac1N FF^*\diag(\hat c)Fx = \diag(\hat c)\,\hat x$, since $FF^* = NI$ ().

The fast Fourier transform applies $F$ in $O(N\log N)$ operations, that is, at most a constant times $N\log N$, for $N$ a power of $2$ (J. W. Cooley and J. W. Tukey, Math. Comp. 19 (1965) 297–301; Golub and Van Loan, Matrix Computations, 4th ed., ch. 1). With the corollary, a circular convolution then costs $O(N\log N)$ operations instead of the $O(N^2)$ of a direct matrix-vector product.

Let $c \in \R^N$ and $C = \operatorname{circ}(c)$. Then $\hat c_{N-k} = \overline{\hat c_k}$ for every $k$ (indices modulo $N$), and $C$ maps the real Fourier modes to scaled, phase-shifted copies:

$$C\big(\cos(2\pi kj/N)\big)_j = \big(\abs{\hat c_k}\cos(2\pi kj/N + \arg\hat c_k)\big)_j,\qquad C\big(\sin(2\pi kj/N)\big)_j = \big(\abs{\hat c_k}\sin(2\pi kj/N + \arg\hat c_k)\big)_j,$$

where $\arg\hat c_k$ is the argument (phase) of $\hat c_k$, arbitrary when $\hat c_k = 0$.

Proof. $\hat c_{N-k} = \sum_m c_m\omega^{m(N-k)} = \sum_m c_m\omega^{-mk} = \overline{\hat c_k}$, because $c$ is real and $\omega^N = 1$. The cosine and sine vectors are $\Re\varphi_k$ and $\Im\varphi_k$, the entrywise real and imaginary parts. Since $C$ is real, $C\,\Re\varphi_k = \Re(C\varphi_k) = \Re(\hat c_k\varphi_k)$ by , and $\hat c_k(\varphi_k)_j = \abs{\hat c_k}\,e^{i(2\pi kj/N + \arg\hat c_k)}$; likewise for the imaginary part.

For $C \in \C^{N\times N}$ the following are equivalent: (i) $C$ is circulant; (ii) $CP = PC$; (iii) $C$ is a polynomial in $P$, and then $C = \sum_{m=0}^{N-1}c_mP^m$ with $c$ the first column of $C$; (iv) every Fourier mode $\varphi_k$ is an eigenvector of $C$, that is, $C = \frac1N F^*DF$ for a diagonal matrix $D$.

Proof. (i) $\Rightarrow$ (iii): $(\sum_m c_mP^m)_{jl} = c_{(j-l)\bmod N} = C_{jl}$, as in the setting. (iii) $\Rightarrow$ (ii): polynomials in $P$ commute with $P$. (ii) $\Rightarrow$ (i): $(CP)_{jl} = C_{j,l+1}$ and $(PC)_{jl} = C_{j-1,l}$, so $C_{j,l+1} = C_{j-1,l}$ for all $j$, $l$: $C$ is constant along cyclic diagonals. (i) $\Rightarrow$ (iv) is . (iv) $\Rightarrow$ (ii): $P = \operatorname{circ}(e_1)$ is itself of the form $\frac1N F^*D'F$ by , and $\frac1N F^*DF$ commutes with it because $D$ and $D'$ commute and $FF^* = NI$. In (iii), a polynomial in $P$ reduces to degree below $N$ since $P^N = I$, and its coefficients are its first column because $P^me_0 = e_m$.

Equivalences and the invariant behind them (). Shift invariance is the symmetry; the Fourier modes are its eigenvectors, and every operator with the symmetry is a multiplier $\hat c_k$ on them. Familiar filters read off at once: the shift has $\abs{\hat c_k} = 1$ (all-pass, a pure phase $\omega^k$); the moving average $c = \frac13(e_{-1} + e_0 + e_1)$ has $\hat c_k = \frac13(1 + 2\cos(2\pi k/N))$ (low-pass); the first difference $c = e_0 - e_1$ has $\abs{\hat c_k} = 2\abs{\sin(\pi k/N)}$ (high-pass, zero at $k = 0$).

Let $t_m$, $m \in \Z$, be absolutely summable with $t_{-m} = \overline{t_m}$, so that the symbol $f(\theta) = \sum_m t_me^{im\theta}$ is real and continuous. Let $T_N = [t_{j-l}]_{j,l=0}^{N-1}$ be the Toeplitz matrix, the non-cyclic analogue of a circulant, with eigenvalues $\lambda_1(T_N), \dots, \lambda_N(T_N)$. Then every eigenvalue of $T_N$ lies in $[\min f, \max f]$, and for every continuous function $g$ on $[\min f, \max f]$,

$$\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac1N\sum_{k=1}^N g\big(\lambda_k(T_N)\big) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}g\big(f(\theta)\big)\,\dd\theta .$$
Proof sketch

For $x \in \C^N$ and $X(\theta) = \sum_l x_le^{il\theta}$, expanding the products gives $x^*T_Nx = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}f(\theta)\abs{X(\theta)}^2\,\dd\theta$ and $\norm{x}_2^2 = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}\abs{X(\theta)}^2\,\dd\theta$. So every Rayleigh quotient of the Hermitian matrix $T_N$, and hence every eigenvalue, lies in $[\min f, \max f]$. For the limit, the boundary rows make $T_N$ differ from a circulant by a matrix whose effect on the eigenvalue distribution vanishes as $N \to \infty$; by the Weierstrass approximation theorem on $[\min f, \max f]$ it suffices to treat $g(\lambda) = \lambda^s$ for integers $s \ge 0$, that is, to show $\frac1N\tr(T_N^s) \to \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}f^s\,\dd\theta$, with $\tr$ the trace.

Without the Hermitian structure, boundaries can change the spectrum completely. The Toeplitz matrix with ones on the superdiagonal is the Jordan block $J_N(0)$, whose only eigenvalue is $0$ for every $N$; the circulant with the same diagonals is the cyclic shift $P^\T$, whose eigenvalues are the $N$-th roots of unity. Chapter 6 shows that for $\eps > 0$ the $\eps$-pseudospectrum of $J_N(0)$, the set of $z$ with $\norm{(zI - J_N(0))^{-1}}_2 > 1/\eps$, is a disk about $0$ of radius at least $\eps^{1/N}$. Its radius is at most $1 + \eps$, because $\norm{(zI - J_N(0))^{-1}}_2 \le 1/(\abs z - 1)$ for $\abs z > 1 \ge \norm{J_N(0)}_2$ (). Since $\eps^{1/N} \to 1$, for fixed $\eps$ the pseudospectra, not the eigenvalues, fill out the unit disk as $N$ grows.

Let $(Tu)(t) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}h(\tau)\,u(t - \tau)\,\dd\tau$ with an impulse response $h$, and let $s \in \C$ be such that $\int\abs{h(\tau)}e^{-\Re(s)\,\tau}\,\dd\tau < \infty$. Then

$$T\,e^{st} = H(s)\,e^{st},\qquad H(s) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}h(\tau)\,e^{-s\tau}\,\dd\tau .$$

Proof. $\int h(\tau)e^{s(t-\tau)}\dd\tau = e^{st}\int h(\tau)e^{-s\tau}\dd\tau$, and the hypothesis makes the integral converge.

For a causal $h$ ($h(\tau) = 0$ for $\tau < 0$), $H$ is the Laplace transform of $h$, and on the imaginary axis $H(i\omega)$, at the real angular frequency $\omega$ (not the root of unity of the DFT), is the frequency response: by with $s = i\omega$, which needs $\int\abs{h} < \infty$, a time-invariant system multiplies $e^{i\omega t}$ by the complex gain $H(i\omega)$, as a circulant multiplies $\varphi_k$ by $\hat c_k$. Like the "eigenvectors" of the multiplication operator in , $e^{st}$ is not square integrable on $\R$: an eigenfunction in a generalized sense. Minimum-norm problems and minimum-energy control

Among all $x$ in a Hilbert space that satisfy finitely many linear constraints $\inner{x}{y_i} = c_i$, find the one of smallest norm. In control the same question reads: among all inputs that steer a system to a target, find the one of least energy.

Let $y_1, \dots, y_n \in H$ be linearly independent and $c \in \F^n$. The problem

$$\text{minimize } \norm{x}\quad\text{subject to}\quad\inner{x}{y_i} = c_i,\ \ i = 1, \dots, n,$$

has exactly one solution $x^* = \sum_{j=1}^n\alpha_jy_j$, where $\alpha$ solves the Gram system $G\alpha = c$ with $G_{ij} = \inner{y_j}{y_i}$, and $\norm{x^*}^2 = c^*G^{-1}c$.

Proof. $G$ is positive definite, since $\alpha^*G\alpha = \norm{\sum_j\alpha_jy_j}^2 > 0$ for $\alpha \ne 0$; so $\alpha = G^{-1}c$ exists, and $x^* = \sum_j\alpha_jy_j$ satisfies $\inner{x^*}{y_i} = \sum_j\alpha_j\inner{y_j}{y_i} = (G\alpha)_i = c_i$. If $x$ is any feasible point, $z = x - x^*$ has $\inner{z}{y_i} = 0$ for all $i$, so $z \perp x^*$ and

$$\norm{x}^2 = \norm{x^*}^2 + \norm{z}^2 \ge \norm{x^*}^2,$$

with equality only for $z = 0$. Finally, using that $G$ is Hermitian,

$$\norm{x^*}^2 = \Big\langle x^*,\, \sum_i\alpha_iy_i\Big\rangle = \sum_i\bar\alpha_ic_i = \alpha^*c = c^*G^{-1}c .$$

In $H = \R^m$ with the rows $y_1^\T, \dots, y_n^\T$ of a full-row-rank $A \in \R^{n\times m}$ as constraint vectors, $G = AA^\T$ and $x^* = A^\T(AA^\T)^{-1}c$: the minimum-norm solution of chapter 2. Two extreme cases: if the $y_i$ are orthonormal, $G = I$ and $x^* = \sum_ic_iy_i$; if two of them become nearly parallel, the constraints can nearly contradict each other, and then $\norm{x^*}$ blows up. For instance, in $\R^2$ the constraints $y_1 = (1, 0)$, $y_2 = (\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$ with $c = (1, 0)$ force $x^* = (1, -\cot\theta)$, with $\norm{x^*} = 1/\abs{\sin\theta} \to \infty$ as $\theta \to 0$.

Consider $\dot x = ax + bu$ with real $a$, $b \ne 0$, $x(0) = 0$, and the target $x(t_f) = x_f$ at the horizon $t_f > 0$. Inputs live in $L^2[0, t_f]$ with $\inner{u}{v} = \int_0^{t_f}u(t)v(t)\,\dd t$, and the energy of $u$ is $\norm{u}^2$. Since $\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\big(e^{-at}x\big) = e^{-at}bu$,

$$x(t_f) = \int_0^{t_f}e^{a(t_f - t)}\,b\,u(t)\,\dd t = \inner{u}{y},\qquad y(t) = b\,e^{a(t_f - t)}.$$

This is one constraint, so with $n = 1$ gives $u^* = (x_f/\norm{y}^2)\,y$:

$$u^*(t) = \frac{x_f}{W_c(t_f)}\,b\,e^{a(t_f - t)},\qquad E^* = \norm{u^*}^2 = \frac{x_f^2}{W_c(t_f)},\qquad W_c(t_f) = \int_0^{t_f}b^2e^{2a\tau}\,\dd\tau = \begin{cases}b^2\,\dfrac{e^{2at_f} - 1}{2a}, & a \ne 0,\\[4pt] b^2t_f, & a = 0.\end{cases}$$

$W_c(t_f) = \norm{y}^2$ is the controllability Gramian of the scalar system (chapter 9); from $x(0) = x_0 \ne 0$, replace $x_f$ by $x_f - e^{at_f}x_0$.

Read the formula in extreme cases. Short horizon: $W_c \approx b^2t_f$ and $E^* \approx x_f^2/(b^2t_f) \to \infty$; fast steering is expensive. Stable $a < 0$: $W_c$ increases to $b^2/(2\abs a)$, so $E^* \ge 2\abs{a}x_f^2/b^2$ however long the horizon, and $u^* \propto e^{-\abs a(t_f - t)}$ pushes late, because early pushes decay before $t_f$. Unstable $a > 0$: $W_c$ grows like $e^{2at_f}$ and $E^* \to 0$; $u^*$ pushes early and lets the instability do the work. And $b \to 0$ makes $W_c \to 0$: the target becomes unreachable.

Minimum norm, orthogonal projection and the Gram system are one structure, and it recurs through the guide: least squares and the pseudoinverse (chapter 2 and chapter 4); minimum-energy control of $\dot x = Ax + Bu$ with $x(t) \in \R^n$ and $(A, B)$ controllable, where the $n$ constraints are the components of $x(t_f)$ and the Gram matrix is the Gramian $W_c(t_f) = \int_0^{t_f}e^{At}BB^\T e^{A^\T t}\dd t$, giving $u^*(t) = B^\T e^{A^\T(t_f - t)}W_c(t_f)^{-1}x_f$ from $x(0) = 0$ (chapter 9); and the Kalman filter of chapter 11, whose estimate is an affine function of the data with an error that has mean zero and is uncorrelated with the data (the proof of the Gaussian-conditioning lemma). In the Hilbert space of square-integrable random variables with $\inner{\xi}{\eta} = \mathbb E[\xi\eta]$, $\mathbb E$ the expectation, this is the orthogonality condition of : each component of the estimate is the orthogonal projection of the corresponding state component onto the span of the constants and the data. Here $B^\T e^{A^\T(t_f-t)}$ is the matrix analogue of $y(t) = b\,e^{a(t_f-t)}$: the adjoint of the input-to-state map (chapter 9).
